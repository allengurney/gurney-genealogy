## Children and the contested link to Richard Gurney of Weymouth

This is the area where the **confidence level drops sharply**—not because the hypothesis is implausible, but because the surviving *published* record trail being used by most compilers is weakly anchored.

A prominent compiled genealogy (the “Daniel Shed genealogy,” digitized from early-20th-century work) is unusually frank: it states that John Gurney’s history is “very obscure” and that the list of children is “uncertain,” then offers a **probable** set of sons (including **Richard**, **John**, **Peter**, **Isaac**) and raises the possibility of a daughter **Mary** who may have married Daniel Shed as an explanation for a “father” reference in other local history. citeturn5search36

Separately, a regional family-history/genealogy sketch (later reproduced online) reports a tradition that **Richard Gurney of Weymouth—described as the earliest “definite knowledge” ancestor in that line—“appears to have been” John Gurney of Braintree’s son**, but explicitly frames this as coming from family records/manuscript tradition rather than a quoted will or deed. citeturn19search1turn18view0

A broad compendium of early-settler summaries adds another relevant nuance: it lists **John Gurney of Braintree** (noting he “may be” the Newgate apprentice, and giving an approximate death and wife death year), and separately lists **Richard Gurney of Weymouth** with wife and children—without explicitly stating that Richard is John’s son. That omission matters: it suggests that even the compilers who were comfortable summarizing early families did not have a clean, citation-ready parent-child record connecting the two men. citeturn5search39turn25search5

**Inference (clearly labeled):** If John Gurney was indeed born around 1603 (see the deposition issue below), then a son Richard born around 1630 is chronologically plausible. If instead John was born in 1615, fathering a child in the late 1620s–early 1630s becomes problematic. The *chronology strongly favors* either (a) an earlier birth for the immigrant John, or (b) the existence of more than one John Gurney in Massachusetts whose records became blended. This inference is driven by the conflicting compiled evidence described in the next section. citeturn20search10turn5search36turn5search39

Bottom line for “general-consumption confidence”:  
- **Richard Gurney of Weymouth is well-attested as a real early Massachusetts figure** in compiled town/family histories (wife Rebecca, children, freeman status), but  
- **the claim that he is the son of John Gurney of Braintree is best treated as “probable but not proven”** until a primary document (probate, deed, guardianship) is surfaced that states the relationship explicitly. citeturn19search1turn5search36turn5search39turn20search10
