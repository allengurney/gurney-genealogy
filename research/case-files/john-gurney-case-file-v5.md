<h2 id="s1">1. THE QUESTION: WHO WAS JOHN GURNEY BEFORE MASSACHUSETTS?</h2>

John Gurney arrived in Massachusetts Bay around 1640 and left a good documentary trail once he got there. Where he came from is unknown. No published authority has identified his parents or his home parish, and the standard reference work on Great Migration emigrants — Robert Charles Anderson's *Great Migration Directory* (2025) — records his origin simply as "Unknown."<sup class="fn"><a href="#n1" id="ref-1">1</a></sup>

**This file argues that he was the son of Francis Gurney, a Merchant Taylor of Norfolk and London, by Francis's first wife Margaret Rybett.** It also states plainly what that argument does not have: no surviving document names a John Gurney as Francis's son.

### What a candidate has to account for

Everything known about the man himself comes from colonial records made after he arrived. Those records give us the shape any English candidate must fit.

**Two of those rows correct the family lists that circulate in compiled genealogies.** The published accounts give John five or six English-born children. Sweeps of every major parish-baptism collection turn up **no Peter Gurney baptism anywhere in Great Britain between 1627 and 1647, and no Isaac between 1632 and 1652** — so both boys were almost certainly born in America, and their absence from English records is expected rather than a puzzle to solve. **Sarah is doubtful on her own account**: she is the only member of the roster with no record attesting that she lived, and she is absent from the standard survey of New England marriages before 1700.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n9" id="ref-9b">9</a></sup>

### The Central Question

<div class="discovery">Who was John Gurney before he appeared in Massachusetts in 1641? Where in England was he born, who were his parents, and why did he emigrate?
</div>

### The short answer

**The identification is open.** After eliminating every other documented John Gurney the records will yield, no candidate stands close to proof. Candidate B — the son of Francis Gurney — remains the leading named candidate and the only one with a coherent circumstantial case, and this file presents that case in full. But the most likely single answer, on the evidence assembled here, is not a name at all: it is that **the record which would identify him has not survived, or has not yet been searched.** Section 11 sets out the weighing.

<h2 id="s2">2. THE CANDIDATE: JOHN GURNEY, SON OF FRANCIS GURNEY AND MARGARET RYBETT</h2>

The case rests on one primary record and four threads of circumstance.

| Evidence | What it shows |
|---|---|
| **★ The Margaret Rybett marriage** (1611) | Francis Gurney had a first wife nobody had found. Children of that marriage would be born about 1612–1617 — the right generation for the emigrant. |
| **A documented Norfolk household** | Francis was baptising children at East Dereham in 1610 and again in 1618, on either side of a gap in the register. |
| **Trade** | John the emigrant was a tailor; Francis was a Merchant Taylor, and trades commonly passed from father to son. |
| **Corridor** | Francis's life ran between Norfolk and London for forty years — precisely the geography that produced the Great Migration. |
| **Motive** | Francis sold every acre he owned in 1634, leaving a grown son nothing to stay for. |
| **Network** | Francis's brother was a militant Puritan clergyman; his London parish adjoined the city's busiest emigration hub. |

**What the case does not have is John himself.** No document names him as Francis's son, no baptism of his has been found, and no record of his English marriage or of any of his children's baptisms has been located. Section 10 weighs both sides.

### 2.1 Francis Gurney of West Barsham, Norwich and London

Francis Gurney was born on 13 September 1581 at West Barsham Hall, Norfolk, a twin with his brother Anthony and the sixth son of Henry Gurnay, Esquire, of Great Ellingham and West Barsham. Henry had twelve children. The family was old Norfolk gentry with a coat of arms and a long pedigree, but a sixth son inherited little and had to make his own living.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n11" id="ref-11">11</a></sup>

He made it in cloth. On 14 May 1599, aged about seventeen, he was bound apprentice in London to Henry Tryme of the Merchant Taylors' Company, for a seven-year term. In February 1605 the Company's court transferred him to another master ahead of a documented journey north — the first concrete sign of his re-engagement with Norfolk, six years before he married there. He was admitted to the freedom of the Company on 30 June 1606.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n12" id="ref-12">12</a></sup>

The family historian Daniel Gurney, writing in the 1840s, noted that Francis's "commercial life began at Norwich," and an account book from Hunstanton Hall bears him out: Francis acted as "a sort of agent, or banker" for the Lestrange family, with payments running from January 1612 to May 1636.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n13" id="ref-13">13</a></sup>

### 2.2 The first marriage nobody had found

<div class="discovery">
    ★ <strong>PRIMARY RECORD:</strong> On 23 September 1611, "Franc Gurny &amp; Margaret Rybett" were married at St Martin at Palace, Norwich.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n14" id="ref-14">14</a></sup>
</div>

**This record opens a family that no previous account of Francis Gurney knew existed.** Daniel Gurney spent decades on the Gurney records and never located it; nor did the genealogist Charles Bernau, writing in 1913. Both knew Francis only through his second wife, Anne Browning, whose children begin at St Benet Fink in London in 1619 — thirteen years after Francis gained his freedom, an unexplained gap in every account written before 2026.

A first marriage in 1611 fills that gap and produces children of exactly the right age to be the Massachusetts emigrant. It also confirms one detail that had circulated only as family tradition. The compiler Charles Pease's *Descendants of Anthony Gurney* gives Francis a first wife "Margaret Ryvett" — and that turned out to be checkable, and to check out.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n15" id="ref-15">15</a></sup>

**What the same tradition says next does not survive checking.** Pease also gives Francis a son John, born about 1610, and identifies him with the Braintree emigrant outright by naming his second wife, Grizell Fletcher. That is exactly the claim in dispute, and it rests on nothing: the entry carries **no source reference of any kind**, while entries on the same page do carry them; it gives a daughter Anne the identical year, which reads as a compiler's placeholder rather than two researched dates; and it does not come from the American researcher named in the genealogy's source list, whose contribution is confined to a nineteenth-century branch of the family.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n16" id="ref-16">16</a></sup>

### 2.3 Margaret's family, two miles from East Dereham

Margaret is identifiable in the parish registers as a **Rivet of Garveston** — either Margaret Rivet, baptised 21 May 1586, daughter of Francis Rivet, or, a live alternative, Margarett Rivet, baptised 1 February 1577/8, daughter of Robert Rivet. Garveston lies two miles from East Dereham and Gressenhall three, and the Rivett presence in both parishes is continuous. Richard Ryvett of Gressenhall, who married in 1602, is a documented source for the name John gave his eldest surviving son — Margaret's first cousin under the 1586 reading, her full brother under the 1577/8 one.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n17" id="ref-17">17</a></sup>

Two things about this family are commonly misstated.

1. **They are village copyholders, not gentry.** The Garveston Rivets held their land by copyhold — a customary tenancy recorded in the manor's own court roll — and are a different social class from the Suffolk gentry Ryvetts of Rishangles and Rattlesden, with whom they are often merged. Whether the two families are related at all is unresolved, and the evidence points against it. The family writes itself *Rivet*; "Rybett" is the 1611 clerk's one-off spelling.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n18" id="ref-18">18</a></sup>
2. **The marriage parish is unexplained.** St Martin at Palace is sometimes described as the family's own church, on the strength of Rivett entries in that register between 1539 and 1603. Those are Norwich city Rivetts, and nothing connects them to the Garveston copyholders fifteen miles away. Francis and Margaret married in a parish where neither family is otherwise documented — an open question, not a settled explanation.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n18" id="ref-18b">18</a></sup>

### 2.4 Financial collapse

Francis's commercial life ended badly, and the collapse is documented in detail.

- **1622–1625 — a failed venture at King's Lynn.** An agreement of 17 October 1622 set him up to teach the borough's poor children to spin worsted yarn. The venture failed and left Sir Hamon Lestrange to pay Francis's £100 bond.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n19" id="ref-19">19</a></sup>
- **11 July 1634 — he sold everything.** A single indenture conveyed all his Norfolk and Suffolk manors and lands to Sir Owen Smith of Irmingland for £1,000, in a forced liquidation conducted through the Court of Wards.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n20" id="ref-20">20</a></sup>
- **9 January 1646/7 — he was buried** at St Botolph without Bishopsgate, London.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n21" id="ref-21">21</a></sup>

The 1634 sale is the strongest single element of the motive argument. It falls squarely inside the years the emigrant would have been deciding whether to leave, and it means a grown son had no land to expect at home.

Two qualifications belong with Francis's own ending. His burial survives only at transcript level — no one has seen the parish register entry — and the largest London parish-register collection does not carry it at all. And **his probate is negative in four separate jurisdictions**: the Prerogative Court of Canterbury, the Commissary Court of London (which held his own parish of St Benet Fink), the Norfolk church courts, and the Essex Record Office. Only the Archdeaconry Court of London is unchecked. A man ruined by the 1634 sale may simply have left nothing worth proving.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n21" id="ref-21b">21</a></sup>

### 2.5 Room for an older son

For John to be Francis's son he must be older than Roger Gurney, whom the Heralds' Visitation of London in 1633 recorded as Francis's "eldest sonne." [Heraldic visitations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldic_visitation) were periodic official tours that registered coats of arms and recorded family pedigrees, so this is a formal record made in Francis's own lifetime with his own assent. **It is the sharpest documentary objection to the identification, and it stands as written.**<sup class="fn"><a href="#n22" id="ref-22">22</a></sup>

It is not, however, a closed list, for three reasons.

1. **A visitation recorded the children a man presented, not necessarily all he had.** A grown son of a first marriage, living independently or estranged, had no reason to be presented in a London pedigree.
2. **The compilers themselves treated Francis's child list as incomplete.** Daniel Gurney described Dorothy as the eldest child "mentioned in" the St Benet Fink register — a carefully hedged phrase — and judged Roger to have "probably died young." He added, beyond the register, a son John of Maldon that Francis "seems" to have had and a George who was "probably another son." Bernau warned outright that no complete record of Francis's children survives.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n23" id="ref-23">23</a></sup>
3. **Neither compiler knew of the 1611 marriage**, so no child of it could appear in their reckoning at all.

Roger was baptised at St Benet Fink in December 1621 and was the eldest son of the *second* marriage. **The London register supports the reading rather than undercutting it:** seven children of Francis and Anne were baptised there between 1619 and 1637 — Dorithy, Roger, Frances, Francis, Lucretia, Thomas and Margaret — and there is no John among them. If John was Francis's son at all, he belongs to the first marriage.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n24" id="ref-24">24</a></sup>

<h2 id="s4">4. THE EAST DEREHAM REGISTER: WHAT IT HOLDS AND WHAT IT LOST</h2>

Francis Gurney baptised children at East Dereham, Norfolk, in the years before he moved to London. That parish register is the single document the identification has been built on, so its contents and its limits both matter.

### 4.1 What the register is

East Dereham's record for this period is not a bound book. It is **PD 86/41, "Indented register bills, 1593–1641"** — a file of loose annual certificates, one per year, digitised as 110 contiguous images. Read across all 110, the file runs strictly in order, and each annual return carries a heading written into the parchment itself giving the year it covers.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n25" id="ref-25">25</a></sup>

### 4.2 Two windows of loss, and neither can ever be recovered

**The returns for 25 March 1611 to 25 March 1616 do not survive.** The file runs from the 1610 return straight to the 1616-17 return.

**No second witness to those years has ever existed.** The Norfolk Record Office's own survey of the parish gives its registers as covering about 1549 to 1596 (and unfit to produce), then nothing until 1679. The archdeacon's and bishop's transcripts — the copies normally sent up to the diocese, which often survive where a register does not — begin only in 1698, eighty-two years after the missing window. The loose bills of PD 86/41 are the only record the parish ever had for 1593–1641.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n26" id="ref-26">26</a></sup>

**This is a permanent limit, not a pending search.** No future access — subscription, record office or otherwise — will change it.

**A second, earlier gap matters too.** Between 1594 and 1608 the file holds four annual returns and is missing twelve. Only 1593-4, 1595-6, 1601 and 1608-9 survive, and no Gurney-variant entry appears in any of them.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n27" id="ref-27">27</a></sup>

**Edward's baptism is the load-bearing one for chronology**, because it falls sixteen months *before* the September 1611 marriage. The East Dereham household was already producing children in May 1610.

### 4.4 A note on Marye of 1618

Marye's baptism on 25 May 1618 falls only nine months before Dorothy's at St Benet Fink in London on 2 March 1619 — tight spacing for one mother. Marye is nevertheless most likely Anne Browning's child rather than Margaret's: Margaret died about 1616–17, and a May 1618 baptism implies conception around August 1617, after the remarriage. The alternative — that Margaret lived into late 1617 and Marye was her last child — cannot be excluded while Margaret's burial remains unfound.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n29" id="ref-29">29</a></sup>

<h2 id="s5">5. THE BAPTISM THAT IS NOT THERE</h2>

**No baptism of a John to this family survives in the East Dereham register, and none ever will.**

This matters enough to state directly, because a particular entry in that register has been read as one. On image 00715 sits a line indexed by FindMyPast and FreeREG as *"John the sonne of Nicholas Gorne."* A structured comparison of that line against confirmed "ffrancis Gurnie" entries in the same register and hand was taken to show that it reads *"John the sonne of ffrancis Gurnie"* instead — which would have placed a John Gurney, son of Francis, at East Dereham about 1609/10.

**A full re-reading of the image run against recorded coordinates does not support that.** The line adjudicated on image 00715 is on image `00722`, and it reads *"Amorye the daughter of Robart Enderby, Ianuarij 10."* The record the index actually describes is on image `00726`, six years later, and reads *"John the sonne off Nicholas Horne bapt — Janua 10"* — 10 January 1616/17, in a Horne family.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n30" id="ref-30">30</a></sup>

Two further readings that depended on the same page are also unavailable. A reconstructed page-to-year chronology assumed one page per annual return, where in fact each return runs across several membranes and each membrane was photographed twice; and the assignment "page 00725 = the 1615 register year" was never possible at all, because images 00724 and 00725 are the marriages-and-burials membranes of the **1610** return and no 1615 return exists.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n25" id="ref-25b">25</a></sup>

### What this costs the hypothesis, and what it does not

**The cost is real.** Candidate B has no baptism, and — because the 1611–1616 returns are gone — the window in which a legitimate first child of the marriage would fall **can never be searched**. The hypothesis has lost its last testable prediction at East Dereham. That is a structural weakness, not a neutral fact: it means the identification cannot be confirmed or refuted from the one parish it points to.

**Two things nevertheless survive intact.** Edward's baptism of 1610 and Marye's of 1618 are unaffected; Francis's East Dereham household is documented on both sides of the gap. And the loss actually **relieves a chronological strain rather than creating one.** While a c.1609/10 baptism stood, John's birth had to be squeezed in before his father's September 1611 marriage, from a father who had been a bound London apprentice until 1606, and awkwardly early against a deposed age of about fifty in 1653. Without it, Francis's own chronology runs cleanly: Norwich from about 1606, married 1611, an East Dereham household 1610–1618, London from 1618-19.

**The friction now sits on a different question: where a John born about 1603–1608 came from.** Sections 6 to 11 are about that question.

<h2 id="s6">6. JOHN'S OWN FAMILY, AND THE ENGLISH SEARCH</h2>

### 6.1 No English record of his household has been found

**No English parish register anywhere records a John Gurney and a wife Mary baptising the colonial children.** That has now been tested four independent ways, and each way returns nothing.

1. **By child.** Sweeping every major parish-baptism collection for a Mary, a Richard or a John born to a father John Gurney, England-wide and across the full range of surname spellings, produces **exactly one** record in the whole country that belongs to no otherwise-accounted-for household: **Mary Gurny, baptised at Hitcham, Buckinghamshire, on 22 January 1631, father John, mother not recorded.**<sup class="fn"><a href="#n31" id="ref-31">31</a></sup>
2. **By father.** A complete year-by-year enumeration of every baptism in Great Britain 1615–1649 to a father named John Gurney — 598 records — returns the same single answer. In the tight 1626–1636 band the sweep returns 149 records, of which 68 carry a Gurney-family surname, and **every one belongs to a household already accounted for**.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n31" id="ref-31b">31</a></sup>
3. **By mother.** The register has now also been asked who the mother was — an obvious question, given that a wife named Mary is among the firmest facts about the household. Across Great Britain 1620–1640, **exactly one household with a securely Gurney surname has a father John and a mother Mary: Eythorne, Kent** — and it is eliminated, its head buried in 1648 and his wife six days after childbirth in 1641.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n32" id="ref-32">32</a></sup>
4. **By marriage.** See below.

### 6.2 The missing marriage, on five independent instruments

Mary Gurney, John's daughter, married Daniel Shed at Braintree in 1647. Even at a minimum marriage age of sixteen she was **born by 1631**, which puts her parents' marriage no later than about 1630. That is a primary constraint on the English marriage, and it has been searched hard.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n33" id="ref-33">33</a></sup>

- **England-wide, both parties indexed:** only **two** John Gurney × Mary marriages exist anywhere in England before 1660 — Eythorne, Kent, in 1632 and Ackworth, Yorkshire, in 1636. Both are already on the table in Section 8.
- **Hertfordshire:** no John Gurney marriage of any kind between 1600 and 1654.
- **Buckinghamshire, tested from the groom's side and the bride's side:** five John Gurney marriages inside the emigration window, and **not one bride is a Mary** — Alice 1628, Susan 1629, Elizabeth 1630, Avis 1638, Rebecca 1640. The earliest Mary to marry a Buckinghamshire John Gurney is Mary Kidgell at Edlesborough in **1663**, two years after the colonial Mary had died.
- **FreeREG**, whose records name both parties: ninety-seven Gurney marriages across England 1615–1645, **every John Gurney groom's bride identified, and not one a Mary**.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n34" id="ref-34">34</a></sup>

**What makes this more than an absence is that the name was plainly available.** In those same holdings nine Gurney men married Marys — Robert × Mary Lame at Norwich in 1622, Edward × Mary Bedward at Stepney in 1641, Walter × Mary Hammond at Westminster in 1642, and six more before 1660. **It is specifically the Johns who have none.**

**One further household appears at the outer edge of the surname scale and fails on its own terms.** John and Mary **Gurley** of St Clement Danes, Westminster, baptised Eliz 1627, Ellynor 1628, Alexander 1629, **Mary 1631** and Sara 1633, and then the record stops. That satisfies the father, the mother, Mary's primary birth bound and the departure pattern — and fails completely on the sons: there is no Richard and no John, the boy being an Alexander, a name that appears nowhere in the colonial family.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n35" id="ref-35">35</a></sup>

### 6.3 How to read these negatives

**Each of these searches states a minimum, not a maximum**, and the reason is worth understanding because it governs how much weight the negatives can bear.

- **No single index is complete, and their gaps do not overlap.** Tested record by record: one major collection lacks the Hitcham 1631 baptism, the Berkhamsted 1626 one, the Stewkley 1626 one and the Aylesbury 1638 one; FreeREG lacks three of those four but holds the fourth; two other providers hold all four. **Four partial instruments that each drop something different do not add up to a complete one.**<sup class="fn"><a href="#n36" id="ref-36">36</a></sup>
- **Spelling is a scale, not a filter.** Seventeenth-century clerks wrote Gurney, Gurny, Gourney, Gurnie, Gurne, Gorne, Garne, Gurnard and more, and a search on any one stem misses the others. *Gurnard* in particular is not a look-alike: the Heralds' Visitation of London of 1634 records letters patent to "Richard Gurny, **alias Gurnard**, Sheriff of London," and the Norfolk family's own crest is a gurnard fish — a visual pun on the name.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n37" id="ref-37">37</a></sup>
- **Forenames drift too.** Clerks wrote Marie, Marye and Maria for Mary, and *Margaret* was routinely shortened to "Marg." — where the looping tail of the `g` is nearly identical to a `y` in the hands of this period, so a transcriber can read either name for the other in either direction.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n37" id="ref-37b">37</a></sup>

**The honest conclusion is that these are strong negatives with stated limits, not proofs of absence.** The most likely place for the missing marriage is a class of record that has never been searched at all — above all the archdeaconry marriage-licence series of the south Midlands, where a couple marrying away from home would surface.

### 6.5 The trade does not point only to Norfolk and London

John the emigrant was a tailor, and his father under this hypothesis was a Merchant Taylor. Two bodies of evidence bear on that match, and they pull in opposite directions.

**Against the transmission being provable:** the Merchant Taylors' Company records contain **no John Gurney son of Francis at any binding between 1583 and 1800, and no Gurney freedom by patrimony at all**. The Norwich freemen and apprentice registers likewise contain no John Gurney in the emigration window. Both formal routes by which a trade passed from father to son are documented blanks. If Candidate B holds, John trained under a country master or inside the household — possible, but unrecorded.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n38" id="ref-38">38</a></sup>

**Against the match being distinctive:** Buckinghamshire holds a documented Gurney tailoring trade across four parishes and eighty-five years — William Gurneye of Aylesbury, tailor (1571); Luke Gurneye of Aylesbury, tailor (1591); Thomas Gurney of Rowsham in Wingrave, tailor (1628); Richard Gurny of Mursley, tailor (1631); and Thomas Gurney of Weston Turville, tailor (1633). Four of the five fall inside the emigrant's own working life. **A Gurney tailor is not a Norfolk-and-London signature.**<sup class="fn"><a href="#n39" id="ref-39">39</a></sup>

One further qualification on the colonial side: the "tailor" style rests on a single primary record, John's own 1661/2 deed. His estate inventory of 1663 lists plough irons, oxen and grain — and no tailoring tools at all.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n2" id="ref-2b">2</a></sup>

### 6.6 The naming pattern: a missing Francis and a predicted Richard

Under the standard English naming custom of the period, the eldest son takes the **paternal grandfather's** name, the second son the **maternal grandfather's**, and a later son the father's own. Three tests follow, and they run in different directions.

**John named no child Francis.** This is the strongest naming argument against Candidate B, and it is sharper than a simple absence: the slot the custom governs most strongly — the eldest son — is occupied by **Richard**. It gets sharper still under the reconstruction of Margaret as a daughter of *Francis* Rivet of Garveston, which would make both of John's grandfathers Francis. Four explanations have been offered — estrangement after the 1634 ruin, avoidance of a name attached to that ruin, maternal naming priority, or an earlier Francis who died young — and each is a hypothesis, not a record.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n40" id="ref-40">40</a></sup>

**Read forwards, the same custom predicts a paternal grandfather named Richard — and Candidate B's is Francis.** Setting aside Sarah, whose existence rests on nothing, the pattern resolves cleanly: Mary is the eldest daughter, named for her mother; **Richard is therefore the eldest son and occupies the paternal-grandfather slot**; John Jr. carries the father's own name, as the custom allows once the grandfather slots are filled. The rival explanation, that Richard honours Margaret's kinsman Richard Ryvett of Gressenhall, is structurally awkward: a *maternal* relative belongs in the second-son slot, not the first.

**That prediction was tested, and it returned a candidate.** Searching the Buckinghamshire church records for a John Gurney baptised about 1595–1615 with a father named Richard returns one: **Johannes Gurney, baptised at Cheddington on 5 August 1608, son of Richard Gurney and Jane Founteine**, who married there in 1606. His father's probate follows in 1626, leaving him fatherless at eighteen. He never appears in the Cheddington record again. He is weighed in §8.7 and §11.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n41" id="ref-41">41</a></sup>

**A second, weaker naming signal points the same way.** John's youngest son was **Isaac** — a name absent from every Norfolk Gurney household surveyed, which run to Edward, Marye, Agnes and Francis. Buckinghamshire Gurneys do use it, and one instance is both contemporary and in the same parish: an Isaac Gurney and Martha were baptising children at Cheddington in 1636 and 1641, and their own son John was baptised there in 1634. **Isaac and John were both live forenames in that parish's Gurney households in the emigrant's own generation.**<sup class="fn"><a href="#n41" id="ref-41b">41</a></sup>

<h2 id="s7">7. WAS ANN GURNEY JOHN'S SISTER?</h2>

**The claim is attractive and unsupported.** Ann Gurney married John Gilman, a worsted weaver, at Hingham, Norfolk, on 1 October 1626; their children were baptised at West Dereham and Hingham; Ann was buried at Hingham on 23 November 1651; and their son John emigrated to Exeter, New Hampshire. If Ann was John's sister, a second child of Francis's first marriage crossed the Atlantic, and the corridor argument gains a documented instance.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n42" id="ref-42">42</a></sup>

**What is missing is the link from this bride to Francis's household.** The only authority for it is the same unsourced Pease entry that supplies the son John, and it gives Ann the same "c.1610" year — a compiler's placeholder rather than a researched date. A search of Gurney-variant brides against Gilman-variant grooms in the Norfolk marriage index returns four results, none of them an Ann Gurney and John Gilman, and none before 1667.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n43" id="ref-43">43</a></sup>

The 1626 Hingham marriage is a real record and Hingham was genuinely Gurney country — the manor of "Hingham Gurneys" was an ancient family holding. **But nothing connects this Ann to Francis, and the section is retained as a lead rather than as evidence.** Note also that West Dereham, where her first children were baptised, lies some twenty miles from East Dereham; the similar name has caused confusion, and the two are different places.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n42" id="ref-42b">42</a></sup>

<h2 id="s8">8. THE OTHER JOHN GURNEYS</h2>

England held a great many men named John Gurney in this period — more than forty distinct heads of household between 1600 and 1670, and five to seven simultaneously in Buckinghamshire alone. Identifying the emigrant means accounting for all of them.

**The test that survives that density** is that a household is eliminated only by a record that individuates **the man** — a burial, a probate, a tax certificate, a tenancy — or by a chronological impossibility. Continuity of the surname in a parish register is not enough, because in a county with several simultaneous John Gurneys, one man's departure is invisible.

**One further check is unusually powerful and easy to miss.** The colonial John's first wife Mary lived until September 1661. **Any Englishman who marries a different woman between 1637 and 1661 is therefore not the Braintree John** — which converts a great many ordinary marriage entries into eliminations.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n44" id="ref-44">44</a></sup>

### 8.1 Candidate A and the Buckinghamshire evidence

**Candidate A is two men, and both are eliminated.** The records once read as one continuous household split at 1641 on their own evidence.

- **A(i), the certificate man.** Married Alice Oliffe at Bierton with Broughton on 24 April 1628. In 1641 the Exchequer issued a certificate of residence showing "John Gurney to be liable for taxation in Northamptonshire, and not in the half-hundred of Aylesbury, Buckinghamshire, the previous area." He was a tenant at Walgrave, Northamptonshire, by 1650.
- **A(ii), the Saint Mary Aylesbury father.** Five children baptised at Saint Mary, Aylesbury, between 1638 and 1653.

**Why they are two men.** A government record that a specific John Gurney had stopped being taxable in the Aylesbury half-hundred in 1641 is hard to reconcile with the same man fathering children at Aylesbury in 1645, 1647 and 1653. Aylesbury demonstrably held more than one John Gurney — separate marriages there in 1620, 1638 and 1644 — and the Saint Mary baptisms are indexed with a father and no mother, so no index can assign the children to a wife. **Splitting them strengthens the elimination, because it eliminates two men rather than one.**<sup class="fn"><a href="#n45" id="ref-45">45</a></sup>

A later record closes any surviving reading: **John Gurney married Mary Kidgell at Edlesborough in 1661** — the same year the colonial John buried his first wife and remarried at Braintree. Either this is A(i) himself, in which case that marriage eliminates him on its own, or he is yet another Buckinghamshire John with a wife named Mary.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n46" id="ref-46">46</a></sup>

**Land and estate records add a second individuating tier, and every man they name is a man who stayed.** The county archives yield four John Gurney households the parish registers could not individuate: a **John Gurney, gentleman, of Bishopstone**, who sold the family's capital messuage and about 115 acres in 1648/9 for £1,700, alive in England thirteen years too late and with a son and heir named Edward; **John Gurney the younger of Owlswick**, settled in marriage in 1619; **John Gurney of Farnham Royal**, yeoman, who married Susan in 1629 and sold meadow in 1633; and **John Gurney of Aylesbury, ironmonger**, who held parish office from 1652 to 1660. **Not one of them disappears.** Where the Buckinghamshire record is rich enough to individuate a man, that man is visibly settled.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n47" id="ref-47">47</a></sup>

The Bishopstone household deserves a moment on its own, because it is the structural twin of this file's motive argument: **a landed Gurney family liquidating its whole estate**, in Buckinghamshire rather than Norfolk. It shows that "the family sold everything, so a son had reason to leave" is not a Norfolk-specific circumstance.

### 8.2 Candidate C — Berkhamsted, Hertfordshire

**Candidate C is two men, and only the elder is eliminated.** Searched by parent rather than by child, the Great Berkhamsted baptisms to a father John Gurney run from **1597 to 1637** — forty years, which is not one man. The natural break is the nine-year gap between Sara 1615 and Jhon 1624, and **a Jhon Gourney was buried in the parish in 1620**, between the two.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n48" id="ref-48">48</a></sup>

- **C(i), the elder John** — six children 1597–1615. Fathering from 1597 puts his birth about 1570–75, twenty-five to thirty years before the colonial John. **Eliminated on age**, a ground immune to the same-name problem.
- **C(ii), the younger John** — Jhon 1624, Richard 1626, Elizabeth 1629, Michaell 1631, Sarah 1634, Francis 1636/7. Fathering from 1624 puts his birth about 1598–1602, so **about 51 in 1653** — the best documented age fit in the whole field. He carries three colonial forenames, and no other English household reaches three.

**Why C(ii) does not rise higher: his household stopped because his children were dying, not because he left.** Great Berkhamsted's burial register survives and holds sixteen Gurney-variant burials between 1579 and 1643. Five fall between November 1637 and March 1638/9, and two of them — Elizabeth and Francis — match to the day the recorded death dates of two of C(ii)'s own children.

Three further constraints run against him.

1. **No Mary Gurney is baptised in the parish at any date**, under any spelling or any father, between 1544 and 1888.
2. **No John Gurney marriage occurs anywhere in Hertfordshire between 1600 and 1654**, so his wife cannot be named from county records and cannot be tested against a Mary.
3. **A Richard Gurney was buried in the parish on 28 June 1638.** If that is C(ii)'s son baptised in 1626, the candidate collapses outright — his best name-match child died in England while the colonial Richard was alive in Massachusetts into the 1680s. The parish held four contemporaneous Gurney households (fathers John, Thomas, Edward and William), so the burial may instead be the Richard baptised in 1635 to the Thomas household. **The index cannot tell them apart; the register image can.**

What remains open in his favour is only that the head himself has no recorded burial after 1620 — and the record set states that many Hertfordshire registers are defective for 1643–1660, so a death in that window would be invisible.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n49" id="ref-49">49</a></sup>

### 8.3 The wider Buckinghamshire and Chiltern cluster

Buckinghamshire held a dense cluster of further John Gurney households, each documented by a different parish, wife and child set. The heads consistent with the emigrant's age window carry their own rows above; the rest fall into three groups, none of which can be the emigrant:

- **Generational predecessors** — men fathering children before about 1620, when the colonial John was still a boy: Haddenham (1620, 1622), Great Kimble (married 1619), Weston Turville's earlier record.
- **Continuing English households** — East Claydon (John and Elizabeth, John buried there 1654), Chesham (John and Elizabeth, burials 1672 and 1678), Wing (children 1650, 1652), Cublington (John and Mary, son Isaac 1664, wife Mary buried 1669).
- **Post-1660 baptisms and marriages** — Chenies 1644, Aylesbury 1644 and 1669, Edlesborough 1663, Cublington 1666, Puttenham 1670, High Wycombe and Whitchurch 1671, Stone 1673, and in Hertfordshire Tring 1655 and a Northchurch probate of 1661. All are generationally later; they document the Aylesbury Vale and Chiltern Gurney families expanding, not fresh candidates.

Each is listed with its individual clearance ground in the §8.8 table.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n50" id="ref-50">50</a></sup>

### 8.4 Candidate D — the Old Change draper's son

**Candidate D holds the best trade and age fit of any rival, and has no household at all.** He is John Gurney, son and executor of Robert Gurney, citizen and draper of Old Change in the City of London. His father was described as **a tailor at Old Change** from his own admission in 1581, and John was admitted to the Drapers' Company by redemption on 11 February 1623/4 — an admission that implies a birth about 1598–1603, an excellent fit to "aged 50 or thereabouts" in 1653.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n51" id="ref-51">51</a></sup>

**What holds him at a low probability is a chronological argument that is strong but not airtight.** Two London tax returns twenty-four years apart place a John Gurney at the same St Augustine Watling Street address: a 1638 rents return at £10, and a 1662 hearth-tax entry at one hearth, assessed "poore." The 1662 entry falls in the same calendar year the colonial John was dying at Boston. **But the two returns are linked by parish and precinct rather than by a record naming Robert's son twice** — it is a strong reading, not a document.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n52" id="ref-52">52</a></sup>

A second, independent ground runs the same way: **no marriage of this John to a wife named Mary, and no baptism of any of the colonial children, appears in any London parish register or company record for 1620–1641.** He is a documented man with no household.

**His burial has never been searched, and it cannot be searched the obvious way** — a St Augustine Watling Street burial is not reachable by parish keyword in the main burial index and has to be routed through Boyd's London Burials or the parish register itself. A burial before 1641 would eliminate him outright; one after 1662 would confirm his continuity. **It is the single record that would settle the row, and it has never been pulled.**

### 8.5 The 1636 Newgate apprentice — a different man

**The 1636 apprentice and the Braintree man are not the same person**, and separating them explains a persistent piece of family tradition. On 21 July 1636 John Newgate brought his apprentice John Gurney before the Boston governor, and the court ordered service until age 24, three years from the following 29 September. That sets the apprentice's birth at about 29 September 1615.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n53" id="ref-53">53</a></sup>

Two independent tests separate the two men.

1. **Family status.** The Braintree John had married Mary by about 1627 and had three or four English-born children by the late 1630s. The apprentice was a servant under indenture until September 1639, and apprentices were customarily unmarried; a court extending a married father of four's service to age 24 is not a credible reading of the order.
2. **Chronology.** A 1615 birth sits twelve to thirteen years from the 1653 deposition and outside any reasonable rounding of "about 50."

**This also explains two loose ends.** John Newgate himself came from Horningsheath, Suffolk, three miles from Bury St Edmunds, and the apprentice was most plausibly drawn from Newgate's Suffolk network — which cleanly accounts for the genealogist Charles Banks's Bury St Edmunds attribution, most likely tracking the apprentice rather than the older Braintree man. And the recurring American family tradition of a 29 September 1615 birth and a Southwark origin fits the apprentice exactly, and should not be carried as a chronology for the emigrant.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n54" id="ref-54">54</a></sup>

### 8.6 The Buckinghamshire probate tier

Most Buckinghamshire husbandmen and shepherds proved their wills in the **Archdeaconry of Buckingham**, not at Canterbury, and that series is not indexed item by item in the national catalogue — so a Prerogative Court search leaves the county substantially unsearched. The printed index to that local tier carries **forty-two Gurney-variant testators to 1660, of whom nine are named John**.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n55" id="ref-55">55</a></sup>

**Three of the nine are individuating English deaths that eliminate a household outright:**

- **John Gurney of Monks Risborough, 1623**
- **John Gurney of Hulcott, yeoman, 1630** — eliminating the Hulcott household whose daughter Joyce was baptised there in 1603
- **John Gurney of Southcott in Linslade, 1636** — a death at the very start of the emigration window, so the Linslade records stopping in 1636 is explained by a death rather than a departure

**The index also corrects a reading of the Stewkley household.** The Stewkley Gurneys did not go silent after 1614: the probate index carries Robert Gurnie, husbandman, 1618; Robert Gorny, husbandman, 1631; and Robert Gurney, yeoman, 1651 — the last rising in status. The parish registers agree, with Gurney baptisms at 1615, 1616, 1618 and 1626, all to a father Robert. The family continued there for another forty years.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n56" id="ref-56">56</a></sup>

**Where the index is silent is equally load-bearing**, because it runs to 1660: **no John Gurney probate at all for Haddenham, Weston Turville, Hitcham, Amersham, Ivinghoe, or Bierton after 1559.** Those men cannot be eliminated from this direction.

**Two coverage limits bound the negatives.** Hertfordshire is absent from the published-index dataset entirely — a control search on a common surname also returns zero — so this is a missing volume rather than an absence of Gurneys, and the records themselves survive at Hertfordshire Archives. Northamptonshire is represented only by administration indexes from 1677, so A(i)'s death window is not covered.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n57" id="ref-57">57</a></sup>

**Two cautions govern how the blanks in that table should be read.**

**"No probate" says very little.** Probate exists only for men with property worth proving, so a probate blank is nearly meaningless for a husbandman or a country tradesman. The record that individuates an ordinary man is his **burial**.

**And a burial blank only counts where burials were being recorded.** Tested with a common-surname control in each parish: **Stewkley and Weston Turville are well covered** in the window, so an absent Gurney burial there means something — and at Stewkley, where the control passes, a John Gurney burial duly appears. **Hitcham has no seventeenth-century burial coverage at all**, its control returning a single burial dated 1827, so the Hitcham blank is uninterpretable. Cheddington's coverage is unproven.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n59" id="ref-59">59</a></sup>

**Why they are unaccounted for rather than live.** The colonial John was fathering children in England from about 1628 to about 1635. A household whose record stops in 1603, 1608, 1622, 1627 or 1631 is not baptising children anywhere these searches can see during exactly the years he demonstrably was. Sustaining any of them as a candidate requires positing an undocumented move to an unindexed parish, a marriage there, several baptisms there, and then emigration — **the same absence-explaining structure that weakens Candidate B, applied to men with far less circumstantial support.** In a county holding five to seven simultaneous John Gurneys, being unaccounted for is the ordinary state of a thin record, not evidence of departure.

<h2 id="s9">9. SEPARATING THE TWO FRANCIS GURNEYS</h2>

Two men named Francis Gurney lived in the same parishes at the same time, and the identification collapses if the wrong one is picked up. The antiquary Walter Rye first separated them.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n62" id="ref-62">62</a></sup>

| | **Francis-A — "our" Francis** | **Francis-B — the laceweaver** |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin** | West Barsham, Norfolk gentry | Unknown; a Norwich tradesman |
| **Trade** | Merchant Taylor, freed 1606 | Laceweaver |
| **Norfolk parish** | East Dereham, about 1608–1618 | Norwich, St Peter Mountergate |
| **London parish** | St Benet Fink, 1619–1637 | St Giles Cripplegate, 1638–1640 |
| **First wife** | Margaret Rybett, married 1611 | Unknown |
| **Second wife** | Anne Browning | Mary |
| **Costessey property** | Not this Francis | Almost certainly this Francis |
| **Death** | 9 January 1646/7, St Botolph Bishopsgate | Unknown |

**The two are now separated by simultaneous independent records rather than by trade alone.** James Gurney, son of a Francis of St Peter Parmentergate — the laceweaver's own Norwich parish — was bound apprentice at Michaelmas 1627 and admitted a freeman of Norwich in 1648. In 1627 Francis the Merchant Taylor was baptising children at St Benet Fink in London. **Two Francis Gurney households, two cities, one year.**<sup class="fn"><a href="#n63" id="ref-63">63</a></sup>

**A third Francis belongs on the same watch-list.** A St Giles Cripplegate burial of December 1640 reads "John sonne of ffrancis Garney Joyner." The trade is joiner, not laceweaver, so this is neither of the two above — and the child was buried two days after his christening, so he is not the emigrant either.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n64" id="ref-64">64</a></sup>

**A fourth belongs to the generation after.** Francis Gourney and Ann baptised ten children at Maldon, St Mary the Virgin, Essex, between 1655 and 1667, including a **John (1655)** and a **Thomas (1660)**. Those two supply, at register level, the bachelor John of Maldon who died in 1681 with letters of administration to his brother Thomas — a family composition that had rested on compiled authorities alone. The household begins in 1655, a generation after the emigration window, so it bears on Francis's descendants rather than on the colonial John.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n65" id="ref-65">65</a></sup>

### 10.3 The argument in connected form

**Four threads tie John to Francis's household, and none of them is a document naming him a son.**

*Trade.* Francis was a Merchant Taylor and the colonial John was a tailor. Of the named candidates only Candidate D shares any textile link, and he stayed in London; the remaining eliminated men were landholders, yeomen or shepherds. But no register records the transmission, and Buckinghamshire has a Gurney tailoring trade of its own.

*Corridor.* Francis's commercial life sat inside the East Anglia-to-London belt that produced most Massachusetts Bay emigrants of the 1630s. His parish adjoined Coleman Street Ward; his brother was a militant Puritan rector. The *Diligent* of Ipswich, carrying Norfolk passengers to Hingham, Massachusetts — under ten miles from Weymouth — in 1638, is the corridor event nearest in time and place to John's appearance at Weymouth in June 1641.

*Motive.* The 1634 sale liquidated everything. This is documented rather than inferred.

*Network.* The colonial associations are real but they describe reception rather than origin — where John landed and who received him, not where he came from.

**One long-standing rationale for ranking Candidate B above documented rivals does not hold.** It is tempting to say that only Candidate B has an evidenced reason and route to leave England. The colonial record does not support that.

- **Candidate B has an English-side corridor with no Gurney at the colonial end.** Francis's geography, the 1634 liquidation, the Puritan uncle and the Coleman Street proximity all describe a departure environment. None of it puts a Gurney on a ship or in a New England town record.
- **The Buckinghamshire candidates have a colonial-side reception channel with no Gurney at the English end.** Two of John Gurney's own land-neighbours at Weymouth came from Wendover and Ashton Clinton — three to five miles from parishes where Gurneys demonstrably lived. Weymouth had a documented settler stream out of the Aylesbury Vale and no Norfolk stream at all.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n70" id="ref-70">70</a></sup>

**Neither side has the middle link.** The right statement is a symmetry, not an advantage.

**Essex, searched from the English end, gives a mixed result.** The two parishes the colonial evidence names directly are empty: the county register set holds **one** Gurney-variant baptism at Finchingfield across its whole run, and **none at Stanford Rivers**. But the country four miles from the Tyng estate is not empty — a cluster of Gurney-plausible households sits in the Epping, North Weald Bassett and Fyfield triangle right through the emigrant's window, and one of its records names a **father John in 1616**. Whether that family wrote itself Gurney, Gurnett or Gurnard cannot be settled from index entries; it needs the registers, and that is the specific next step.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n71" id="ref-71">71</a></sup>

### Reading the table

**No option exceeds twenty-two percent, and the leading rows are not people.** Three of them describe the state of the record rather than a man, and together they hold **forty-four percent** — more than every named candidate combined. **The honest reading is that the identification is open.** External calibration agrees: Anderson's *Great Migration Directory* gives John's origin as "Unknown."

**Candidate B remains the leading named candidate**, because the circumstantial case is real and every rival is independently eliminated or reduced. It is not close to a documentary identification, and this file does not present it as one.

**The candidates fall into two groups, and no candidate is in both.** This is the most useful thing the exercise produced.

- **Documented households with the wrong shape** — C(ii), Hitcham, Ackworth and the eliminated Eythorne each have a father, sometimes a mother, and children with dates. What they lack is the right children.
- **Documented men with no household at all** — Candidate B, Cheddington 1608, A(1603), Candidate D and Weston Turville are, respectively, the best trade-and-corridor fit, the only candidate reached by prediction, the exact age match, the best trade match and the best Weymouth-proximity fit. Every one of them has no wife and no children on record.

**The emigrant needs a man of the right age *and* his household, and every candidate supplies one half.** That is the actual structure of the problem, and it is the strongest argument in this file for the record-state rows: **the man's own baptism and his household's baptisms are probably not in the same searchable place.**<sup class="fn"><a href="#n69" id="ref-69b">69</a></sup>

### Each leading row implies different work

- The **unassembled** row is tested by re-sorting records already in hand. That has been repeatedly productive and is now largely spent: with the father axis enumerated to completion, the mother axis swept, and the marriage settled on five instruments, the searched corpus contains exactly **two** unaccounted-for households in the window — Hitcham and Ackworth — and both already carry named rows.
- The **not-yet-searched** row is tested by opening named sources that exist and have never been consulted: the south-Midlands archdeaconry licence series, the Suffolk gap, the Norwich licence bonds of 1606–11, and the Buckinghamshire archdeaconry wills.
- The **no-record** row is moved only by new discovery or by Y-DNA, which bypasses records entirely.
- **Candidate B** can now only be advanced from outside East Dereham.

### What would change these numbers

A probability table is exactly the kind of artefact that looks like rigour while resting on judgement. Two guards apply: every row names the evidence it turns on, and the numbers are stated so a record can move them. Specifically:

- **If a Norwich marriage licence bond turns up a Francis Gurney first wife between 1606 and 1611**, Candidate B should rise sharply.
- **If the 1626 will of Richard Gurney of Cheddington names a son John living at that date**, that row should rise sharply — a will naming children is the one document that can turn a predicted household into a documented one.
- **If the Berkhamsted burial register entry for 28 June 1638 reads "son of John"**, C(ii) should fall to near zero; if it reads "son of Thomas," C(ii) recovers what it lost.
- **If the East Dereham household proves to belong to a different Francis**, Candidate B should fall toward the record-state rows.
- **If the south-Midlands licence series, the Norwich licence bonds and the Buckinghamshire archdeaconry wills are all opened and return nothing**, the *not-yet-searched* row should fall — those are named, extant sources, and the row is largely made of them.
