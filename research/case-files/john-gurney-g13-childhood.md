# John Gurney G13 — the childhood question

**What this file is.** A standing assessment of one question: where and how did John Gurney
grow up in England, between his birth at East Dereham in Norfolk about 1609/10 and his first
appearance in Massachusetts in 1641?

It is a companion to the main identification case file, which argues *who* John was. This
file assumes that identification and asks the next question: *what happened to him.* It moves
from the largest frame down to the smallest, and it ends with a reconstruction that is
explicitly guesswork, labelled as such.

---

## 1. The 100,000-foot view — what kind of story is this?

The search has long been framed as **"which household raised John?"** — and an honest,
exhaustive hunt has been built around that question. But the evidence that has accumulated
tells a different and, I think, better story. It is not really a story about a lost child. It
is a story about **a gentry family that ran out of land and tried to convert itself into
credit — and the credit failed.**

Henry Gurnay of Great Ellingham, John's grandfather, was a cultivated man. He kept a notebook
of some six hundred poems, collected books and wrote his opinions of everything he borrowed,
and corresponded with one of the largest named social circles anyone has catalogued for the
Tudor and early Stuart period.[^tanner][^swann] He was lord of West Barsham, Great Ellingham,
Irstead and a manor at Hingham, and in 1587 he bought back Harpley, the medieval Gurney seat
his ancestors had held three centuries earlier.[^blomefield-g15]

He also had **twelve children and a fixed amount of land.**[^henry-1614-will]

What happened to those children is the whole story in miniature. The eldest, Thomas, married
a knight's daughter and then **died in 1614 or early 1615, leaving a five-year-old
heir**[^nichols][^norris-1615] — so the main estate fell into a long minority, meaning the
land was held by a widow and by guardians rather than by a grown owner. One son, Edmund, went
into the church and became a well-known Puritan preacher.[^blomefield-edmund] And the sixth
son, **Francis** — John's father — was sent to London at seventeen to be apprenticed into
trade.[^mt-francis]

Francis is the interesting one, and the material now supports a sharp characterisation of him.
**He was not a tailor.** He was the family's credit man: the person who converted a Norfolk
gentry name into commercial trust. For twenty-four years he kept accounts for the L'Estrange
family of Hunstanton Hall, paying their London creditors and the East India Company on their
behalf, supplying them goods, and taking their deliveries at his own house.[^ukda-5726] He
borrowed heavily on his own account — one entry charges him two years' interest on £100 owed
to a London tailor and moneylender named Lock.[^ukda-lock] That is a respectable and
precarious way to live, and it ended badly.

**So the frame this file proposes: John Gurney was not misplaced. He was surplus.** A
first-marriage son in a family with no land left, a father whose credit was failing, and —
after 1619 — a stepmother producing a new child roughly every eighteen months.[^st-benet-fink]
That is one of the commonest shapes of person in the Great Migration to New England.

## 2. The 30,000-foot view — the six childhoods John could have had

When a boy of this class lost his mother young, there were a limited number of places he could
end up. Each leaves a different documentary trace, and each has been tested to a different
depth. Here they are, from the closest circle outward.

### (a) His own father's household

The obvious answer, and the weakest one — because Francis's household kept moving and, for
long stretches, was in two places at once.

The family were at **East Dereham** in Norfolk while John was small; his baptism and those of
several siblings are in that parish register.[^east-dereham] His mother Margaret died about
1616–17, and Francis remarried **Anne Browning** within a year or so.[^bernau] From 1619 the
new family was baptising children at **St Benet Fink** in the City of London.[^st-benet-fink]
But in October 1622 Francis signed a contract with the borough of King's Lynn that required
him to "inhabit and dwell" there until Michaelmas 1625 — while London baptisms continued
throughout.[^lynn-deed][^st-benet-fink]

So for at least three years the household was demonstrably split, and the pattern looks
lifelong rather than exceptional. A glimpse from 1634 captures it: another family's son,
Roger L'Estrange, had his trunks and bedding carried from school at Eton to "Frank Gurney's
house," and from there onward to Cambridge, with Francis himself travelling to fetch
him.[^roger-sequence][^roger-venn] That is a household working as a waystation, not a settled
home.

**Verdict: the least likely single answer, and probably the wrong question.** Francis's home
was not a fixed place for most of John's childhood.

### (b) His father's family — the Gurneys at Great Ellingham and West Barsham

John's grandfather Henry lived at Great Ellingham until his death in 1623, and Francis had
several brothers who were John's uncles.[^henry-will-1621] On the face of it, a wealthy
grandfather four counties closer than London is an excellent candidate.

Two findings weaken it. First, **the uncle who held the family seat was already dead.** Thomas
died in 1614/15, before John was orphaned, so his household was never available.[^nichols][^norris-1615]
Second, when Henry made his last will in 1621 he **named no grandchild at all** — not John,
not Edward, not anyone.[^henry-will-1621] A grandfather sheltering two motherless boys might
be expected to remember them.

**Verdict: possible but unevidenced, and quietly discouraged by the 1621 will's silence.**

### (c) The cousins at West Barsham — a widow's household full of children

This is the scenario that has become much better documented, and it deserves more weight than
it has had.

When Thomas died in 1614/15, his widow **Martha — a daughter of Sir Edward Lewkenor of
Denham** — was left holding the family deeds and raising a son and heir aged about
five.[^nichols][^norris-1615] That son, **Edward Gournay**, was therefore born about 1609/10:
**almost exactly John's age.** Edward's own will, made in 1639, names a brother Thomas and
five sisters — Susan, Dorothy, Elizabeth, Ellin and Margarett.[^west-barsham-wills]

So from 1615 onward there was a widowed gentlewoman running West Barsham with roughly seven
children of John's own generation in the house — precisely the years John needed raising, and
precisely the demographic a fostering hypothesis wants.

Against it sits a soft but pointed argument. Sir Nicholas L'Estrange kept a book of family
anecdotes and jokes which works as a social census of who was in the Gurneys' orbit in the
1630s and 40s. It names **Ned Gurney** of West Barsham, **his brother Thomas**, **their sister
Dorothy**, **uncle Edmund the parson**, and **Francis the London merchant** — John's own
father.[^nichols][^anecdote-roster] **John Gurney is not in it.** Absence from a jest-book
proves nothing on its own, but if John had grown up among those cousins you might expect him
to appear beside them.

**Verdict: the best-documented household, undercut by John's absence from the family's own
record of itself.**

### (d) His mother's family — the Rivetts of Garveston and Gressenhall

John's mother was a **Rivett of Garveston**, a village about four miles from East Dereham
where he was born. The family were copyholders there — meaning they held their land by
custom of the manor rather than owning it outright — and their presence in the parish runs
from 1547 to 1652.[^rivett-register]

Which Margaret Rivett she was is still unsettled: the parish yields two of the right name, one
baptised 1586 and one 1577/8, and the choice between them changes which relatives were nearest
in 1616.[^rivett-candidates] Under the first, John's grandmother **Elizabeth Rivett was alive
at Garveston until her burial in 1626** — a living grandmother four miles from his
birthplace.[^elizabeth-1626] Under the second, his mother's brother was **Richard Rivett of
Gressenhall**, three miles away, whose own five children were aged roughly five to fifteen
when John was orphaned — a ready-made set of foster siblings.[^richard-gressenhall]

And there is one plain, hard piece of evidence pointing this way that nothing else matches:
**John named a son Richard.**[^john-children] The best-documented adult male in his mother's
family is Richard Rivett of Gressenhall.

**Verdict: the strongest circumstantial case, resting on geography, a living grandmother or
uncle, and a name.**

### (e) A trade — apprenticeship, formal or informal

John worked as a **tailor** in Massachusetts; his own 1661/2 deed styles him "John Gurney of
Braintry Taylor in new England."[^john-tailor] His father belonged to the Merchant Taylors'
Company of London. That looks like an obvious inheritance — but it does not survive contact
with the records.

The Merchant Taylors' apprentice and freedom books for 1583–1800 contain **no John Gurney son
of Francis**, and no Gurney admitted by patrimony — the route by which a freeman's son could
claim membership through his father — anywhere at all.[^mt-no-john] The Norwich freemen
register and the enrolled Norwich apprenticeship indentures are likewise empty for the
window.[^norwich-negatives]

Both formal doors are shut. Whatever training John got, it was informal or provincial.

**Verdict: certainly happened, and certainly not through either official system — which is
itself a clue about who taught him.**

### (f) The wider circle — patrons, partners and creditors

Francis moved in a network that could in principle have absorbed a boy: the **L'Estranges of
Hunstanton**, whose accounts he kept for twenty-four years; **Lawrence Michael**, a London
tailor and moneylender who appears with Francis seven times between 1618 and 1622 and who is
separately recorded taking an apprentice in 1616; and the two partners of the King's Lynn
venture, a Thetford glover and a Great Ellingham man.[^ukda-5726][^ukda-michael][^lynn-deed]

The L'Estrange accounts have been searched in full. They name **no John, Edward or Anne
Gurney**, and no Gurney child in any household role — ward, apprentice, pupil, servant or
member.[^ukda-negative]

**Verdict: a real network, comprehensively searched, and empty of John.**

## 3. Four things the evidence now says that change the picture

### 3.1 The financial collapse was not an event in 1634. It was a slow foreclosure beginning by 1623.

This is the largest single reframing, and it sits in two records that had never been read
against each other.

- On **5 August 1623**, Francis gave a bond to **Sir Owen Smith**.[^smith-bond]
- On **11 July 1634**, Francis sold every acre he held in Norfolk and Suffolk, for £1,000, **to
  Sir Owen Smith of Irmingland**.[^smith-sale]

The same creditor, eleven years apart. The 1634 sale was in all likelihood the endgame of a
debt relationship that had been running for over a decade — a man being taken apart slowly
across the whole of his son's adolescence, rather than ruined suddenly when his son was
twenty-four. The original indenture survives at The National Archives and would settle
it.[^ward2-indenture]

### 3.2 That explains the biggest hole in the case — why John has no apprenticeship record.

The absence of any formal trade admission has stood as an unexplained mark against the
identification. But look at the dates.

In **October 1622** Francis took £200 of King's Lynn's money to run a wool-spinning scheme
employing the town's poor, and bound himself to live in the borough until September
1625.[^lynn-deed] The scheme failed. By 1625–26 the town was still chasing the debt, and **Sir
Hamon L'Estrange had to stand bound for Francis's £100.**[^lynn-obligations]

John was born about 1609/10. He was therefore **twelve to sixteen years old exactly while his
father's credit was collapsing** — precisely the age at which an apprenticeship is arranged,
and precisely the arrangement that requires money and standing. A father in that condition
cannot place his son in a London company.

The missing apprenticeship is not evidence against the relationship. It is what the
relationship predicts.

### 3.3 The trade match is being argued the wrong way round.

"Merchant Taylor father, tailor son" reads as strong support, but Francis was not practising
as a tailor. The Merchant Taylors were a merchant company by this date, and Francis's actual
occupation was finance and agency.[^ukda-5726] A man who worked with a needle in Braintree,
Massachusetts did not learn it from him.

That cuts both ways, and the two effects largely cancel: the *company-name* match is weaker
than it looks, but the *absence of company records* is correspondingly less damaging. What the
evidence really points to is a working craftsman master somewhere in the country, outside the
formal systems — which is what the maternal-kin reading would predict anyway.

### 3.4 John is missing from the family's own record of itself.

The L'Estrange anecdote book names five Gurneys across the 1630s and 40s, including John's
father, his great-uncle-by-marriage's circle, and three of his cousins at West
Barsham.[^anecdote-roster] John is not among them. Nor is he named in his grandfather's 1621
will,[^henry-will-1621] nor anywhere in the twenty-four years of L'Estrange
accounts.[^ukda-negative]

A boy raised inside the paternal family's social world should leave some mark on it. John
leaves none. This is soft evidence, but it all points the same way: **away from the Gurney
side.**

## 4. The 10,000-foot view — two readings corrected

**The 1622 witness is a town clerk, not a relative.** The King's Lynn deed ends `Teste me—Tho
Rivett coi clico`.[^lynn-witness] Because Francis's first wife was a Margaret Rybett, that
signature was for a time treated as a strong kinship signal. But the abbreviation expands most
naturally as *communi clerico* — **common clerk**, the town clerk — and the document is a
contract with the borough corporation, so the corporation's own clerk attesting it is entirely
routine. A same-name Lynn civic career follows the officer reading exactly: a Thomas Rivett
called "gentleman of Lynne" in 1629, and **Alderman** Thomas Rivett in
1646.[^rivett-1629][^rivett-1646] The kinship weighting was reasoning from a surname
coincidence in a town where the surname is common and the man's job explains his signature
completely. It should be treated as roughly even odds, and the cheap test is whether the same
clerk attests other Lynn deeds of the same years.

**Uncle Thomas's death date is settled, and it closes one scenario.** Four independent
witnesses put Thomas Gurney's death in 1614 or early 1615: a printed notice stating the year
outright in a passage about his wife's parentage;[^nichols] a memorandum of 23 March 1615
describing his widow holding the family deeds and his heir aged about five;[^norris-1615] an
indexed inventory of his goods for 1615–16;[^thomas-inventory] and his son's succession to the
estate as a *grandson* in 1623.[^dg-succession] Against those stands a single phrase in a will
of 1621.[^henry-will-1621] The consequence for this file is direct: **uncle Thomas's household
was gone before John needed one.**

## 5. Educated guesswork

*This section is inference, not evidence. It is offered because the shape of the evidence now
supports a reconstruction, and because a stated guess is easier to test than an unstated one.*

His mother died when he was six or seven and his brother Edward four or five. His father
remarried within a year or two and began a second family in London which, between 1619 and
1637, produced at least nine more children.[^st-benet-fink][^bernau]

I do not think there was a single foster household, and I suspect that looking for one is why
the search keeps coming up empty. **Francis's household was structurally split for his whole
career** — a travelling agent with business in Norfolk, obligations at King's Lynn and a
family in London. The 1634 glimpse of another man's son having his trunks delivered to "Frank
Gurney's house" on the way to Cambridge is the clearest picture we have of how that household
actually worked: a place things and people passed through.[^roger-sequence]

My reconstruction is that the two boys stayed in mid-Norfolk with their mother's relatives, in
the triangle formed by Garveston, Gressenhall and East Dereham — no more than four miles
across — while their father travelled and their stepmother's London house filled with babies.
John's tailoring was learned locally, from a working craftsman, which is why it appears in no
company register.[^mt-no-john][^norwich-negatives]

The strongest single support is also the plainest: **John named a son Richard**, and the
best-documented adult in his mother's family is Richard Rivett of
Gressenhall.[^john-children][^richard-gressenhall] The supporting pattern is his complete
absence from every paternal record that survives — the grandfather's will, the family's
anecdote book, and twenty-four years of the L'Estrange
accounts.[^henry-will-1621][^anecdote-roster][^ukda-negative]

And the motive for leaving follows from the same reconstruction. By his mid-twenties John was
an adult son of a first marriage in a family whose land had just been sold to its principal
creditor, with eight or nine young half-siblings in a London house he may barely have lived
in.[^smith-sale][^st-benet-fink] He appears in Massachusetts seven years after the sale. The
usual phrase for this is "no land to expect." **Displacement** is the better word.

## 5A. Temporary working section — the marriage parish, the birth date, and three silences

*Marked temporary. This section holds a hypothesis under active test, not a finding. It should
be either promoted into the body or removed once the East Dereham baptism entry has been
re-read.*

### The marriage parish is not explained — the standing explanation has been superseded

Francis and Margaret married at **St Martin at Palace, Norwich**, on 23 September 1611 — a
parish in which neither family is otherwise documented.[^marriage-1611] The standing
explanation has been that St Martin at Palace was **Margaret's own family church**, on the
basis that the Rybett or Ryvett family were Norfolk and Suffolk gentry and that the parish
register carries Rivett entries running 1539–1603.[^stmartin-claim]

That reasoning depended on identifying Margaret with the **gentry** Ryvetts of Rishangles,
Rattlesden and Bildeston. The maternal-family research has since identified her as a **Rivet
of Garveston** — the daughter of a **copyholder**, a customary tenant of the manor, in a
village roughly fifteen miles west of Norwich. Whether the Garveston family are related to the
Suffolk gentry Ryvetts at all is unresolved, and the clearest status marker points against it:
the Suffolk Ryvetts generated Court of Wards business because they held land directly of the
Crown, while no mid-Norfolk Rivett appears in that series at all. "Rybett" was the 1611 clerk's
one-off spelling; the family writes itself *Rivet*.[^garveston-status]

There may well be Rivetts at St Martin at Palace, but on the present identification they are
**Norwich city Rivetts** with no established link to the Garveston copyholders. The parish is
therefore an open question, not a settled one.

### The sequence is one journey, not a move and a return

Read as "born in one parish, married in a second, then straight back to the first," the
chronology feels awkward. That is not its shape. **East Dereham is the constant** — it is where
Francis traded, where the children were baptised, and where the household lived. Norwich
appears exactly once, for a single day. They rode a day to marry and came home.

Travelling to marry away from the home parish was ordinary, and it was done for a reason:
distance from local eyes. Two reasons for wanting that distance are already documented here.
The first is **status**. Francis was the sixth son of Henry Gurnay, Esquire — lord of four
manors, armigerous, a family the Heralds visited.[^blomefield-g15][^henry-1614-will] Margaret
was a copyholder's daughter.[^garveston-status] The second is the possibility that **a child
had already been born**.

### The birth date already sits before the marriage

The considered position on John's birth is **about 1602–1610, most probably about
1603–1608**, drawn from three estimators — the 1652/3 deposition age, the East Dereham
baptism reading of about 1609/10, and the bound set by his daughter Mary's 1647 marriage. Two
of the three pull earlier than the baptism.[^birth-window] On that assessment John was born
**three to six years before his parents married**, and possibly eight.

### Three silences, and one explanation that would cover them

Six features of the record have been treated as separate puzzles:

1. John is absent from the Heralds' Visitation of London of 1633, where his father presented
   the pedigree and Roger was called eldest son.[^visitation-1633]
2. His parents married in a parish belonging to neither family.[^marriage-1611]
3. His most probable birth window precedes that marriage.[^birth-window]
4. He is absent from his grandfather's will of 1621.[^henry-will-1621]
5. He is absent from the family's anecdote book, where his father and three cousins
   appear.[^anecdote-roster]
6. He has no formal apprenticeship or company freedom anywhere.[^mt-no-john][^norwich-negatives]

**A single hypothesis would account for all six: that John was born before his parents
married, and was therefore never entered in the family's status-bearing records.**

The legal position matters here. Under English common law a child born before the parents
married was illegitimate **and remained so** — England specifically declined to adopt
legitimation by subsequent marriage. The consequences are concrete rather than merely social:
such a child could not inherit land; he **could not claim the freedom of a London livery
company by patrimony**, the route requiring legitimate birth to a freeman; and he would not be
entered in a Heralds' Visitation pedigree, which is a formal declaration of standing to the
College of Arms. The Merchant Taylors' negative — no Gurney freedom by patrimony in a
3,391-row sheet — becomes a predicted result rather than an anomaly.[^mt-no-john]

**The sharpest supporting detail is the second John.** Francis had another son named John, by
Anne Browning — John of Maldon, who lived at St Mary's Maldon until 1681 and died a
bachelor.[^john-maldon] English families reused a forename after a child died. Two *living*
sons of one father bearing the same name, by two marriages, is unusual unless the first was
not being counted in the formal line.

### What argues against it

The baptism entry as currently read says **"John the sonne of ffrancis Gurnie"** — naming the
father plainly.[^entry-e-reading] Illegitimate baptisms were frequently marked, either by a
qualifying word such as *base* or by naming the mother instead. A plain patronymic entry leans
legitimate. The year on that page is inferred rather than read, with a margin of two to three
years, so the baptism could fall after September 1611 and dissolve the question
entirely.[^entry-e-dating] And the existing explanation for the Visitation absence — an adult
son living independently in another county, simply not presented — remains reasonable on its
own.[^visitation-1633]

### The structural risk this exposes

If John was born before September 1611, **Margaret's motherhood is not established.** The only
thing making her his mother is the assumption that he was a child of that marriage. Remove it
and the baptism attests Francis's paternity and nothing about the mother.

That matters because a chain rests on it: John named a son Richard; the best-documented adult
male in Margaret's family is Richard Rivett of Gressenhall; therefore maternal Rivett kin;
therefore the fostering ground is the Garveston–Gressenhall triangle — the reconstruction in
§5 above. The chain is not empty, because the name Richard is itself mild evidence that
Margaret *was* his mother, but it is mildly circular. The identification case file's title,
"son of Francis Gurney **and Margaret Rybett**," asserts more than the baptism carries;
"son of Francis Gurney" is what it supports.

### The test

**Re-read the East Dereham baptism entry at full resolution as an open transcription** —
looking not for the surname, which has been worked hard, but for whether the clerk qualified
the child's status, whether a mother is named, and whether the page carries a year. That
single line bears on the birth date, the legitimacy question, and Margaret's maternity at
once.[^entry-e-reading][^entry-e-dating]

## 6. What to do next

The return on enumerating possible households has gone to zero. The return on reading actual
documents has been high. These are the documents that would move the question, in order of
value. All are at The National Archives at Kew unless noted.

| **C 142/613/60** | The inquiry held after Edward Gournay's death in 1641/2, recording what he held and **the age of his heir**. Fixes a generation of dates.[^c142-613] |
| **C 5/609/120** | Chancery suit of 1648: Edward's widow Frances and her son Henry against Edmund Cocke over Great Ellingham. The pleadings state relationships.[^chancery-cocke] |

| **WARD 7/57/80**, **WARD 7/57/157** | The inquiries following uncle Thomas's death, 1616/17 — confirming the death date and naming the heir.[^ward7-thomas] |

- **Dorothy Gurney's will, proved 1641.** Edward's unmarried sister. An unmarried woman's will
  names siblings, nephews and nieces, which would test the whole West Barsham cousin group. A
  sweep of the Canterbury probate index shows no Dorothy Gurney in 1641, so her will must be
  at **Norwich**, not London.[^dorothy-will][^pcc-sweep]
- **The King's Lynn Hall Book for October 1622**, to settle whether there were two instruments
  and to confirm the town clerk's attestation.[^lynn-deed][^lynn-hmc]
- **The Whinburgh manor records at the Norfolk Record Office.** Whinburgh was the manor whose
  courts governed the land at Garveston where John's mother's family lived. Because they were
  copyholders, inheritance and the guardianship of orphans were handled in that manor's own
  court. Its records for 1604–1626 were never microfilmed and can only be read at
  Norwich — and they are the one series that could name John's mother's parents
  outright.[^whinburgh]
