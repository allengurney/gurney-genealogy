# La Tour Hue — the Gournay tower

The fortified citadel built by **Hugh de Gournay I (G36)** at Gournay-en-Bray, named after him in Old French (*Hue* = Hugh), and standing for approximately eight centuries before its early-to-mid-18th-century demolition. This topic file collects the layered French / Latin tradition on the tower, drawing on William Brito's *Philippide* (c. 1224), Père du Plessis's *Description de la Haute Normandie* (early 18th c.), Nicolas Cordier's MS *Histoire de Gournay* (c. 1710–1738), Daniel Gurney 1848 + DG-Supp 1858, Hannay 1867, Potin 1842, NRP-I 1852, Pattou *Racines Histoire*, and Painchault 2012.

---

## 1. The original construction — Hugh I as fortifier

The repo's `g36-hugh-de-gournay-i-fact-sheet.research.md` synthesises the surviving evidence on Hugh I's life. Briefly: Hugh I (G36) was the first lord of Gournay born in Normandy, contemporary with William Longsword (d. 942), and the founder of the citadel and tower at Gournay-en-Bray.

Per Père du Plessis, *Description de la Haute Normandie* T. premier (early 18th c.), quoted via Potin 1842 p. 76:

> "Il y bâtit assez près de l'église de Saint-Hildevert, une citadelle entourée d'un triple mur et d'un double fossé qui la rendait inaccessible, et fortifiée d'une tour appelée la Tour Hue, qui a subsisté jusqu'au commencement du siècle dernier; ses fossés, extrêmement profonds et remplis d'eau, en augmentaient tellement la force, qu'elle était capable de résister à une armée entière, dit Guillaume le Breton, *quand bien même il n'y aurait eu personne pour la défendre*."

Translation: *"He built, fairly near the church of Saint-Hildevert, a citadel surrounded by a **triple wall** and a **double ditch** which rendered it inaccessible, and fortified with a tower called la Tour Hue, which subsisted until the beginning of the last century; its ditches, extremely deep and full of water, augmented its strength so greatly that it was capable of resisting an entire army, says William the Breton, even when there would be no-one to defend it."*

The construction was concentrated near the future site of the **Collégiale Saint-Hildevert**, in the area Cordier later identifies as "le Chasteau" (distinct from "la ville," which developed later around the church of Notre-Dame).

## 2. William Brito's *Philippide* (c. 1224) — the original Latin source

Guillaume le Breton (William the Breton, c. 1165–1226), court poet of Philip Augustus, completed his epic *Philippide* c. 1224 celebrating Philip's reign. In Delaborde's critical edition the Gournay set-piece stands in **book VI** (verse lines 210–262, printed vol. 2 pp. 160–162), not in "lib. xi" as Daniel Gurney's citation (DG-I p. 24) transmits — the book-XI locator carried in earlier repo notes and via Potin 1842 p. 76 is an edition/citation slip. The `munitum triplice muro … inexpugnabile` verse Daniel Gurney quotes is line VI, 212; the index of the same volume fixes the four Gournay loci as description (VI, 210), Hugh de Gournay in command (VI, 216), the dam-breach capture (VI, 221), and Arthur of Brittany knighted at Gournay (VI, 262).

Standard published edition: H.-F. Delaborde (ed.), *Œuvres de Rigord et de Guillaume le Breton, historiens de Philippe-Auguste*, 2 vols. (Paris: Renouard, *Société de l'histoire de France*, 1882–1885). The book VI verses on Gournay are now transcribed verbatim in `sources/corpus_supplement/oeuvres-rigord-guillaume-breton-delaborde-vol2-gournay-philippide.md`; the key lines run:

> *Rure situm plano, munitum triplice muro,*
> *Deliciosa nimis speciosaque vallis habebat*
> *Nomine Gornacum, situ inexpugnabile solo,*
> *Etsi nullus ei defensor ab intus adesset;*
> *Cui multisque aliis preerat Gornacius Hugo.*

*"Set on level open country, fortified with a triple wall — a most delightful and handsome valley held it, Gournay by name, impregnable by the ground of its site even had no defender been within; over which, and many other places, Hugh de Gournay presided."* Brito then describes how Philip Augustus, unable to force the Epte-fed ditches, had the earthen dam (*terreus agger*) of a great pond above the town broken through the middle, so that the released deluge overwhelmed the walls: *"Diluvio capitur inopino; menia sternunt undosi assultus, arces brevis eruit unda"* — *"it is taken by an unlooked-for flood; the watery assaults throw down the ramparts, a brief wave overturns the citadel."* Rigord's prose chronicle §138 and the *Chronicon* c. 112 parallel the account. (Guillaume le Breton, *Philippide* VI, 210–262, ed. Delaborde vol. 2; Source ID `oeuvres-rigord-guillaume-breton-delaborde`.)

The *Philippide* describes Gournay's fortifications during Philip Augustus's campaigns in Normandy. By Brito's time (c. 1224), the fortifications included not just Hugh I's original tower but also any subsequent reinforcements (DG-Supp Note 7 p. 730 records Philip Augustus himself "appears to have repaired and extended the fortifications"). So Brito's description blends Hugh I's original 10th-century work with 12th–13th-century extensions — his "triple wall" matches Père du Plessis's *triple mur* at §1 above.

## 3. Nicolas Cordier MS *Histoire de Gournay* (c. 1710–1738) — topographical apparatus

Nicolas Cordier was curé of Notre-Dame de Gournay from 1710 to 1738. His MS *Histoire de Gournay* survives only as a transcription via Potin 1842 (preface acknowledges Cordier as one of his three principal manuscript sources, along with Langloys's late-17th-century notes and René Potin's notes). Whether the original Cordier MS still exists in any archive is unresolved.

Cordier's description of Gournay's fortifications, as preserved in Potin 1842 (pp. 77–82), covers four gates of the town and three gates of the Château:

> "Pour entrer en la ville, il y a quatre portes différentes, chacune garnie d'une herse ou sarrasine, qui se voit encore [c. 1710] dans les coulisses ménagées dans le corps de la muraille…et chacune de ces portes est accompagnée d'une seconde porte, ou avant-porte, éloignée de vingt toises de la première, ce qui fait que chaque porte est double.
>
> Avant cette porte intérieure, il y avoit un fossé couvert d'un pont de bois que l'on pouvoit lever ou oster et qui servoit de pont-levis afin de défendre la porte et empêcher les surprises. Ces fossez ont été comblez et remplis de terre depuis 30 ans [= since c. 1680] et l'on a pavé par-dessus, en sorte qu'il n'y a plus de pont-levis.
>
> Mais pour mieux faire comprendre la forme qu'avoit cette ville jadis, il faut distinguer la ville du Chasteau.
>
> Le Chasteau était le terrain qui forme la paroisse de Saint-Hildevert. Il avoit trois portes par où on y entroit; l'une qui étoit à l'horloge…une porte de ce chasteau dans la rue de Cantemesle [aujourd'hui rue de Berri]…la troisième porte étoit située au moulin où il y a encore deux arches par-dessous lesquelles passe l'eau de la rivière qui venant d'en haut tombe aux Planquettes."

Translation (selective): *"For entering the town, there are four different gates, each garnished with a portcullis or 'sarrasine,' still visible [c. 1710] in the slots reserved in the body of the wall…and each of these gates is accompanied by a second gate, or pre-gate, distant twenty toises from the first, so that each gate is double. Before this interior gate, there was a ditch covered with a wooden bridge which could be raised or removed and served as a drawbridge to defend the gate and prevent surprises. These ditches have been filled in with earth since 30 years ago [c. 1680] and one has paved over them, so that there is no longer any drawbridge."*

The **Château** (the inner fortification, the heart of Hugh I's "La Tour Hue" complex) had three gates:
1. The "horloge" gate (clock-tower gate), connecting the Château to the town — *"on y voit encore [c. 1710] les gonds sur lesquels tournoit la porte, et une coulisse ménagée dans le cintre de la muraille garnie de sa herse ou sarrasine."*
2. The "Cantemesle" gate (later renamed rue de Berri), beyond the Saint-Hildevert presbytery, with two flanking pierre stone towers documented in 16th-century titles ("on le voit dans les titres de 1500"). Later named the "porte Baudry."
3. The mill gate, at the riverside, two arches above the watercourse.

The gradual demolition is documented:
- c. 1680: surrounding ditches filled in.
- 1700s: gradual loss of the walls and towers.
- "Au commencement du siècle dernier" (Potin 1842 = early 1700s): La Tour Hue itself "a subsisté."
- DG-Supp Note 7 (1858) writing "a century ago": demolition c. 1750s.

## 4. Surviving fragments — what Cordier saw c. 1710 and what remained in Potin's day (1842)

Cordier's description (Potin 1842 pp. 80–81) records:
- Stone-arch fragments in private gardens and houses (Larcher, Bourgeois, Comte de Gaillon's properties).
- "Une tour est presque entièrement conservée dans la maison de M. Carruette."
- Wall remnants near the "petit boulevard" and "grands boulevards."
- Foundations of the Cantemesle / Baudry gate towers in the rue de Cantemesle.

DG-Supp Note 7 (1858):

> "La Tour Hue was of course of an earlier date; it is now entirely gone, although remaining a century ago."

This means the tower bearing Hugh's name survived until approximately 1758 (= 1858 minus 100 years), or somewhat earlier. By Potin's writing in 1842, it was already "au commencement du siècle dernier" — early 1700s — that the fortifications had largely come down.

L'Anglois (an 18th-century author of MS notes preserved by Potin) **disagreed** with DG and Père du Plessis on the original fortress extent. L'Anglois thought the original Château consisted only of the area later called the "Château" and was *not* of greater size during the rule of the ancient Gournay lords. L'Anglois believed there was originally a strong gate at what became the "tour de l'Horologe."

Père du Plessis and Brito describe a more extensive original — triple wall, double ditch, the named tower. The two readings are difficult to reconcile beyond noting that the 12th-13th-century Philip Augustus repairs (DG-Supp Note 7) likely extended whatever Hugh I built initially.

## 5. The wider fortification triad — Painchault 2012

Aude Painchault's 2012 chapter in *Journées archéologiques de Haute-Normandie* (Évreux 6–8 mai 2011), *Presses universitaires de Rouen et du Havre*, pp. 209–218, frames Gournay-La Ferté-Gaillefontaine as a coordinated **frontier-fortification triad** at the head of the Bresle valley.

> *"Les châteaux sont situés à la naissance de la vallée de la Bresle, à la frontière du duché et du royaume de France, et constituent un enjeu politique de premier ordre tant du côté français que normand."*

Translation: *"The castles are situated at the source of the Bresle valley, at the frontier of the duchy and the kingdom of France, and constitute a political stake of primary importance for both French and Norman sides."*

The triad's strategic role:
- **Gournay** (Hugh I, c. 980s–1040): the senior fortified caput, with La Tour Hue.
- **La Ferté** (founded by Gauthier de la Ferté, brother of Hugh II; château documented standing by 1000 per NRP-I 1852 p. 77): the cadet fortification commanding the Bresle valley.
- **Gaillefontaine** (forteresse from 1050 per Potin 1842 p. 66): the third frontier point.

In 1089–90, **Gerard de Gournay** (G32) delivered all three to William II Rufus (Orderic Vitalis [879] — see G32 §2.3). The triad continued to operate as a coordinated frontier system until Philip Augustus's 1202 conquest of the Norman seigneurie.

In 1118, Hugues IV de Gournay's rebellion against Henry I (Orderic Vital, also cited via Painchault 2012) re-mobilised the triad: *"en 1118, ces châteaux sont à nouveau mentionnés par Orderic Vital dans sa chronique, alors que Hugues de Gournay s'associe à d'autres seigneurs et se rebelle contre le nouveau duc de Normandie et roi d'Angleterre, Henri Ier Beauclerc."*

## 6. The post-Conquest Continental fortifications

- 1130: Hugues IV's father-in-law Hugh le Grand (Comte de Vermandois) and other Capetian-network kin would be aligned with the Gournay defensive posture.
- 1147: Hugues IV's crusading departure (Louis VII's Second Crusade) leaves the triad in stewardship.
- **1151**: Henri II of England burned La Ferté. (NRP-I 1852 p. 79 documents the resulting transfer of the la Ferté chanoines to Saint-Laurent-en-Lions.)
- **1180** or 1185: Hugues IV's death at end of his reign (DG: 1180; Pattou alternate: 1185 *"in the 30th year of Henry II's reign"*).
- c. 1180–1223: Philip Augustus repairs and extends the Gournay fortifications (DG-Supp Note 7).
- **1202**: Philip Augustus seizes Gournay and the broader Norman seigneurie. The end of Norman-line tenure.

By 1500 the towers were documented in town titles but had begun to deteriorate; by 1680 the surrounding ditches were filled in; by c. 1750–1760 La Tour Hue itself had been demolished.

## 7. The Saint-Hildevert connection

Hugh I built his citadel "fairly near the church of Saint-Hildevert" (Brito via Père du Plessis via Potin 1842 p. 76). The **Collégiale Saint-Hildevert** at Gournay-en-Bray, a 12th-century structure (still standing today), succeeded an earlier church on the same site that received the relics of Saint-Hildevert in 990 (Potin 1842 pp. 86–88). Before Saint-Hildevert, the church was dedicated to Saint-Étienne (pre-Frankish establishment) and then to Saint-Guitmar (after his burial there c. 750).

The Caister and Cantley English-parish tithes given to the Saint-Hildevert chapter at Gournay-en-Bray by Hugh III (G33, see G33 §5.3) — and confirmed by the Bishop of Norwich — form the documented **Channel-spanning ecclesiastical link** between the Norfolk barony cluster and the family's home Norman collégiale, anchored next to Hugh I's tower.

## 8. Open questions

1. **Brito *Philippide* Gournay verses** — resolved. Located in Delaborde's edition at book VI, lines 210–262 (vol. 2 pp. 160–162), transcribed verbatim in `sources/corpus_supplement/oeuvres-rigord-guillaume-breton-delaborde-vol2-gournay-philippide.md` and summarised in §2 above. Daniel Gurney's "lib. xi" locator does not match the Delaborde edition; the set-piece is book VI. Residual: whether a fuller Latin excerpt of the Arthur-knighting verse (VI, 262) is worth transcribing.

2. **Cordier MS *Histoire de Gournay***: survival status unknown. The Bibliothèque municipale de Gournay-en-Bray, the Bibliothèque nationale (Paris), and the archives départementales de Seine-Maritime are the most likely repositories.

3. **L'Anglois's "Les Andelys MS"**: cited by DG-Supp Note 7 as dissent on the original fortress extent. Survival status unknown. Was likely a 17th–18th-century antiquarian's manuscript; possibly preserved at Les Andelys / Évreux.

4. **The Saint-Hildevert / Saint-Hildebert distinction**: the Lorraine source (G37 §3.2) calls the church "S. Hildebert"; modern French toponymy uses "Saint-Hildevert" or "Hildevert." Probably a single saint with variant orthography.

5. **The 1750s demolition**: did the tower stones go into other Gournay constructions? A local building survey in modern Gournay-en-Bray could potentially identify reused stones.
