# Gurney false friends — a cross-cutting registry

A working registry of name-forms, surnames, and jurisdictional labels that recur across searches for the Gurney family and have been mistaken for a Gurney, or could be, before the underlying record was read in full. Each entry states the name-form as it actually appears in a record, the records it turns up in, and the resolution that separates it from a genuine Gurney — so that the next search does not re-run the same disambiguation. Most entries below arose from the G13 John Gurney research program and are cross-referenced to the fuller treatment there rather than restated; a few are general search-method notes that cut across every Gurney line.

## Massachusetts colonial name-forms

### Hingham GARDNER / GARNET

David Hobart's Hingham family journal records, under 10 April 1651, "**John Garnet maried at Boston**" — a plain **Garnet**, not Gurney, and the only "John G-" surname anywhere in the journal's transcribed pages close enough in form to warrant a second look.[^hobart-garnet] *History of the Town of Hingham* resolves it: the town's own genealogies carry a continuous **Gardner/Garnet** family running from this John Garnet (married Mary; he died by 18 June 1669, when "Mrs. Mary Garnet, the wid. of John Garnet" remarried) through Stephen and Sarah (Warren) Garnet, John and Rebecca, Samuel, Benjamin, and Simeon, deep into the eighteenth century, with the compilers repeatedly glossing the surname "Garnet, or Gardner." This John Garnet is the family's Hingham progenitor, and the 1651 Boston marriage the journal records is his.[^hingham-garnet] No seventeenth-century Gurney appears anywhere in Hingham's own town-history genealogies; the surname's only Hingham appearances before 1700 are this distinct Gardner/Garnet line. The corollary for search practice: any "Gurney-ish" string surfacing in Hingham records should be checked against **GARDNER/GARNET** first, before it is treated as a Gurney lead.[^hingham-garnet]

Hingham's later record does carry genuine Gurney in-marriages, all eighteenth-century and all incoming rather than native to the town: "Joseph Gurney of Wey[mouth], int. of m'ge, May 3, 1718"; a Job [—] who "m. a Gurney, and lived some time at West Hing[ham]"; and Sophia Gurney of Abington, married about 1800.[^hingham-garnet] None bears on the seventeenth-century question.

### Dorchester GURNELL

Four independent Dorchester-area record streams, spanning more than half a century, render one resident — John Gurnell — under a "Gurney"-adjacent spelling. Dorchester's own town book carries him as Gornell, Gornill, Gornehill, Gurnell, Gurnel, gornett, Gurnett, Gurnet, and Gurnit, including "**John Gurnet**" in the 13 (12) 1653 fence-viewers list; he was a town officer into the 1670s.[^dorchester-town-records] Margery Laver's 1664 Dorchester will leaves a keepsake "to **Jane Gurnet**," his son's widow.[^laver-will] The Suffolk County Court's own 1676/7 record of a bastardy case names a surety, "**Goodman Gurney of Dorchester**."[^suffolk-court-481] First Church Dorchester's record of 11 May 1709 still calls the same widow "**mrs. Burgesse alias Gurnet**," recovering a legacy she had left the church decades earlier.[^dorchester-first-church] And neighboring Dedham's own town-meeting book adds a fourth, independent witness with a new detail: on 2 January 1670/1 the town noted that "Thomas Walker, last Apprentice to **Goodman Gurney of Dorchester, a Tanner**," wished to set up his trade in Dedham.[^dedham-tanner] Dorchester's own town-record footnote resolves the widow directly: "Jane Gornell, widow of John Gornell, married John Burge" — the same Jane every other record calls "Gurnet."[^dorchester-town-records] No independent Gurney household existed at colonial Dorchester; every "Gurney"-form record there belongs to this one Gornell/Gurnell family, and the county court's own general index kept its "Gurney, ——" line separate from its Gornell/Gurnell entries only as an indexing artifact, not evidence of two families.[^suffolk-court-481]

The tanner's trade is a new discriminator worth keeping: John Gurney-1 of Braintree was a tailor, and his sons husbandmen and soldiers — no Gurney tanner existed in the colonial record, so a "Gurney, tanner" hit anywhere in New England should be read as this Dorchester Gurnell family, not a Gurney.[^dedham-tanner] The full resolution, with every source, is carried in the G13 companion topic on colonial collaterals; the entry here cross-references it rather than restating the household reconstruction.[^collaterals-crossref]

### Providence GARNET

A distinct **Thomas Garnet** family held property at Providence, Rhode Island — his probate inventory is referenced at two points in the town's printed probate volume, and his widow, Mary Garnet, survived him.[^providence-garnet] This is unconnected to any Gurney: the genuine Providence **John and Sarah Gurney**, documented in the town's own records between 1695 and 1714, sit in different record volumes than the ones carrying the Garnet family, and the two should not be conflated on surname resemblance alone.[^providence-garnet] The Gurney couple's own record is treated in full elsewhere and cross-referenced, not restated, here.[^providence-gurney-crossref]

## English name-forms

### Suffolk GARNEYS rendered "Gurneys"

Joseph James Muskett's *Suffolk Manorial Families* repeatedly misrenders the distinct Suffolk gentry family **Garneys**, of Mickfield, Boyland, and Kenton, as "**Gurneys**." Volume 1 carries the family under "Garneys of Hoxford" in its armorial notes.[^muskett-v1-garneys] Volume 2's prose names "Margaret, daughter of Edmond Poley Esq., of Badley, by **Mirabel, daughter of John Garneys, of Kenton**" — correctly, with an "a" — but the same woman appears twice in Muskett's own pedigree charts as "**Mirabel Gurneys**," with a "u." Muskett is therefore internally inconsistent about his own family's spelling within the same volume.[^muskett-v2-garneys] The working rule for Suffolk sources: a surname printed "**Gurneys**," with the plural -s, is presumptively this Garneys family until the record is checked, not a Gurney family; the Gurney line does not use a terminal -s.

## Search-mechanism false friends

These are not name-forms mistaken for Gurney so much as search techniques or record labels that trip up a Gurney (or Gurney-associate) search and are worth flagging on their own.

### Wymondham "John Braintree"

A Norfolk manorial court roll, 1659–71, records a copyhold admission "to the use and behoof of **John Braintree of Wymondham**" — a real Norfolk person surnamed Braintree, in the same south-central-Norfolk corridor the Gurney research already tracks.[^wymondham-braintree] He is not a Gurney and has no known connection to one; the reason to log him is narrower: a full-text search for New England's **Braintree**, Massachusetts (John Gurney-1's home town from about 1645) will legitimately surface this English surname match, and without this note a future sweep could waste time chasing it as a place-name lead. Logged so it is recognized and set aside on sight.[^wymondham-braintree]

### "Old" Norfolk County (Massachusetts jurisdiction)

Not a person at all, but a jurisdictional label trap. "Old" Norfolk County, Massachusetts (created 1643, effectively dissolved 1679) covered Salisbury, Haverhill, Hampton, and the towns north of the Merrimack — nothing to do with Weymouth or Braintree, which sat in **Suffolk** County for the whole of John Gurney-1's colonial life (1643 onward). Any colonial-era "Norfolk County" record hit for this family is therefore a false-friend trap: it either belongs to an unrelated Merrimack-valley household, or it is a modern cataloguer's back-projection. The Norfolk County that does contain Weymouth, Braintree, Quincy, and Randolph was created only in 1793 and is relevant solely to post-1793 series — nineteenth-century cemetery and vital-record transcriptions, and labels on sites such as Find a Grave. The full jurisdictional scope map lives on the Weymouth place record; cross-referenced, not restated, here.[^weymouth-jurisdiction-crossref]

## Search strategy: name-variant discipline for associate searches

The same false-friend discipline applies to the surnames of John Gurney's documented colonial associates, not only to Gurney itself. A narrow-to-wide search plan built for his Weymouth East Field and Mill Field neighbors illustrates the method: start with the exact recorded spelling, add conservative period variants, and only widen to a modernized reading once the record supports it — not before. Two examples carry the caution best. **Ludden** (John's fellow 1641 gunpowder-fine petitioner) should be searched first as Ludden, Luddin, or Luddon; broader forms such as Ludham, Luddam, or Luddum should not be assumed to be the same surname without independent record support. And **Wulinges**, the Mill Field neighbor Richard Rawlings, is normalized to "Rawlings" only in place notes — the Wulinges-to-Rawlings reading is a working hypothesis, not yet a closed search conclusion, and should not be treated as settled when planning a new sweep. The full nine-surname table (Ludden, Porter, Randoll, Addames, Richardes, Staple, Wulinges, Gilles, Brandones), with conservative and broader forms for each, is carried in the immigration-by-association topic; cross-referenced, not restated, here.[^search-strategy-crossref]
