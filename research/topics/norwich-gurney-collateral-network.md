# Norwich Gurney collateral network

The medieval and Tudor Norwich material contains several Gurnay/Gurney
appearances that are not yet tied securely to the West Barsham direct line.
They should be treated as a collateral-name network, not folded into the
pedigree by surname alone.

## Medieval deed-calendar cluster

Walter Rye's printed Norfolk deed calendar indexes a tenement of John Gurnay
at St George at Gates, Norwich, and Gurnay appearances with Elys and John
Stuteville in property or trustee contexts involving Somerton, Sloley, and the
central-Norfolk corridor. A separate 1463 Great Melton feoffment names William
Gurnay alongside Sir William Calthorpe and John Wyndham, extending the
Gurney-Calthorpe feoffee circle documented later in the direct-line material.
The exact deeds remain to be pulled and identified before any person-level
connection is asserted.[^rye-calendar]

## Norwich freemen, 1317–1603 — a complete Gurney census

The printed calendar of Norwich freedom admissions (L'Estrange/Rye, covering 1317–1603) yields exactly two Gurney admissions across 286 years: **Geoffrey Gurney, "le barbour" (barber), admitted 20 Richard II (1396/97)**, and **William Gurney, "barker" (tanner), admitted 9 Henry IV (1407/08)** — and **no Tudor Gurney freeman at all**.[^freemen-calendar] Two consequences: the West Barsham gentry line never took the Norwich freedom; and the sixteenth/early-seventeenth-century Norwich-city Gurneys this file tracks (the St Gregory's line, the 1608 deposition deponents, Stephen the 1613–19 decedent) operated outside the enfranchised trading class or under other spellings. Geoffrey the barber is contemporary with the G22/G23 era; a Norwich barber is unlikely to be a West Barsham cadet — more plausibly kin or a dependant of the city orbit around Edmund Gournay (G23), the city's fee'd counsel a generation earlier. Rye's own caveat applies: the calendar is L'Estrange's transcript, unchecked against the original roll, and "it by no means follows that what is here printed is all that is contained in the roll."

Entries and the editor's caveat verbatim at [`sources/corpus_supplement/norwich-freemen-calendar-1317-1603-gurney-entries.md`](https://github.com/allengurney/gurney-genealogy/blob/main/sources/corpus_supplement/norwich-freemen-calendar-1317-1603-gurney-entries.md). ## John Gurnell (once written "Gurney"), 1556 — classified toward Gurnell

Rye's deed index contains "Gurnell (orig. written Gurney) Jno., St. Jno. Sepulchre" with a 1556 reference. The classification question — Gurnell family or Gurney family? — now resolves toward **Gurnell**: the freemen calendar records **John Gurnell, cordwainer ("Cordwr., not app."), admitted 5 Edward VI (1551)**, and a 1551-enfranchised cordwainer John Gurnell fits a 1556 city-deed party exactly, while the same calendar shows no Tudor Gurney freeman.[^rye-calendar][^freemen-calendar] The scribe's "orig. written Gurney" remains evidence the name was read both ways; the underlying deed would still be needed for certainty.

## Named Gurney clergy of the diocese (1435, 1577–1618)

Two beneficed clergy of the Norwich diocese carry the surname a century apart, neither yet tied to the West Barsham line:

- **Thomas Gurnay, instituted to Rushmere, Suffolk, 1435, on Aslak presentation.** A Thomas Gurnay was presented in 1435 to **the living of Rushmere in Suffolk** by **Walter Aslack, Esq., and Margery his wife** — Daniel Gurney's Supplement states the parish outright ("Walter Aslack and Margery his wife presented Thomas Gurnay to the living of Rushmere in Suffolk, in 1435. Who this Thomas Gurnay was does not appear"), and the Suffolk Institute institutions list carries the same presentation.[^gurnay-1435-institution] This is the same Walter Aslak whose 1426 quarrel with the Pastons is a set-piece of the Paston correspondence, so the cleric sits squarely in the Paston-circle patronage world at the very moment G20 Thomas Gournay II and his father were active. Because his benefice was Rushmere — not Great Ellingham — he is **not** the Thomas Gurnay of Great Ellingham who died intestate in 1454 (below), and he remains a live alternative candidate for the 1462/63 "clerk convict" discussed below. He does not appear in the Clergy of the Church of England database, whose coverage begins only at 1540; confirming his later career needs the pre-Reformation Norwich episcopal registers (Bishops Alnwick/Brouns, institutions 1426–45).[^cced-negative]
- **George Gurnay, rector of Tacolneston, 1577 – c. 1618.** George Gurnay held the rectory of Tacolneston (Depwade hundred, about four miles from Hempnall) from 1577 on the presentation of Sir Edward Clere; in the 1603 diocesan return he "returned answer, that here were 120 communicants"; his successor was presented in 1618.[^george-tacolneston] George is a rare forename in the family, and Tacolneston's nearness to Hempnall makes him a plausible kinsman of the Hempnall Gurney family (carried as lead L-152) rather than of the West Barsham gentry line.

## The armigerous cadet at Cawston and Booton (15th–16th c.)

A gentry Gurney bearing the West Barsham arms differenced with a crescent (second-son cadency) was buried at Cawston in 1578 (William Gurney, gent., d. 10 March 1578, wife Anne Wayte), and a "William Gurnay of Boton [Booton]" appears in a Cawston property grant of 1481 (Ancient Deed B.991). Together they argue for a recognized Gurney cadet branch seated at Cawston/Booton across the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries — a distinct collateral reservoir from the Norwich-city names above. Full treatment, with the monument text and heraldry, is on [`research/people/gurney-family-cawston-manorial.md`](../people/gurney-family-cawston-manorial.md).

## Thomas Gurney "clerk convict," Christmastide 1462 / January 1463 — a murder by command; identity open

In plain terms: around Christmas 1462 a Thomas Gurney was convicted in Norfolk of ordering his servant to kill a cousin of the Bishop of Norwich (Walter Lyhert, bishop 1445–72). The servant was hanged; Gurney himself escaped the gallows by claiming **benefit of clergy** — the medieval rule that a man who could prove clerical status (in practice, by the 1460s, any literate man who could read the "neck verse") was handed over to the bishop's court instead of the royal gallows. "Clerk convict" is the technical label for a convicted felon so transferred. Which Thomas Gurney he was remains unresolved.

The episode is preserved in two Paston letters. **Margaret Paston to John Paston, 19 January 1463** (Gairdner's marginal date; his footnote ties the letter to the election for the Parliament that met 29 April 1463, "quite certain that it was written during Edward's reign"):

> "Skypwith shall telle you suche tydyngs as bethe in this contre, and of **Thomas Gornay and of his man; hym self is clerk convicte, and hys man is hangyn; ye shall here her after what they and oder wer purposyd to a do to her master.**"

The tail sentence — "what they *and other* were purposed to have done to their master" — carries a wider-conspiracy flavour. Immediately following in Gairdner (No. 537) is an abstract of **Thomas Playter to John Paston, January 1463**, quoted by Fenn "from a letter now lost":

> "Please your maistership wete, that as for **my Lord of Norwich cosyns deth, Thomas Gurneys man hath confessed that he slewe hym by commaundment of his maister**, and confessed over that the same dager he slewe hym with, **he kest in a sege [a privy] whiche is founden and taken up al to-bowyd [bent double], for he cowde not breke it, and in prison is bothe he and his maister.** Also on Thursday next after Cristemasse was a man slayn, by whom no man woot…"

So the servant confessed to killing the bishop's cousin **by his master's command**; the murder weapon was recovered, bent double, from a privy; master and man were both imprisoned before the man hanged and Gurney pleaded clergy. The killing plausibly fell in late 1462, with the trials done by 19 January 1463.[^clerk-convict]

**The clergy plea does not imply he was actually a cleric.** By the 1460s benefit of clergy was available to any literate layman; an esquire could plead it. The earlier inference on this file that the plea "points toward a cleric" over-weighted the term. It also explains the record trail: a clerk convict was delivered to the ordinary's (bishop's) prison for purgation, not pardoned by the crown — which is why the printed *Calendar of Patent Rolls* for 1461–67 contains **no Thomas Gournay/Gurnay pardon of any kind** (complete index and full-text sweep). The trail, if it survives, runs through King's Bench indictment files (KB 9, Norfolk, 2–3 Edward IV — the indictment would name the victim and Gurney's style and residence, likely settling the identity), the KB 27 rex side, and Bishop Lyhert's own act books (purgation of clerks convict).[^cpr-clerk-convict-negative]

If the clerk convict is G20, it is a dramatic addition to [his companion](../people/g20-thomas-gournay-ii-fact-sheet.research.md); until the indictment is found, the identity stays open.

## Unplaced fifteenth-century Gurneys (Paston circle and Westminster)

The Gairdner Paston Letters — with one Westminster deed added — preserve several further Gurney/Gornay appearances in and around the Fastolf–Paston circle that are not securely tied to the West Barsham line and are carried here as unplaced collateral names:

- **A Gurney tenant-farmer of Orwellbury (Horwellbury), near Royston, Hertfordshire, c. 1444–1453**, chronically in arrears. Agnes Paston urged her son to write to "the seide Gurnay" over unpaid rent — "he owyth be my reknyng at Myhelmesse last passed, beside your faddes dette, xviij li. xiiij s. viij d." — and, "owing to Gurney's utter inability to pay his rent," to find another tenant. A Hertfordshire/London-orbit Gurney of the 1440s–50s, leasing from Justice William Paston.[^paston-minor]
- **Master John Gurnay, "the King's squire," of Westminster (fl. 1455–63) — a Lancastrian household man stripped after 1461.** Ancient Deed A.1554 records a "Grant by **Master John Gurnay, the King's squire, of Westminster**, to William Dyxson, of the same, of a chace next the Lamb Brewhouse, in King Street, Westminster, with free entry for man, horse, and cart … through **Gurnay's gate** to the ground of the said William, for 30 years … 20 February, 36 Henry VI [1457/8]." Three Patent Roll entries of 1462–63 now give this royal-household Gurnay a full profile and a Yorkist-confiscation arc:[^westminster-squire][^cpr-john-gourney]
  1. *CPR 1461–67 p. 188 (2 Edw IV, 1462):* grant for life to Master James Fryse of "a messuage called 'le grete belle' by le Barbican in the parish of St. Botolph in Aldrisshegatestrete in the suburbs of London with 14 other messuages annexed, **late of John Gourney, esquire, and Nicholas Browne of the grant of Henry VI**."
  2. *p. 273 (20 June 1463):* recital that the king had committed to Richard Felde "the custody of the lodges between the round tower within the palace [of Westminster] and the little conduit **which John Gurney lately held** … of the grant of Henry VI."
  3. *p. 297 (5 July 1463):* grant to Robert Grey, king's minstrel, of "a tenement or brewhouse called '**le Lambe**' in Distaflane [Distaff Lane], London … **which John Blakeney, esquire, and John Gourney lately had of the grant of Henry VI** by letters patent, having come into his hands by virtue of an act of resumption in Parliament at Westminster, 9 July, 33 Henry VI [1455]."
  The four records cohere into one figure: a Henry VI household esquire holding Westminster-palace lodge custodies, a fifteen-messuage Barbican/Aldersgate block, and a Lamb brewhouse (with John Blakeney, esq. — a known East Anglian/Fastolf-orbit name, which strengthens without proving an East Anglian origin), all lost by the 1455 act of resumption and Yorkist regrants of 1462–63. The A.1554 "Lamb Brewhouse, King Street, Westminster" versus the CPR's "le Lambe, Distaff Lane, London" are probably two distinct Lamb-named brewhouses, or a calendaring imprecision — flagged, not merged. Daniel Gurney's *Record* knows no such John. **A new hypothesis makes him potentially direct-line kin: Blomefield's summary of G20 Thomas Gournay II's 1471 will names a son John** (Depeden grant confirmation, will made 1469/70) — so the earlier premise here that "no adult direct-line John existed in 1458" no longer holds if Blomefield is right. A Norfolk esquire's son in Henry VI's household, stripped by the new regime in 1462–63, would fit the CPR arc; the tests are whether "Johanni filio meo" appears in the will's ultima voluntas at image level, and the original Henry VI grants (CPR Henry VI volumes, 1446–61), which would name his office and origin.<!-- L-236 --> The older note that he may be kin to the Orwellbury tenant (both Westminster/London-orbit Gurneys of the 1440s–50s) remains an untested hunch.
- **A young friar Gurnay, curate of Worstead**, described in a Latin letter to John Paston as "Gurnay suus socius et procurator, frater juvenis nostri conventus et unus sacerdos simplex, curatus de Worsted" — a minor Norfolk cleric.[^paston-minor]
- **"Cousin Gornay," a court-savvy intermediary**, c. 1475–1500, advising an approach to the Duchess of Norfolk over Oxnead through "my Lady Calthorpe, my moder in lawe" — a Gurney kinsman moving in the Mowbray/Howard and Calthorpe orbit.[^paston-minor]
- **"Writings… of Gorney"** among the Fastolf/Hellesdon muniments, 1450: a correspondent thanks the parson of Hellesdon for "the three writings of Wiltshire's will and Gorney" — deeds relating to Gurney lands or interests held within the Fastolf archive, consistent with Fastolf's absorption of ex-Gurney holdings in the Saxthorpe/Swathings orbit.[^paston-minor]

Two further Paston references are *not* collateral — they concern **G20 Thomas Gournay II and his wife Margaret Jerningham** directly, and are written up on [his companion](../people/g20-thomas-gournay-ii-fact-sheet.research.md): the Countess of Oxford's letter about **Margaret Gurnay**, and John Jerningham's Corpus Christi 1458 letter to Margaret Paston naming "myn ownkll Gournay… and myn awnte his wyfe" (the primary text behind Daniel Gurney's Gournay–Jerningham marriage proof).
