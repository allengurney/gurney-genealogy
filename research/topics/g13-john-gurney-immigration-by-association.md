# John Gurney (G13) immigration by association

## Finding

This angle is worth pursuing, but the first-pass signal is not "John plainly arrived with a known group." It is more precise than that: John's earliest Massachusetts orbit is a Weymouth land-and-military-readiness network, with a later Braintree/Mendon layer. The strongest immediate research value is to separate three kinds of associates:

1. people named with John in the June 1641 gunpowder-fines matter;
2. people whose lands define or later absorb John's early Weymouth grants;
3. later Braintree, Billerica, and Mendon associates who show community standing but are less likely to reveal his original emigration party.

The Porter ambiguity has been the highest-priority open question, and an image read now tips it toward **Richard Porter of Weymouth**. The printed Massachusetts Bay *court order* (MBCR 1:331, 2 June 1641) names **John Porter**, and the Porter genealogy simply repeats it. But the underlying **petition** — calendared in the Massachusetts State Archives collection (FamilySearch film 007702977, image 947) and read here at full resolution — is dated **30 May 1641** and names **"John Gurny, Richard Porter, and James Ludden of Weymouth,"** seeking remission of the gunpowder fines. The calendar is a careful fair-copy index, the forename is plainly **Richard**, and the group tag "of Weymouth" fits Richard Porter (the documented Rev. Hull–company Weymouth settler) far better than John Porter (a Hingham/Salem line). The weight therefore shifts to **Richard Porter**, with the printed court order's "John" the likelier slip — and, if so, John Gurney's cleanest 1641 associate is a Weymouth/Hull-company comparator, strengthening the early-Weymouth-settler reading below. The remaining clincher is the petition document itself (referenced by the calendar's page number into the Archives volume); the calendar, though careful, is an index, not the petition.[^mbcr-porter][^military-calendar]

## Current evidence

The first colonial anchor remains the June 1641 General Court record: John Gurney, James Ludden, and John Porter had fines remitted for want of gunpowder. The court line itself does not supply residence, age, origin, ship, or family structure. The Weymouth placement comes from supporting local-history context, especially the Porter genealogy's gloss that Ludden and Gurney were of Weymouth.[^mbcr-porter] The two men John is grouped with are both established Weymouth settlers, not fellow newcomers: **James Ludden** was an "Old Planter," at Wessagusset/Weymouth by 1632 (Winthrop's guide across Luddam's Ford), and the Porter of the underlying petition is **Richard Porter**, a 1635 Rev. Hull–company settler. John's later Weymouth ties run the same way — his 1663 estate owed "Goodman King of Waymouth," who was **John King**, an early King's-Cove seaman and planter. The 1641 fine group, and these continuing debts, therefore read as the reception of a newcomer *into* an established Weymouth community rather than as evidence of a shared emigration. A separate 1644 colony-accounts entry adds a paper tie to **Rev. Thomas Jenner**, Weymouth's minister 1636–40: the General Court accepted "John Gurny his byl for 3. 16. 4d" with Thomas Lake's note of £1 3s 8d as the £5 due "fro Mr Ginner" (Jenner) — a credit relationship of unstated origin, not the "16 March 1641/2" transaction an earlier misreading of the figures had produced.[^ludden-king-jenner]

The stronger Weymouth pattern comes from the early land memory. Nash preserves three separate entries in which land later listed under other men was "first granted" or "first given" to John Gurny/Gurnie: two East Field parcels and one Mill Field parcel. The later/current landholders and abutters create the best starting map for John's immediate colonial neighborhood, but they should not be treated as personal friends without additional evidence.[^nash-land]

The Gurney-Ludden connection has a longer tail. Benjamin Ludden's 1690 Weymouth inventory names a pasture/wood lot adjoining John Gurney land, probably the later Weymouth John or a family parcel still carrying the Gurney name. This does not prove James Ludden and John Gurney immigrated together, but it makes Ludden the best first-ring family to investigate.[^ludden-1690]

The later Braintree/Billerica/Mendon records show John as trusted in another household's probate and as a participant in settlement/speculation networks, but these are probably not first-arrival evidence. They should be used after the Weymouth-first ring is exhausted.[^baxter][^ballou]

## The 1640s gap, Weymouth land timing, and the Rehoboth offshoot

John's documented Massachusetts life is anchored at **Braintree**, not Weymouth: the *History of Weymouth* places him "in Braintree before 1645," he owned and sold Braintree land (sold 12 February 1661), his wife died at Braintree (20 September 1661), he remarried there (12 November 1661), and he died at Braintree in 1662/3. The same town history concludes that "whether he ever resided in Weymouth is a matter of doubt."[^how-gurney]

Read precisely, his Weymouth land is an early holding he did not keep — not a 1651-52 paper entitlement. Nash's Appendix C is the **1643 partial record of Weymouth property owners** ("Property Owners and Description of Property in 1643… First Division, 1636; Second Division, 1651"); the text notes "in 1643 a partial record of the then property owners was made which has been preserved." Within it, a two-acre East Field parcel (p. 258) and a four-acre Mill Field parcel (p. 278) are recorded as "first granted to John Gurny/Gurnie" but **already held by other men** (Robert Randoll; the lot under Thomas Richards) by 1643 — so John was an *original* grantee whose parcels had passed to others by the 1643 list, his original grants made as a new Weymouth inhabitant by about 1642. A separate, later grant — great lot no. 16 in the Second Division — is dated **2 February 1651-52** (p. 282) and was likewise "subsequently granted to others." This *sharpens* rather than removes the place files' looser "by 1642-44": the firm anchor is Nash's **1643** record.

His **arrival** is tightly bounded by these data: he is at Weymouth by the 30 May / 2 June 1641 gunpowder matter and an original grantee in the pre-1643 division, so he reached New England in **1638–early 1641** — and cannot be later. That window sits in the tail of the Great Migration, which fell off sharply after 1640. The grant-book entries 258/270/278/282 (now staged for image work) would date John's original grants directly and place them against named neighbours.[^nash-land][^how-gurney][^nash-1643]

So John held real Weymouth land in his first years (c. 1641-43), an early-settler profile — yet had **moved to Braintree** by 1645. Because his Weymouth parcels were already divested by the 1643 record, the move itself falls **c. 1642–1645** (after his 1641 grant, bounded by the "in Braintree before 1645" datum). This sharpens the reception question:

- **Early Weymouth settler, then Braintree (supported).** John took up East/Mill Field land in his arrival years — all three loci abut the **Hingham line** in East/North Weymouth — then moved to Braintree (c. 1642–1645) as his parcels passed to others; the 1651-52 great-lot was a later grant he never used.
- **Following the Weymouth congregation is weak.** When the Weymouth church partly dispersed under **Rev. Samuel Newman to found Rehoboth in 1643-44**, John did not go; he moved the opposite direction, to Braintree. A "came to align with the Weymouth congregation" reading is therefore not supported by where he actually went.[^how-newman]

**The Braintree move was into a forming plantation, not an established town.** Braintree was incorporated only in May 1640 — set off from Boston's Mount Wollaston grant of 1634 — and was actively drawing settlers through the early-to-mid 1640s, when the colony's first iron works went up there (1644-45). Charles Francis Adams notes that even after 1637 the Mount Wollaston allotments were "large allotments, indicative of a non-resident ownership," and that William Tyng, the Boston merchant, bought the greater part of the Mount Wollaston land in 1639. John's arrival c. 1642–1645 was therefore a *join into a new, still-forming town*, most plausibly drawn by land availability and his own network rather than any forced removal. Braintree took its name from **Braintree, Essex**, and carried a pronounced Essex-origin settler character — which dovetails with John's own Essex ties (his Tyng-estate landlord of Stanford Rivers, Essex, confirmed baptised there 1602/3 and died 1653; his son-in-law Daniel Shed of Finchingfield, Essex, in Braintree records by 1643). The early Braintree influx was multi-source (Boston, Weymouth, Hingham, and direct-from-England), so John was one of many forming the town rather than part of a single Weymouth contingent. Adams's *History of Braintree* is North-Precinct/Quincy-scoped and names no Gurney — John's own freehold lay in the South Precinct on the Monatiquot — so his Braintree neighbours are better recovered from the Braintree town records, Sprague's *Genealogies of the Families of Braintree*, and the Suffolk Deeds abuttals than from that narrative.[^braintree-founding][^adams-braintree-genesis]

Profiling the neighbours and the later great-lot cohort answers part of this. John's East/Mill Field abutters were not a single arriving party but a mix of the two documented Weymouth streams — an Aylesbury-Vale Buckinghamshire group (Randall of Wendover, Brandon of Ashton Clinton) threaded with a West-Country/Dorchester group (Richards and Hart via the *Mary and John*; Norton via the Hull company) — set out in the cluster analysis below. And the men listed with John in the 2 February 1651/2 great-lot allotment did not move *with* him: the documented removals among them run to Rehoboth (Samuel Newman, John Allin) and Boston (Thomas Rider), with William Brandon already dead by 1647 — none to Braintree. John's move to Braintree was therefore an individual one, drawn by land and his own network, not part of a Weymouth-to-Braintree contingent. The 1651/2 list itself was a deferred laying-out of "formerly granted" rights that demonstrably included absentees and deceased men, so John's appearance on it reflects a surviving proprietary entitlement, not residence — exactly the non-resident-proprietor position Charles Francis Adams describes for early Massachusetts towns, where such men "were frequently of controlling influence ... yet, not having their residence with the actual settlers, were not entitled to certain exclusive privileges granted to inhabitants." Weymouth's own records fit this: the town "was not settled as a plantation by a definite body of proprietors," its property records are undated (the conventional "1643" is an inference), and a town order of 6 April 1646 forbade any inhabitant to "lett or sell any house, or land, to any person ... that is not an inhabitant amongst us" without first tendering it to the town — the climate a departed proprietor like John faced when his Weymouth right outlasted his residence.<!-- L-182, L-188: neighbour streams + 1651/2 cohort dispersal profiled 2026-06 -->[^adams-braintree-genesis]

## The Weymouth land system and John's grant cohort

Weymouth ran several distinct land systems, and reading John's parcels against them is what dates and contextualizes them. The scattered open-field system — small one-to-eight-acre strips in named planting fields — was active before 1636: the East Field was already being granted by 1635 (Zachary Bicknell's East Field parcel was sold after his death by 9 March 1636/7). John's known package — two acres in the East Field, two more there, and four acres in the Mill Field — is ordinary settler accommodation in that system, and his parcels sit among roughly 176 "first granted" chain-of-title clauses spread over about 138 holders, with close analogues in the same record (Walter Harris's eight Mill Field acres, John Harding's six, John Barnard's eight, Matthew Pratt's composite twenty). They are normal, not exceptional.

A separate, household-scaled allocation — the 1636 Fresh Pond "great lots," six acres per adult and three per child under twelve — went to sixteen named men: Edward Bennett, Joseph Hull, Henry Kingman, Thomas Jenner Sr., Thomas White, William Fry, Robert Lovell, Edmund Hart, Thomas Rawling, Thomas Jenner Jr., William Read, Richard Sylvester, Richard Adams, William Smith, Stephen French, and John Upham. **John Gurney is absent from it** — evidence against his being at Weymouth as early as 1636 — though two of his later East Field abutters, Edmund Hart and Thomas Rawling, were already in that 1636 cohort.

John's other Weymouth land, great lot no. 16 of 2–3 February 1651/2, belongs to yet another exercise: the laying-out of rights "named in the old town Book and formerly granted." That list demonstrably carried absentees and the dead — Samuel Newman (no. 6) had gone to Rehoboth in 1643/44, John Allin (no. 17) to Rehoboth in 1643, Thomas Rider (no. 24) to Boston, James Britton to Woburn (and was executed in 1643), and William Brandon (no. 20) was dead by November 1647 — so John's place on it reflects a surviving proprietary entitlement, not residence, and none of these cohort-mates moved to Braintree with him. The closest parallel to John's whole pattern is **John Read**, who likewise held Weymouth land, took a Tyng Mount Wollaston farm under a ten-year agreement from 14 April 1639, received a 44-acre Braintree grant in 1640, removed to Rehoboth, and still drew an 18-acre Fresh Pond great lot in 1651/2 — a Weymouth right carried alongside a Tyng tenancy and a move elsewhere (Read had married into a Suffolk family, the Lessies of Blyborough). The later 1663 "First/Second Division" lots are different again — laid out 14 December 1663 westward toward the Braintree line — and should not be back-projected onto John's 1640s strips.[^weymouth-land-cohort]

## The Buckinghamshire (Aylesbury-Vale) cluster around John's grants

Profiling those very neighbours answers the "with whom" question concretely, and surfaces a pattern the place files never recorded: **John Gurney's East Field and Mill Field parcels sat inside a coherent Aylesbury-Vale / Chiltern Buckinghamshire settler cluster, threaded with a parallel West-Country/Dorchester stream — and the two had intermarried by the early 1640s.** The Buckinghamshire core is documented under oath: in a 1688 deposition (Massachusetts State Archives 129:16) **Jonas Humphrey and Robert Randall, both of Weymouth, swore they had lived in Wendover, Buckinghamshire** and known **Enoch Hunt of the adjacent parish of Lee**, who with his son Ephraim removed to Weymouth.[^bucks-cluster]

| Nicholas Norton (bounds Mill Field E, 278) | Somerset-adjacent (Hull cattle) | West Country / Hull | by 1637 |
| Jeffery / John Staples (East Field) | — | early Weymouth | 1639+ |

Two further facts lock the cluster together. **Ephraim Hunt** (of the Lee/Wendover Bucks group) married **Anna Richards, daughter of Thomas Richards** — the very landholder whose ground contained John's Mill Field parcel — and named Richards his attorney for Hunt lands at **Beaconsfield and Amersham, Buckinghamshire**. And the cluster largely arrived **via Dorchester** (Humphrey to Dorchester 1637; Hart and Richards in the Mary and John, 1630), then crossed to Weymouth — a Dorchester→Weymouth pathway. External corroboration widens the Bucks contingent: Thomas Pratt (Aston Clinton), Edward Sale (Chesham), Christopher Gibson (Wendover), all Bucks → Dorchester/Weymouth.[^bucks-cluster][^bucks-external]

**Bearing on residence (the user's premise).** Early-Massachusetts towns divided land among admitted inhabitants, so John's place in the 1642–44 East/Mill Field division is itself evidence that he was a **Weymouth inhabitant in the early 1640s** — embedded in this cluster — not a non-resident grantee. The town history's "whether he ever resided in Weymouth is a matter of doubt" understates what the grants imply. By the 1643 property record his parcels already stand under Randall, Richards, and Adams, so he had begun divesting as he prepared to remove to Braintree (there by 1645). The Randall/Staples line then carried into **Mendon** (1663) — where John's own son John settled.

**Bearing on origin (a genuine, caveated thread — not a conclusion).** The Aylesbury-Vale parishes that supplied John's Weymouth neighbours — Wendover, Lee, Ashton Clinton, Chesham, Amersham, Beaconsfield — are the **same Buckinghamshire parishes that independently held dense Gurney households** (the case file's §8.3 Bucks cluster: Aylesbury, Stewkley, Chesham, Amersham, Ivinghoe, Edlesborough, and more). The case file eliminated each *individual* Bucks Gurney as the emigrant on continuing-English-residence grounds, but it never weighed the fact that the colonial John's Weymouth *neighbours* were Aylesbury-Vale emigrants. That coincidence is real and worth a targeted test; it does **not** overturn the favoured Candidate B (East Dereham, Norfolk, ~65%), which rests on the East Dereham baptism, occupation, and finances, but it is a concrete competing signal. (It is *not* connected to the medieval Gournay manor of Wendover, an unrelated 12th-century holding — see G34 — which must not be conflated.) The test is specific: search the Wendover/Lee/Ashton-Clinton/Chesham/Amersham registers and the Humphrey–Hunt–Randall–Brandon associate network for a Gurney emigrant or kin link, and re-read the Bucks Gurney households as a possible *kin source* for a man who travelled with the Wendover cohort rather than as the emigrant himself.

**The counterweight — associations are regionally mixed.** John's *Braintree* life points to a different English region entirely. He leased forty-eight acres within the estate of **William Tyng of Stanford Rivers, Essex** (baptised there 16 January 1602/3, died 1653); his son-in-law **Daniel Shed came from Finchingfield, Essex** (in Braintree by 1643, married Mary Gurney 1647); and Braintree, Massachusetts took its name from Braintree, Essex. The closest structural parallel to John's own position is **John Read**, who likewise held early Weymouth land, took a Tyng farm at Mount Wollaston under a ten-year agreement (from 14 April 1639), received a Braintree grant, later removed to Rehoboth, and still drew an 18-acre Weymouth great lot in 1651/2 — the same pattern of a Weymouth proprietary right carried alongside a Tyng tenancy and a move elsewhere; Read had married into a Suffolk family (Lessie of Blyborough). John's *paired* Tyng co-tenant, Gregory Belcher, was from Warwickshire (Aston). By his last years John held three Braintree-area interests at once — the small five-acre Monatiquot freehold he sold in 1661/2, the larger leased Tyng farm, and a residual Weymouth proprietary right — an ordinary mixed tenure for a tradesman without the capital to buy a working farm, not an anomaly. So John's documented colonial networks span several English regions — **Buckinghamshire** (his Weymouth land-neighbours), **Essex** and **Warwickshire** and **Suffolk** (his Braintree estate, son-in-law, and Tyng-tenant peers), and **Norfolk** (the favoured Candidate-B origin) — with no single stream claiming him. That mixing is itself a lesson: John attached to whichever local network held land or kin, so association is a *weak* lever for most of these regions. It is weak specifically for the Buckinghamshire neighbour-cluster as a *kin source*: the Bucks Gurney households cluster in the Aylesbury–Stewkley–Chesham–Edlesborough arc, with **no Gurney recorded in Wendover, Lee, or Ashton Clinton** — the very parishes that supplied the Humphrey–Hunt–Randall–Brandon neighbours — so there is no Gurney kin behind that cluster. The Bucks neighbour-cluster is therefore best treated as a **reception context to test**, not an origin claim, and the direct evidence (the East Dereham baptism) remains the stronger basis for Candidate B.[^essex-braintree][^tyng-read-belcher]

**The Hingham bridge — a second reception model, and the one that fits Candidate B.** The neighbour cluster shows early Weymouth was not single-region: it drew at least a West-Country/Hull stream and an Aylesbury-Vale Buckinghamshire stream. A man of Norfolk origin would be an outsider to *both* — unless he entered through the colony next door. **Hingham, Massachusetts** — under ten miles from Weymouth, and directly abutting John's East/Mill Field parcels on its town line — was settled overwhelmingly by the **Norfolk Hingham / Diligent-of-Ipswich (1638) cohort**: Gilman, Peck, Cushing, Folsom, Stephen Paine of Great Ellingham, Sutton of Attleborough, the Lincolns of Wymondham — out of the very parishes that frame the Henry/Francis Gurney (G15/G14) Norfolk world (Hingham, Great Ellingham, Attleborough, Wymondham, Deopham, Dereham), and the same corridor as the Ann Gurney × John Gilman marriage at Hingham, Norfolk. A Norfolk-corridor arrival that landed at Hingham, Massachusetts and stepped the short distance to Weymouth is therefore a clean reception model **for Candidate B specifically**, requiring no membership in either Weymouth regional stream. The move is geographically trivial — John's lots literally "but on Hingham line" — and the genuinely interesting open question is the empirical one: **how regionally concentrated were Weymouth's landholders, and how many were outsiders to the West-Country/Bucks streams?** Weymouth was not monolithic but it was firmly West-Country-led — Rev. Joseph Hull's 1635 company out of Somerset and Dorset — with the Aylesbury-Vale Buckinghamshire stream second; no Norfolk stream fed it, so a Norfolk John would indeed have been an outsider to Weymouth's own settlers. Adjacent Hingham is the opposite case, and the correlation is strong and quantified: Daniel Cushing's contemporary list records that "the whole number that came out of Norfolk (chiefly from Hingham, and its vicinity) from 1633 to 1639, and settled in this Hingham, was two hundred and six," the 1638 *Diligent* alone bringing 133. That cohort came out of the very parishes in which the direct-line Gurneys are documented — Hingham, Wymondham, Attleborough, Great Ellingham, Deopham — with named founders Stephen Payne of Great Ellingham, John Sutton of Attleborough, the Lincolns of Wymondham, and the Gilman, Peck, and Cushing families of Hingham, the same orbit as the Ann Gurney × John Gilman marriage at Hingham, Norfolk. A dense community from John's own family's Norfolk country thus sat under ten miles away, directly across the town line his Weymouth lots "but on." A Norfolk arrival received through that adjacent Norfolk colony is therefore not a strained hypothesis but the reception model that best fits Candidate B — connecting John's landing directly to the documented Norfolk-Gurney–to–New-England corridor.[^hingham-corridor][^hingham-cushing-list]

## Prioritized next work

1. **Resolve the Porter identity.** Locate the underlying petition or best calendar image for the 1641 gunpowder-fines matter. If it is Richard Porter of Weymouth, Porter becomes a major origin comparator; if it is John Porter of Hingham/Salem, he becomes a weaker jurisdictional or militia-administration comparator.
2. **Build a Ludden mini-profile.** Verify whether James Ludden was an "old planter," whether any primary or near-primary record places him before 1635, and whether his claimed English origin has source support. If he predates the 1635 Hull/Weymouth wave, he may explain John's local grounding without explaining John's transatlantic party.
3. **Profile the East Field cluster.** Randoll/Randall, Nathaniel Adams, Edmond Hart, Robert Cooke, and Jeffery Staple should be checked as a group, not one by one in isolation.
4. **Profile the Mill Field cluster.** Thomas Richards, Nicholas Norton, Richard Rawlings/Wulinges, John Giles, and William Brandon should be checked as a Hingham-boundary group.
5. **Only then broaden to Braintree/Mendon.** Gregory Baxter, Moses Payne, Richard Brackett, Edmund Quincy, Goodman King, and the 1662 Mendon list are valuable for John's adult colonial network, but less likely to answer how he first arrived.

## Working interpretation

Current confidence is **moderate (~60%)** that John's first colonial grounding was through a Weymouth neighborhood network rather than random settlement-hopping after arrival. The evidence is not yet strong enough to say he emigrated with those neighbors. A family with older children by the 1640s could have joined an existing Weymouth community through land availability, kin, occupation, or militia obligation, and still appear quickly in the town's land records.

The immediate test is whether Ludden and the correct Porter are documented as:

- early Wessagusset/Weymouth old planters;
- 1635 Hull-company or Weymouth-stream immigrants;
- Hingham/Braintree-adjacent settlers who moved through local opportunity rather than shared emigration;
- or late 1638-1641 arrivals.

If the first-ring men mostly prove to be older established Weymouth proprietors, the association angle shifts from "shared voyage" to "John's reception into an established Weymouth community." If one or more prove to be late arrivals with families and matching English geography, the shared-migration hypothesis becomes materially stronger.
