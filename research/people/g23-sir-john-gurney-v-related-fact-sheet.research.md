# Sir John Gurney V (d. 1408) — Research Companion

Research companion for `g23-sir-john-gurney-v-related-fact-sheet.md`. See `.claude/rules/research-files.md` for the paired-file rule.

Sir John Gurney V is a **collateral**, not a direct ancestor: the eldest son of Edmund Gournay (G23) and elder brother of the direct ancestor Robert Gournay (G22). The line did *not* pass through him — his only son died under age — so the inheritance moved sideways to Robert's son Thomas I (G21). His material also appears, in its bearing on the direct line, on the [G23](https://github.com/allengurney/gurney-genealogy/blob/main/research/people/g23-edmund-gurney-fact-sheet.research.md) and [G22](https://github.com/allengurney/gurney-genealogy/blob/main/research/people/g22-robert-gournay-fact-sheet.research.md) companions; this file is his own consolidated treatment. The full text of his History of Parliament biography is held at [`sources/corpus_supplement/John-Gurney-d1408-The-History-of-Parliamentx.md`](https://github.com/allengurney/gurney-genealogy/blob/main/sources/corpus_supplement/John-Gurney-d1408-The-History-of-Parliamentx.md).

## Career and the Arundel-to-Lancaster pivot

John's legal career was under way by 1382, and within four years he had been retained as steward of the Norfolk estates of Richard Fitzalan, earl of Arundel — the magnate from whom the Gurneys held their manor at Harpley; the connexion probably lasted until 1402.[^hop] The Arundel tie drew him into high politics: he gave the earl staunch support during the Lords Appellant's seizure of government in 1387–88. When Richard II took his revenge and Arundel was executed in 1397, John was reinstated on the Norfolk bench in July 1397 but, judging the danger, purchased a royal pardon in spring 1398 to cover his earlier adherence to the Appellants. He then aligned with the Lancastrians: in October 1398 he became a trustee of the estates of Sir Thomas Erpingham, who was leaving to join the exiled Henry of Bolingbroke. When Bolingbroke took the throne as Henry IV in 1399, Norfolk returned John to the Parliament that acclaimed the new king, and he was appointed sheriff of Norfolk and Suffolk (3 November 1399 – 24 November 1400) while that Parliament still sat. He sat again for the Coventry Parliament of October 1404, served as escheator, and was named to a long series of royal commissions of inquiry (murder 1387, theft, maintenance, piracy, the 1406 insurrection at Bishop's Lynn, and more).[^hop]

The 1392 murder of **Edmund Clippesby** — the lawyer who had served with John's father Edmund as joint steward of the duchy of Lancaster estates — bears on John: he was himself threatened with death if he tried to prosecute the killers, who were servants of Bishop Despenser of Norwich (no friend of John of Gaunt).[^hop]

John's own military/affinity footprint includes letters of attorney for service in Gascony/Aquitaine on 20 October 1394, in the retinue of John of Gaunt, then duke of Lancaster and Aquitaine (The National Archives C61/104, m. 7) — a direct continuation of his father's Lancastrian client relationship.[^msd]

## The Heylesdon marriage and inheritance

John married **Alice Heylesdon**, daughter and eventual sole heir of **John Heylesdon**, a wealthy London mercer and former alderman. The foundation document is John Heylesdon's will, dated London 14 April 1384 and proved at the Husting on 20 July 1384: it names his daughters Alice and Margaret, leaves Alice his tenements in Norwich and — "under certain conditions" — his Westcheap warehouse called the "Crowned Seld" (*la Selde coronata*), and endows two perpetual chantries at Hellesdon church.[^husting] Margaret died before her majority, so Alice inherited the whole: the manors of Hellesdon and Drayton, the two chantry advowsons, the Norwich houses, and the Crowned Seld. In 1392 John and Alice collected the £300 from the London authorities that had been allotted to her late sister.[^hop] The settlement is the larger reality behind the single "La Selde Coronata" warehouse that the older family tradition highlighted.[^hop]

John and Alice did not keep the core Heylesdon manors long: in 1395–96 they conveyed **Hellesdon and Drayton, with the advowsons**, to John Winter and partners — recorded in Walter Rye's feet-of-fines calendar (Part II, entry 262) and reported from three parishes (Heigham, Drayton, Taverham) by the eighteenth-century antiquary Mostyn John Armstrong.[^rye-heylesdon][^armstrong] The three-fold Armstrong cross-attestation, naming "John Gournay and Alice his wife," is decisive evidence that Sir John V's wife was **Alice Heylesdon** — settling the conflict with the Wansey pedigree's "Alice Bavard" (see the G23 companion).

## Death, the inquisition, and the failed succession

John was reappointed sheriff on 15 November 1408 and died on **4 December 1408** (the History of Parliament date, anchored to the escheator's writ; Daniel Gurney's reading of the inquisition gives the Tuesday before St Nicholas, 5 December).[^hop][^dg-supp-121] The inquisition is calendared in the *Calendar of Inquisitions Post Mortem*, vol. 19 (entries 489–94; The National Archives C 137/70/6): writ 16 February 1409; inquisitions at East Rudham, Holt, and Attlebridge; **heir Edmund his son, aged ten**; and the decisive finding that he **"held nothing in Norfolk"** of the Crown in chief — the family's manors were all held of mesne lords, which is why no substantial royal record of the estate exists.[^cipm19]

His only son **Edmund** "died sine prole [without offspring], and probably under age" not long after his father; with the senior line extinct, the whole estate passed by right of blood to John's nephew **Thomas Gournay I (G21)**, son of John's brother Robert (G22).[^dg-supp-121] John's own will was registered at the Norwich Consistory Court but cannot now be found: the register Harsyke ends in 1404 and the next register (Hyrnings) does not begin until 1419.[^dg-supp-118]

## Standing among the Norfolk gentry

John moved in the leading knightly circle of his county. **Sir John Howard's** will left him a new silver cup with a cover, alongside Sir William Beauchamp, Sir John Marmion, Sir Stephen Hales, Sir John de Burgh, and Sir Richard Sutton.[^armstrong-howard] The History of Parliament life of **Sir Robert Berney** independently places John in the Lancastrian Norfolk bloc — fellow shire knight of 1399, Erpingham co-trustee, and co-feoffee in the Saxthorpe/Loundhall circle (Erpingham, Wynter, Berney, Shelton).[^berney] Not every relationship was friendly: early in 1408 he began building a bridge over the river Wensum at Hellesdon, and the citizens of Norwich, fearing lost toll revenue, obtained a royal writ suspending the work.[^hop]

## Downstream: Alice's later life and the Heylesdon feud

Alice survived John by at least twenty-five years. She sold "Loundhall" to John Wynter to pay his debts, married twice more (the Fitzalan retainer Sir John Wiltshire, d. 1428, and then Richard Selling, esquire), and in **1433 sold the bulk of her Heylesdon inheritance to Sir John Fastolf KG**.[^hop] That sale is the upstream event for the Heylesdon → Fastolf → Paston → Heydon chain that drew John's great-nephew William Gurney IV (G19) into the 1472 Saxthorpe contest — treated in full on the [G19 companion](https://github.com/allengurney/gurney-genealogy/blob/main/research/people/g19-william-gurney-iv-fact-sheet.research.md).
