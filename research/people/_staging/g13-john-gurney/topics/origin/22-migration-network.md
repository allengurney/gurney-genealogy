# Origin — migration and reception network

**John Gurney left no record of his Atlantic crossing — no passenger list names him, no arrival document survives — but that silence is what the period predicts, not a puzzle in itself: he crossed in the tail of the Great Migration's 1638 peak, exactly when named New England passenger lists thin to nothing. What the colonial record *does* show is how he was received. His earliest appearances place him among men already a half-generation settled at Weymouth, so his migration reads as the reception of a newcomer into an established community rather than arrival with a known emigration party. That reception network fixes the pattern of his settlement but is a weak lever for his English origin, because his colonial associates and landlords point to several English regions at once. The one exception worth stating is geographic: the adjacent town of Hingham drew a dense Norfolk cohort out of the very parishes that frame the Gurney family's Norfolk world, so a Norfolk-origin arrival received through Hingham is the one reception model that fits the favoured origin — an argument developed, and held, in the identity work.**

## Reception into an established community, not a shared voyage

The men John is first grouped with were established Weymouth settlers, not fellow newcomers. In the 30 May / 2 June 1641 gunpowder-fines matter — dated and read at image level in the [colonial arrival-chronology topic file](../colonial/01-arrival-chronology.md) — he stands with **James Ludden** and **Richard Porter** "of Weymouth." Ludden was an "Old Planter," at Wessagusset/Weymouth by 1632, the guide Winthrop's party followed across Luddam's Ford; Porter was a settler of the Rev. Joseph Hull company that reached Weymouth in 1635.[^ludden][^porter] John's continuing Weymouth ties run the same way: his 1663 estate still owed "Goodman King of Weymouth," John King, an early King's-Cove planter.[^king] A newcomer of the later 1630s attaching himself to men already settled is a reception pattern, not the signature of a shared crossing.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000049 -->

Read as a network, the same picture deepens. John's first colonial ground was a Weymouth land-and-neighbourhood web: his East and Mill Field parcels — town grants he took up as an admitted inhabitant in his arrival years and had largely passed on by the 1643 property record — sat among neighbours drawn from Weymouth's two documented settler streams, an Aylesbury-Vale Buckinghamshire group (Randall of Wendover, Brandon of Ashton Clinton) threaded with a West-Country/Dorchester group (Richards and Hart of the *Mary and John*), several of them granted their own land in the same few years. The associate rings widen from there — the men named with him in 1641, the neighbours who held or absorbed his parcels, and his later Braintree probate circle — but none resolves into a party he arrived with. The full profile of the Weymouth grant cohort and its English streams is worked out in the [immigration-by-association topic](https://github.com/allengurney/gurney-genealogy/blob/main/research/topics/g13-john-gurney-immigration-by-association.md) and carried, on the record side, in the [Weymouth-community topic file](../colonial/02-weymouth-community.md); the bearing here is only that John entered an existing community by association, land, and locality rather than as one of a named shipload.[^assoc-network]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000061 -->

## No record of the crossing

No passenger list documents John's ship. A search of the three surviving named lists nearest his 1641 appearance — the **Bevis** (1638), the **Diligent of Ipswich** (1638), and the **Mary Anne of Yarmouth** (1637), as transcribed by Drake and arranged by Banks — finds no Gurney of any spelling; the only "Gourney" in Drake is William Gourney, Mayor of Dartmouth, on an unrelated St Christopher voyage. The full working compilation of the three lists, with each passenger's English origin and destination, is the [passenger-list topic file](https://github.com/allengurney/gurney-genealogy/blob/main/research/topics/massachusetts-bay-passenger-lists-bevis-diligent-mary-anne.md).[^banks][^drake]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000050 -->

That John is on none of them means little on its own, because of *when* he crossed. He is at Weymouth by the 1641 gunpowder matter and an original grantee in the pre-1643 land division, so he probably reached New England in **1638–early 1641** — the tail of the Great Migration, which crested in 1638 and fell away sharply after 1640. The years that most plausibly hold his crossing, 1639 to mid-1641, are exactly the years for which named Boston-area passenger lists survive worst: the surviving evidence for them is ship-movement and licence records with passenger counts but no names, not manifests. A man arriving in that window would very often leave no listed name even if the record-keeping had been perfect, so the absence of a passenger entry is the expected outcome, not evidence about his party or his origin.[^gm-timing]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000060 -->

## The Hingham corridor and the Norfolk affinity

The one adjacent community whose records could test a reception corridor is **Hingham, Massachusetts** — under ten miles from Weymouth, its town line abutting John's East and Mill Field parcels. A full-text search of its early town records returns no Gurney of any variant, including the Rev. Peter Hobart journal, a key early Hingham record, does not list a Gurney at Hingham between 1638 and 1641.[^hingham]

The reason Hingham as a possible immigration conduit is worth testing is its origins. New England towns drew regionally concentrated settler cohorts, and Hingham's was overwhelmingly Norfolk: Daniel Cushing's contemporary list records that "the whole number that came out of Norfolk … and settled in this Hingham" between 1633 and 1639 was two hundred and six, the 1638 *Diligent* alone bringing about 133. That cohort came out of Hingham, Wymondham, Attleborough, Great Ellingham, and the Dereham neighbourhood — the very parishes that frame the Henry and Francis Gurney Norfolk world, including **Great Ellingham**, Henry Gurney's (G15) manorial seat, **Hingham**, where the family held Gurney's Manor, and **East Dereham**, the parish of John's probable baptism. A dense community from the Gurneys' own Norfolk country thus sat directly across the town line John's Weymouth lots abutted. Weymouth's own settlers were another matter — a West-Country-led town (Hull's 1635 Somerset-and-Dorset company) with the Buckinghamshire stream second, and no Norfolk stream feeding it — so a man of Norfolk origin would have been an outsider to Weymouth's founders but a natural fit for the Norfolk colony next door. A Norfolk arrival that landed at Hingham and stepped the short distance to Weymouth is therefore the one reception model that suits the favoured Norfolk (Candidate B) origin. The origin case itself belongs to the identity work; what the migration record contributes is the geography that makes the corridor plausible.[^norfolk-corridor]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000062 -->

## A Gurney on the corridor: Ann Gurney and John Gilman

**A Gurney-surname marriage runs through the same Norfolk corridor.** Ann Gurney married John Gilman, a worsted weaver, at Hingham, Norfolk, on 1 October 1626; the couple's children were baptised at West Dereham and Hingham, Ann was buried at Hingham on 23 November 1651, and their son John Gilman Jr. later emigrated to Exeter, New Hampshire. The marriage took place in Gurney country — Hingham held the ancient manor of "Hingham Gurneys" — and joined Ann to a Norfolk worsted-weaving family of exactly the textile world the direct-line Gurneys worked in.[^ann-marriage][^ann-hingham]

Whether this Ann was John Gurney's own sister is unresolved. The Pease genealogy identifies her as a daughter of Francis Gurney by his first marriage, which — if correct — would make her John's sister and place a **second Gurney sibling** on the Norfolk-to-New-England corridor. But no primary record links her to Francis; her Gilman children were baptised at **West** Dereham, in western Norfolk near Downham Market, some twenty miles from the **East** Dereham of Francis's children, so the two Derehams must not be conflated; and a separately recorded Ann Gurney married Peter Woodcocke at West Dereham in 1618/19 and may be a different woman. The kinship rests on a single compiled genealogy, not a record. Even held at that low bar, the marriage is a concrete Gurney instance travelling the corridor this unit describes; the origin conclusion it would reinforce is argued, and held, in the identity work.[^ann-sisterhood][^ann-corridor-external]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000114 -->

## The Brackett brothers and Brampton Gurdon's own borough

John's tightest documented Braintree associates carry a compiled-level echo of the same Gurdon patronage geography already documented for the senior Gurney branch. Richard and Peter Brackett — Richard a Winthrop-fleet emigrant and Boston First Church covenant signer, Peter John's single most recurrent colonial associate (the 1646 meadows co-petitioner, a Billerica right-holder alongside John in 1659, a witness to John's 1661/2 Thayer deed, the officiant at John's 1661 remarriage, and an estate creditor in 1663) — are read by modern compiled genealogy as sons of a Sudbury, Suffolk family, Richard baptised at St Gregory, Sudbury, on 16 September 1610, son of Peter Bracket.[^brackett-brothers] Sudbury was Brampton Gurdon of Assington's own parliamentary borough: Gurdon's family had held the Suffolk manor of Assington, four miles from Sudbury, since 1556, his father sat for the borough in 1571, and Gurdon himself was elected its MP in January 1621 — the same Gurdon whose 1627 letter to John Winthrop carried the West Barsham Gurney wardship business into the Court of Wards, and who was, in the History of Parliament's own words, Winthrop's "friend and neighbour," recruiting him to canvass Suffolk gentry in 1626 and still corresponding with him in New England as late as 1637.[^gurdon-sudbury] If the compiled Sudbury reading holds, John's closest colonial associates were themselves raised inside Gurdon's own parliamentary and patronage geography — the same Stour-valley world, four miles from Assington, that produced the wardship letter for the senior West Barsham Gurneys.

This is a network suggestion at compiled level, not a documented tie. The 1907 *Brackett Genealogy* found no Sudbury connection at all: working from an 1885–6 antiquarian extract, it offers only a distinct, and by its own account "meager," Norfolk scrap — a Richard Brackett of Wreningham admitted to Gonville and Caius College in 1574 — as the sole surviving hint of the family's English origin, and does not establish Peter's own relationship to Richard beyond a probable brotherhood. Neither the Sudbury baptism nor the Wreningham scrap has been checked against a parish-register image, and the modern scholarly citation of record for the Brackett family, the NEHGS *Early New England Families* sketch of Richard Brackett, remains unread — both stay open verification steps, not claims made here. Nothing in this reading connects John Gurney himself to the Bracketts by blood, record, or origin; it shows only that his own closest documented associates sit, on the reading modern compilers give them, inside the same East Anglian puritan-gentry patronage web already documented for the senior branch.[^brackett-network-external]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000117 -->

## A weak lever for origin

For every other region, the reception network fixes the mode of John's settlement without fixing where in England he came from. His documented colonial associates and landlords do not point to one place: his Weymouth land-neighbours were an Aylesbury-Vale Buckinghamshire and West-Country mix; his Braintree landlord was William Tyng of Stanford Rivers, Essex; his son-in-law Daniel Shed came from Finchingfield, Essex; and the town itself took its name from Braintree, Essex. Weymouth's own minister in John's arrival years adds a further eastern-counties thread rather than a settled one: Venn records Thomas Jenner as a Fordham, Essex man who served at Weymouth 1636–40 and, years after leaving it, held the Norfolk living of Coltishall (1652–58) — an eastern-counties figure who passed near, not through, John's own Norfolk corridor, no more than texture alongside the Weymouth credit tie already carried in the colonial-community unit.[^jenner-venn] John attached to whichever local community held land or kin, so association is a weak instrument for origin — strong enough to show he entered an existing network, too diffuse to name the corridor that carried him. The favoured Norfolk-origin reading, and the Hingham/Diligent Norfolk-corridor reception model that would fit it, are arguments for the identity work; the migration record on its own establishes reception, not origin.[^mixed][^origin-external]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000051 -->
