# Origin — trade and training

**John Gurney was a tailor, and a tailor's trade was the kind of thing a man of his time usually learned from his father. The father the identity work proposes for him, Francis Gurney, was himself a Merchant Taylor from a Norfolk family steeped for generations in the region's worsted-and-woollen economy — so if John was trained anywhere it should show in the records that enrolled such training. It does not. Targeted searches of the two formal systems that would have caught a Norfolk or London tailor of his generation — the Merchant Taylors' Company of London and the Norwich city freemen and apprentice registers — turn up no John Gurney in the emigrant window. His craft was therefore learned off the enrolled books, by an informal or country training that left no indenture or company freedom to name his master or fix the place. The one enrolled "John Gurney" apprenticeship anywhere in the New England orbit, the 1636 Newgate indenture, belongs to a younger, de-conflated man, not to the Braintree emigrant. The trade is a real handle on his origin — it points back toward a Norfolk textile world and, if the parentage holds, his father's own line of work — but its specific route stays unrecorded.**

## A tailor's son of a tailoring family

That John was a tailor is not in question here: he is styled "John Gurney, tailor" in his own 1661/2 Braintree deed and entered as a tailor in the compiled Braintree genealogy, a finding documented with its sources in the [Braintree-community unit](../colonial/03-braintree-community.md). What this unit asks is the origin question the trade opens — where and how a man learns a skilled craft is a fact about his English formation — and the natural starting point is the family. In this period a craft passed most often from father to son, and John's likely father was a clothing tradesman himself. Francis Gurney was apprenticed in London in 1599 and made free of the Merchant Taylors' Company in 1606; his commercial life ran through Norwich, and in 1622 he joined an agreement to teach the poor of King's Lynn to spin worsted-yarn, providing the wool for the work, alongside Martin Hill of Great Ellingham, a woolchapman. The wider Gurney family and the Norfolk gentry it married into had sat in East Anglia's worsted-and-woollen economy for generations. So the expectation the record has to answer is a specific one: a tailor's son of a tailoring family should be traceable to a master. The parentage that makes Francis the father is the identity work's, not re-argued here; but on that reading, John's trade fits the family, and the question becomes why no enrolled record of his training survives.[^father-textile]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000059 -->

## The Merchant Taylors' Company: no binding, no patrimony freedom

The most direct place John's tailoring might have been recorded is his proposed father's own livery company, and it is silent. Scott's full transcription of the Merchant Taylors' Company binding books and freedom registers for 1583–1800, filtered for every Gurney variant across all its sheets, holds no binding of a John Gurney son of Francis Gurney to any Merchant Taylor master, and no Gurney at all among the 3,391 freedoms granted by patrimony. The two John Gurney apprentices the dataset does hold are other men — John, son of William Gurney, glover, of "Moborne," Worcestershire (bound 1602), and John Gurny, son of John Gurney, ironmonger, of Aylesbury, Buckinghamshire (bound 1655) — neither of a chronology or parentage that fits the emigrant.[^mt] Whatever produced John's trade, it did not run through the Company, neither as an apprenticeship to a fellow Merchant Taylor nor as a patrimony freedom taken up after Francis Gurney's death. The absence does not refute the case for a Merchant Taylor father — that case never rested on a company record of the son — but it closes the first and most obvious formal channel.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000045 -->

## The Norwich registers: no freedom, no enrolled apprenticeship

The second formal channel is the city of Norwich, the natural place to look if John was trained in East Anglia rather than London. It is silent too. The Norwich freemen register carries the Gurney name only in the sixteenth century — a John Gurney admitted to the freedom, not by apprenticeship, in 1560/61, and a John Gurney, cordwainer, dead by 1580, whose own apprentices were freed at the end of the 1570s — with a single seventeenth-century Gurney freedom, that of James Gurny under Henry Bond in January 1648.[^norwich-freemen] The enrolled apprentice-indenture index tells the same story from the binding side: its one seventeenth-century Gurney is James Gurney, son of Francis of St Peter Parmentergate, Norwich, bound at Michaelmas 1627 for seven years — the indenture behind that 1648 freedom, and a Norwich-side trace of a Francis Gurney household there, not of the emigrant.[^norwich-appr] No John Gurney freedom and no enrolled John Gurney apprenticeship falls anywhere in the colonial-emigrant window, about 1615 to 1660. The two systems that between them enrolled the training of East Anglian and metropolitan tradesmen carry no record of John's.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000046 -->

## The one enrolled "John Gurney" apprenticeship is not his

There is exactly one enrolled apprenticeship of a "John Gurney" anywhere in the New England record, and it belongs to another man. On 21 July 1636 the Boston feltmaker John Newgate brought his apprentice John Gurney before the governor, the young man having "gotten away his indentures," and the court bound him to service until the age of twenty-four, three years from the following Michaelmas — an order that implies a birth about September 1615.[^newgate] That is six to thirteen years younger than the Braintree emigrant, whose 1652/3 deposition gives him "aged 50 or thereabouts," and in whose later Boston records Newgate names no Gurney at all. The apprentice is de-conflated from John of Braintree in the identity work on both chronology and record grounds, and is carried here only for its trade bearing: it documents a different, younger man's service, so no enrolled record of the Braintree John's own trade training survives even in the colony that received him.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000047 -->

## An informal or country training

With both formal pathways blank, the reading that remains is that John Gurney's tailoring was learned off the enrolled books entirely — by a country apprenticeship to a market-town master who never enrolled the indenture, or by informal training within a household — leaving no company freedom, city admission, or enrolled binding to name his master or fix the place. The formation the trade implies is real and origin-bearing: a skilled tailor was a trained artisan, not a labourer, and the craft points to a market-town or urban upbringing of the kind an East Anglian origin would supply, in a family already in the cloth trade. But the specific route stays unrecorded, and the negatives bound the record, not the fact: many competent tradesmen of the period never enrolled an indenture or took up a corporate freedom, so the silence narrows where to look without unsaying that John was trained. Read together with the family picture, his trade is one of the sharpest un-exploited handles on his origin — an enrolled indenture or freedom naming a town and a master, if one is ever found, would place his formation as no colonial record can, and would test the father-son reading directly.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000048 -->
