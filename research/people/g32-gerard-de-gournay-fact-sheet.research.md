## Working Notes

### Gerard's seal — primary evidence (DG-Supp Note 16)
2026-04-18 — DG-Supp Note 16 (p. 735) records Gerard's seal: "Signum Girardi de Gornaco" from the Cartulary of La Trinité de Rouen (ed. M. Deville, Tome III de la collection des Cartulaires de France, Charter No. 94). This is direct physical evidence — a seal impression on a surviving charter — confirming Gerard's existence and his use of the Gournay/Gornaco name. The charter's date is not specified but the Cartulary is a published source.

### St. Wandrille charter — Gerard as overlord (DG-Supp Note 17)
2026-04-18 — DG-Supp Note 17 (pp. 735–736) preserves a full Latin charter text from the Chartulary of St. Wandrille (Caux archives de la Seine Inférieure, f. 314, piece cotée D, II, VI). Gerard is named as the lord whose assent (*annuente Girardo de Gournai*) was required for a land grant to the church of Saint-Wandrégisile. The charter names Turchetillus, Willelmus, Godfridus, and "Hugo filius ejus" as grantors of land at "la Corberere." In exchange, the grantors and their heirs were admitted to the fraternity and society of the church, with the right to enter as monks if they chose.

This charter shows Gerard exercising seigneurial authority — nothing could be given from his lordship without his consent. It's a textbook example of a Norman overlord's role.

### Dispute with William de Britolio — a local conflict (DG-Supp Note 18)
2026-04-18 — DG-Supp Note 18 (p. 736) transcribes a passage from a c. 1125 treatise on the miracles of St. Nicholas, preserved at the Bibliothèque d'Évreux (MS. No. 132). The text describes a "discordia" between William de Britolio (William of Breteuil, lord of Pont-Saint-Pierre) and Gerard de Gournay: William was sheltering one of Gerard's knights who had rebelled. The dispute escalated to the point where the inhabitants of the town (Pont-Saint-Pierre?) feared their homes would be burned and began moving their possessions to safer locations.

This is a rare glimpse of Gerard in a local political context — not as a distant Crusader but as a Norman lord dealing with the messy realities of vassal rebellion and inter-seigneurial conflict. William de Breteuil was a powerful neighbor; the dispute shows Gournay holding its own against a significant rival.

### Death date discrepancy
2026-04-18 — Multiple conflicting traditions for Gerard's death:
- **DG-I:** "died before 1104, Palestine" — died on a second pilgrimage to Jerusalem with his wife Edith.
- **Richardson (soc.genealogy.medieval, 2003):** "died 10 May 1097, Nice, Asia Minor on crusade" — this appears to conflate Gerard's death with the siege of Nicaea (June 1097) during the First Crusade.
- **Genealogy Online / Hannay:** "*Hierosolymam petens in ipso itinere mortuus est*" — died on the journey to Jerusalem.
- **DG-I pedigree p. 277:** "Gerard, eldest son, died vit. pat. in 1104" — this names Gerard's *eldest son* (also named Gerard) as dying during the father's lifetime. 

**The confusion:** There may be two Gerards — the father (G32, the Crusader who died on a second pilgrimage) and his eldest son (also Gerard, who died "vitae patris" i.e. during his father's lifetime). DG-I p. 277 makes this explicit but the pedigree is compressed. Richardson's 1097 date may belong to the son, not the father.

**Working resolution:** DG's chronology has Gerard surviving the First Crusade (returning c. 1100), then departing again for the Holy Land with Edith, dying en route before 1104. The eldest son Gerard died separately. The fact sheet should make this two-person distinction clearer.

### Edith de Warenne's remarriage — Dreux de Monceaux
2026-04-18 — After Gerard's death, Edith returned to Normandy and married Dreux (Drogo) de Monceaux. They had a son, also named Dreux, who (or his father) witnessed the foundation charter of Dunstable Abbey by Henry I. This is documented in DG-I pp. 27–28 and confirms Edith survived Gerard by a significant period.

### Gundred/Gondrada claim as granddaughter of the Conqueror
2026-04-18 — The Warren charters' claim that Edith de Warenne's mother Gundred was a daughter of William the Conqueror is contested by modern historians. The Conqueror's legitimate children are well-documented and Gundred is not among them. The claim is preserved in the Warren charters but was likely a later interpolation to enhance the family's prestige. The fact sheet currently handles this correctly with appropriate caveating ("According to the Warren charters...").

### DG-Supp Note 104 — Walter de Gournay as son of Gerard
2026-04-18 — DG-Supp Note 104 (pp. 776–777, already read for Batch 3) provides the crucial genealogical proof that Walter (G31) was certainly a son of Gerard. DG's argument is generational: "Hugh de Gournay IV, eldest son of Gerard, was of full age in 1112; he was therefore born about 1090; and the younger sons of Gerard de Gournay were therefore born between 1090 and 1104 — the date of Gerard's death." Walter's grandson Matthew first appears c. 1160, consistent with only two intervening generations (Walter → William → Matthew).

This note also proposes the naming: "He might probably have been named by his father Gerard, after his near relation Walter Giffard, Earl of Buckingham" or "after Walter de la Ferté."
