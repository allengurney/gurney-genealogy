# The Buckinghamshire and Hertfordshire John Gurneys — elimination on individuating records

Buckinghamshire held five to seven John Gurneys at once through the emigration decades. That density is
what makes the county hard: a man's departure is invisible when his same-named neighbours keep the name
in the register continuously. This unit records the test that survives that problem, the records that
now carry it, and which men remain unaccounted for.

## The test

A household is eliminated only by a record that individuates **the man**, not by continuity of the
surname in a parish register. Two classes qualify:

- an individuating record of him after the emigration date — burial, probate, certificate of residence,
  tax or tenancy record naming him; or
- a chronological impossibility against the colonial John's own documented life.

**The chronology the test runs against.** The colonial John had a wife **Mary** by about 1627 and
English-born children from about 1628. He was **not among the 1636 Weymouth land grantees**, which is
indirect but substantial evidence that he had not arrived by 1636; pre-1636 arrivals are also a much
smaller and far more heavily worked population, and a century of scrutiny of the early New England
arrivals has produced no John Gurney. His first American record is June 1641. So the crossing falls in
**1637–1641**. His wife Mary lived until her death at Braintree on **20 September 1661**.

Mary's survival to 1661 is the sharpest instrument in the set and had not previously been used as one:
any Englishman who marries a different woman between 1637 and 1661 is not the Braintree John.

## The Buckinghamshire probate tier

The Archdeaconry of Buckingham series (Bucks Archives D/A/We, D/A/Wf) is not item-level indexed in The
National Archives' Discovery catalogue, and most Buckinghamshire husbandmen and shepherds proved there
rather than in Canterbury — so a Prerogative Court negative leaves the county substantially unsearched.
That tier has now been read. The printed **British Record Society vol. 114**, *Index to Buckinghamshire
Probate Records 1483–1660*, is available as page images, and its complete GURNEY block runs across two
printed pages.[^brs114]

**Forty-two Gurney-variant probate records survive in the Buckinghamshire courts to 1660.** The full
verbatim transcription is in the corpus
extract;[^extract] the three findings that bear on the identification are these.

**Nine testators are named John**, at 1523, 1552, 1554, 1556, 1589, **1592**, **1623**, **1630** and
**1636**. Three of those are new individuating English deaths for men the register work could not
resolve: **John Gurney of Monks Risborough, 1623**; **John Gurney of Hulcott, yeoman, 1630**; and **John
Gurney of Southcott in Linslade, 1636**. The Linslade one lands exactly at the start of the emigration
window, so Linslade's records stopping in 1636 is explained by a death rather than a departure.

**A Buckinghamshire Gurney tailoring trade runs across four parishes and eighty-five years.** William
Gurneye of Aylesbury, tailor (1571); Luke Gurneye of Aylesbury, tailor (1591); Thomas Gurney of Rowsham
in Wingrave, tailor (1628); Richard Gurny(e) of Mursley, tailor (1631); Thomas Gurney of Weston
Turville, tailor (1633). Four of the five fall inside the emigrant's working life. **The colonial John
Gurney of Braintree was a tailor**, and the case file's occupational argument for Candidate B rests on
an inherited Merchant Taylor trade that the Merchant Taylors' own apprentice rolls do not support — the
company dataset returns no John Gurney son of Francis at any binding 1583–1800 and no Gurney patrimony
freedom at all. Buckinghamshire, by contrast, has a documented Gurney tailoring trade in the right
county in the right decades. This does not make any single Buckinghamshire candidate more likely, but it
removes the trade argument's exclusivity to Candidate B and gives the Buckinghamshire class an
occupational fit it was not previously credited with.

**The Stewkley Gurneys did not vanish in 1614.** The probate index carries **Robert Gurnie of
Stewkley, husbandman, 1618; Robert Gorny/Gerny of Stewkley, husbandman, 1631; Robert Gurney of
Stewkley, yeoman, 1651** — the last rising in status. The family continued at Stewkley for another
forty years. A John of Stewkley proved in 1592, a generation before the household active 1602–1613,
so no Stewkley John of the right generation is accounted for.

**Correction to how that was originally argued.** This was first written as a *register silence
corrected by probate*, on the premise that "the Buckinghamshire parish-register collections carry no
Stewkley Gurney record of any kind after 1614." **The premise is false.** Searched across every
parish-baptism collection at once rather than one dataset at a time, Stewkley carries Gurney baptisms
at **1615 (two, one of them Anthony), 1616 (Anne), 1618, and 1626 (Richard)** — the 1615, 1616 and
1626 entries all naming a father **Robert**, which is the same household the three probates
describe.[^stewkley-baptisms] There was no register silence; there was a single-dataset search. The
distinction reaches beyond Stewkley: a statement that "the collections carry no record" is a
statement about one transcription unless the cross-collection instrument has been run, and several
negatives in this file are written in that form.

**Where the index is silent.** No John Gurney probate at all for Haddenham, Weston Turville (a Thomas,
tailor, 1633, but no John), Hitcham, Amersham (a Thomas of Woodside 1584 only), Ivinghoe, or Bierton
after Henry Gorney of Broughton in 1559.

## The 1641 certificate of residence

The load-bearing individuating record for Candidate A is TNA **E 115/180/113**, carried in the repo as
unverified. It is verified, and its description says more than had been recorded:

> "Certificate of residence showing **John Gurney to be liable for taxation in Northamptonshire, and not
> in the half-hundred of Aylesbury, Buckinghamshire, the previous area**…" — Location: Northamptonshire.
> Date: 1641.[^e115]

This is a government record of a specific man's **move out of Buckinghamshire into Northamptonshire in
1641**, the same year the colonial John first appears in America. It is precisely the individuating class
the register-continuity eliminations lacked, and it ties Aylesbury hundred to Northamptonshire exactly as
the reconstruction requires — Bierton 1628, then Walgrave in Northamptonshire by 1650.

**It also splits Candidate A.** A man taxed in Northamptonshire and no longer in the Aylesbury
half-hundred from 1641 is hard to reconcile with the St Mary Aylesbury baptisms running to 1653 that the
same household has been given. Aylesbury demonstrably held more than one John Gurney — a marriage to
Anne Cowheard on 25 October 1638, a further marriage in 1644, and a third in 1620 — and the parish's
seventeenth-century baptisms are indexed with a father and no mother, so the index cannot assign the
children. The likelier reading is that **the certificate man and the father of the later Aylesbury
children are two men**. That strengthens rather than weakens the elimination, because it eliminates two,
but the single-household reconstruction should be restated as two rows.

## What the survivors are worth

The five survivors are **unaccounted for, not viable**, and the distinction carries the weight.

The colonial John was fathering children in England from about 1628 to about 1635. A household whose
record stops in 1603, 1622, 1627 or 1631 is not baptising children anywhere the test can see during
exactly the years he demonstrably was. Keeping any of them alive requires positing an undocumented move
to an unindexed parish, a marriage there, four baptisms there, and then emigration — the same
absence-explaining structure that weakens Candidate B. In a county holding five to seven simultaneous
John Gurneys, being unaccounted for is the default state, not evidence.

Two of them nevertheless deserve named rows rather than group clearance, because they fit the deposition
age better than several candidates that already carry rows:

- **Weston Turville** — fathering in 1627, so born by about 1602, so about 51 in 1653 against a deposed
  "aged 50 or thereabouts". The parish also has a Gurney tailor, Thomas, who died there in 1633.
- **Hitcham** — fathering in 1631, so born by about 1606, so about 47; and the child baptised there on
  22 January 1631 is named **Mary**.

## The land records — a second individuating tier

The Buckinghamshire Archives catalogue supplies what parish registers cannot: **records that name a man,
his wife, his father, his heir, his parish, his status and his trade, and tie him to a piece of
ground**. Searched for Gurney across 1570–1660, it produces four households the register work had not
individuated, and every one of them is a John who stayed.[^bucks-archives]

**John Gurney, gentleman, of Bishopstone in the parish of Stone.** The most substantial of them. He
appears as plaintiff in a 1626 fine acquiring property in Stone, Bishopstone, Great Kimble and Marsh;
and on **1 March 1648/9 he conveyed away the family's capital messuage in Bishopstone** with its
cottages, closes and lands in Bishopstone, Stone, Hartwell, Great Kimble and Aylesbury. The
corresponding fine of Michaelmas 1649 names him with **Ann his wife and Edward Gurney, son and heir** —
one messuage, two cottages, a dovecote, and about 115 acres. The consideration in the trust that
followed was **£1,700**. Behind him stands three generations at Bishopstone: Stephen Gurney the elder,
yeoman, holding the site of the manor of Stone from 1573; Stephen the younger, "younger son and heir
apparent", in fines of 1590 and 1598/9; and the probate series that runs Thomas 1520 and 1558, John
1552 and 1556, Margery widow 1558, Stephen yeoman 1600, Francis husbandman 1613.

This household is worth naming precisely because it is **the structural twin of Candidate B's motive
argument** — a landed Gurney family liquidating its estate — and it is in Buckinghamshire, not Norfolk.
It does not make this John the emigrant: he is a party to the 1649 fine, so he was alive in England
thirteen years too late, and he had a son and heir named Edward, not Richard. But it shows that "a
Gurney family sold everything, so a son had reason to leave" is not a Norfolk-specific circumstance.

**John Gurney the younger of Owlswick, in Monks Risborough.** A marriage settlement of 20 September 1619
records **John Gurney the elder of Owlswick, husbandman**, and his wife **Elizabeth**, settling a
messuage and arable in Owlswick in consideration of the impending marriage of their son **John to Alice
Hewett, daughter of John Gyles of The Marsh, yeoman** — the remainder going to the heirs of John the
younger. This is almost certainly the household behind the **1623 Monks Risborough probate**, which
Owlswick lies within.

**It also matters for Candidate A.** Buckinghamshire now holds **two distinct John Gurney × Alice
couples** — this one from 1619, and the Bierton marriage to Alice Oliffe in 1628. A wife's forename was
doing eliminating work in the same-name sweep; here it demonstrably cannot.

**John Gurney of Farnham Royal, yeoman**, and his wife **Susan, daughter of Oliver Cogeram**, sold two
acres of meadow in Beaconsfield to Edmund Waller for £55 in April 1633, and both **signed by mark**. A
new household entirely, at the far south of the county, documented in England in the emigration decade.

**John Gurney of Aylesbury, ironmonger**, serving as an **overseer of the poor for Aylesbury in 1652**,
as a churchwarden's counterparty in 1658, and as a **Surveyor of Highways** in 1660. His trade matches
the Merchant Taylors' record of a John Gurny, son of John, ironmonger of Aylesbury, bound in 1655 —
so the Aylesbury John who held parish office was an ironmonger with a son of the same name, and neither
is a tailor.

Alongside them the catalogue documents a **Gurney estate at Marsworth** (D-X_202, running 1617–1844),
with Richard Gurney of Marsworth, husbandman, buying land in 1650 and 1651 and Richard Gurney the
younger, yeoman, in 1659; and **William Gurney of Chesham, yeoman**, buying land in 1616 and 1618.

**What this tier does to the picture.** It deepens the density problem rather than resolving it — the
county held still more simultaneous John Gurneys than the registers alone showed. But every John it
individuates is a John who stayed, held land, served office, or died in place. **Not one of the newly
surfaced men disappears.** That is a real, if indirect, argument: where the record is rich enough to
individuate, Buckinghamshire Johns are visibly settled. The men who remain unaccounted for are
unaccounted for because the record is thin, not because it shows them leaving.

## Coverage limits that bound these negatives

- **Hertfordshire has had no equivalent probate pass, and cannot yet.** The published-index dataset does
  not contain Hertfordshire at all — a control search on a common surname returns zero as well, so the
  blank is a missing volume, not an absence of Gurneys.[^herts] The Hertfordshire parish collection also
  holds no seventeenth-century Berkhamsted Gurney record, so Candidate C's household is not reachable
  through it. Candidate C is eliminated on the father's own age, which is immune to the masking problem,
  so the gap is real but not urgent.
- **Northamptonshire is covered only from 1677, and only for administrations** (British Record Society
  vols 70 and 92). The death window of the 1641-certificate man is not covered.[^northants]
- The Buckinghamshire index gives testator, parish, occupation, year and court reference — **not the
  will text**. Wills name children; the three new probates could each resolve a household the index can
  only bound. Reading them is a Buckinghamshire Archives request.
