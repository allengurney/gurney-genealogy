# Identity — Candidate B: Norfolk parentage

**John Gurney of Braintree was probably the son of Francis Gurney, a Merchant Taylor of West Barsham, Norwich and London, by his first wife Margaret Rybett. Two primary records carry the claim: a 1611 Norwich marriage that no earlier genealogist had found, which gives Francis a first wife and a first family in the right years, and a degraded East Dereham baptism entry, indexed for a century as "Nicholas Gorne," that on close comparison reads "John the sonne of ffrancis Gurnie." Around them sit a trade that ran from father to son, an emigration corridor, a documented motive, and a Gurney kindred living within five miles of the register. The identification is cumulative and indirect. Nothing in the record names John as Francis's son, no English marriage or child baptism of his own has been found, he named no son Francis, and his one self-reported age reads five to eight years older than the baptism. The case file weighs those offsets and puts the parentage at roughly 65 percent — probable, not proven.**

## The father: Francis Gurney of West Barsham, Norwich, and London

Francis Gurney was born 13 September 1581 at West Barsham Hall, Norfolk, the sixth son of Henry Gurnay, Esq., of Great Ellingham and West Barsham, by his wife Ellen Blennerhassett. Henry had twelve children. The family was old Norfolk gentry with an armigerous pedigree, but a sixth son had little to inherit and had to make his living.[^francis-parentage]

He made it in cloth. He was bound apprentice in London on 14 May 1599, aged about seventeen, to Henry Tryme of the Merchant Taylors' Company "near Ludgate," for a seven-year term beginning at Whitsun. On 3 February 1605 the Company court ordered him assigned over to William Smooth, Merchant Taylor of Lothbury, ahead of a documented journey north — the first concrete sign of his re-engagement with Norfolk, six years before he married there. The Company's binding book records his admission to the freedom on 30 June 1606.[^mt-apprenticeship]

Daniel Gurney, the nineteenth-century family historian, wrote that Francis's "commercial life began at Norwich," and the Hunstanton Hall account book bears him out: Francis acted as "a sort of agent, or banker" for the Lestranges, with payments running from January 1612 to May 1636.[^norwich-lestrange] The business went badly. A King's Lynn worsted-spinning venture, set up under an agreement of 17 October 1622 to employ the borough's poor children, collapsed and left Sir Hamon Lestrange to pay Francis's £100 bond.[^kings-lynn] On 11 July 1634 Francis sold every acre he held — all his Norfolk and Suffolk manors and lands, to Sir Owen Smith of Irmingland for £1,000, a forced liquidation conducted through the Court of Wards.[^1634-sale] He was buried at St Botolph Bishopsgate, London, on 9 January 1646/7.[^rybett-family]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000097 -->

## The first marriage nobody had found

On 23 September 1611, "Franc Gurny & Margaret Rybett" were married at St Martin at Palace, Norwich. The entry sits in the Norfolk Record Office parish register PD 12/1 and was located in March 2026.[^rybett-marriage]

It matters because it opens a family. Daniel Gurney spent decades on the Gurney records in the 1840s and 1850s and never found this marriage; nor did Charles Bernau in 1913. Both knew Francis only through his second wife, Anne Browning, whose children begin at St Benet Fink, London, in 1619 — thirteen years after Francis's 1606 freedom, an unexplained gap in every account written before 2026. A first marriage in 1611 fills the gap and produces children born roughly 1609–1617: exactly the generation the Massachusetts emigrant belongs to. It also confirms a claim that had circulated as unsourced family tradition, the Pease genealogy's report — transmitted from the American researcher Philis Wainford through Sir Joseph Gurney Pease — that Francis's first wife was a "Margaret Ryvett."[^pease-confirmed]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000098 -->

## Margaret's own family, four miles from East Dereham

The Rybett or Ryvett family were Norfolk and Suffolk gentry, documented at Fritton in Norfolk and at Rishangles, Rattlesden, Stowmarket, and Bildeston in Suffolk. St Martin at Palace was their own Norwich parish, with Rivett entries running from 1539 to 1603, and Margaret almost certainly married from her own church. (One reported Ryvett–Heydon marriage does not survive checking: the Visitation of Suffolk gives Myrable Ryvett's husband as William Burd, Customer of London, not Sir John Heydon. The Gurney–Heydon tie is real but comes through Anne Heydon's marriage to William Gurney V, a different link entirely.)[^rybett-family]

The branch that matters is not the Suffolk gentry but a mid-Norfolk one. Margaret is identifiable at register level as Margaret Rivet, baptised 21 May 1586 at Garveston, daughter of Francis Rivet — full evidence, including the two Garveston probates that fail to bridge the generations, on the [Rivett family of Garveston](../../../rivett-family-of-garveston.md) file. Garveston lies two miles from East Dereham and Gressenhall three, and the Rivett presence in both parishes is continuous. This converts a long-standing guess into something the records support. John was left motherless about 1616–17, when Margaret died and Francis remarried and moved to London; his mother's family lived a few miles from where he had been baptised. And Richard Ryvett of Gressenhall, who married Faith in 1602, is a documented source for the name John gave his eldest surviving son.[^rivett-cluster]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000099 -->

## The East Dereham children — and Entry E

The East Dereham parish register (Norfolk Record Office, PD 86/41, covering 1593–1641) was reviewed across 69 microfilm images. Much of it is too degraded for confident surname-sensitive reading, and most date estimates carry a ±2-year margin because the register's year is often not visible on the page. Six entries concern Francis Gurney's household: a baptism of Edward about 1611/12; a burial of a Marye in late 1615 or January 1616, whose relationship word does not read as "daughter" and who is likely a niece; the burial of Agnes, daughter of Francis Gurney, on 31 January 1616; the baptism of Marye, daughter of Francis Gurney, on 25 May 1618; a probable burial of a son Francis on 8 November 1633; and Entry E.[^east-dereham-cluster]

Entry E, on image 00715, was indexed by Findmypast and FreeREG as "John the sonne of Nicholas Gorne." Compared under magnification against four confirmed "ffrancis Gurnie/Gurny" entries in the same register and the same hand, it reads instead as **"John the sonne of ffrancis Gurnie,"** about 1609/10. Four independent letterform tests — the initial stroke cluster, the mid-body structure, the word segmentation, and the terminal formation — favour "ffrancis"; none favours "Nicholas." The index reads "Nicholas Gorne" because the entry sits on degraded microfilm transcribed without particular attention to the Gurney surname universe.[^entry-e]

Two cautions belong with it. This is an AI-assisted paleographic reading, not a professional examination of the physical register; the canonical next step is a professional reading of the original at the Norfolk Record Office in Norwich.[^entry-e-limits] And the c.1609/10 date sits a year or two *before* the September 1611 marriage. Either the ±2-year margin absorbs it, or John was born before his parents married — common enough in the period to be unremarkable, and neither reading is forced.[^premarital] (A separate wrinkle in the same register: Marye's 25 May 1618 baptism falls only nine months before Dorothy's at St Benet Fink, tight for one mother. Marye is still most likely Anne Browning's first child, since Margaret died about 1616–17 and a May 1618 baptism implies conception after the remarriage; but Margaret's burial has never been found, so the timeline marks Marye's mother as probable rather than certain.)[^east-dereham-cluster]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000100 -->

## Room for an older son

For John to be Francis's son he must be older than Roger, whom the 1633 Heralds' Visitation of London recorded as Francis's "eldest sonne." This is the sharpest documentary objection to the identification, and it stands as written.[^visitation]

It is not, however, a closed list. Daniel Gurney treated Dorothy as the eldest child "mentioned in" the St Benet Fink register — a careful phrase — and judged Roger, the eldest son of that London sequence, to have "probably died young." He also added, beyond the register, a son John of Maldon that Francis "seems" to have had and a George who was "probably another son." Bernau went further and warned outright that no complete record of the children of Francis and Anne Browning survives and that what is known is fragmentary. Neither compiler knew of the 1611 marriage, so no child of it could appear in their reckoning. A visitation recorded the children a man presented; a grown son of a first marriage, living independently or estranged, had no reason to be presented. Roger, baptised at St Benet Fink in December 1621, was the eldest son of the *second* marriage. A John born about 1609/10 would have been twelve years his senior.[^child-lists-open]

The London register supports this reading rather than undercutting it. Seven children of Francis and Anne were baptised at St Peter le Poer with St Benet Fink between 1619 and 1637 — Dorithy 1619, Roger 1621, Frances 1625, Francis 1628, Lucretia 1630, Thomas 1636, Margaret 1637 — and there is no John among them. If John was Francis's son at all, he must belong to the first marriage, which is precisely where the East Dereham entry puts him.[^benet-fink-negative]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000101 -->

## The right Francis

Two other men named Francis Gurney were alive in the same parishes, and the identification collapses if the wrong one is picked up. Walter Rye first separated them. Ours — Francis-A — is the West Barsham gentleman, Merchant Taylor, freed 1606, at East Dereham c.1608–1618 and at St Benet Fink 1619–1637, first wife Margaret Rybett, second wife Anne Browning, buried 1646/7. The other — Francis-B — is a Norwich plebeian laceweaver of St Peter Mountergate, later of St Giles Cripplegate 1638–1640, wife Mary, and the man behind the Costessey property that has occasionally been attached to the wrong household.

The Norwich corporate registers now separate them by simultaneous independent record rather than by trade and later parish alone. James Gurney, son of a Francis of St Peter Parmentergate — the laceweaver's Norwich parish — was bound apprentice at Michaelmas 1627 for seven years and admitted a freeman of Norwich on 8 January 1648. In 1627 Francis the Merchant Taylor was baptising children at St Benet Fink in London. Two Francis Gurney households, two cities, one year. The same registers contain no John Gurney freedom or enrolled apprenticeship anywhere in the emigrant window, which is why Candidate B's trade match has to run through informal or country training rather than a company book — the negative searches are set out in [Origin — trade and training](../origin/21-trade-training.md).[^two-francis]

A third man belongs on the same watch-list. A St Giles Cripplegate burial of December 1640 reads "John sonne of ffrancis Garney Joyner." The trade is joiner, not laceweaver, so this is neither Francis-A nor Francis-B, and the Garney spelling sits squarely inside the Gurney/Gurny/Gourney cluster. Note also that this Cripplegate John was buried two days after his christening, so he is not the emigrant either.[^cripplegate]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000102 -->

## The senior branch in the same corridor

If John was Francis's son, then the head of the family — the senior West Barsham line — was his uncle's and cousin's household, and that household was in the East Dereham neighbourhood in exactly his years.

Muskett's *Suffolk Manorial Families* prints a Crowe-of-East-Bilney pedigree that names a Gurney daughter no Gurney source had recorded: "Bozoune Crowe, of E. Bilney; mar. before 20 Dec. 1636 = Elizabeth, dau. of Thomas, sister of Edward Gournay, of West Barsham, Esq." Elizabeth was therefore a daughter of Thomas Gurney III and Martha Lewkenor, and a sister of the West Barsham heir Edward. East Bilney lies about five miles from East Dereham, in the same Launditch hundred. Under Candidate B, a first cousin of John's was keeping house within a morning's walk of the parish where he was baptised.[^elizabeth-crowe]

The same volume settles the senior line's own confused terminal generation. Two Hovell pedigree charts record "Frances Howell maried to Edward Gurney of East Basham" and "Frances, wife of Edward Gurney of East Barsham, co. Norfolk, Esq., 1647," and the 1647 will of Mary Goodwin of St Giles, Norwich — a Hovell by birth — names "sister Frances Hovell of Hillington: Cozen Gurney" among her legatees. So Edward Gournay's widow was Frances Hovell of Hillington, the Hovells still acknowledged Gurney kin in November 1647, and the "Frances Hood" of the L'Estrange anecdotes is a garble of Hovell.[^hovell]

That places the branch geographically as well as socially. Edward Gournay died 6 August 1641, and Blomefield, quoting the inquisition directly, records that it was "taken at East-Dereham, October 13th in the 17th of Charles I" — the senior branch's own post-mortem inquiry sat in the parish of the Entry E baptism.[^ipm-east-dereham] The inquisition itself is C 142/613/60, read at catalogue level.[^c142] None of this names John. What it does is remove the objection that a Gurney child at East Dereham in 1609/10 would be a stranger there: the family was in and around that parish across the whole period, and the branch's affairs ran through the Court of Wards and, by 1627, through a letter from Brampton Gurdon to John Winthrop himself.[^winthrop-letter] One document could still convert this from geography into kinship — Bozoune Crowe's will, made 15 September 1658 and proved 11 March 1658/9, catalogued at The National Archives under the misrendered name "Bozann Crome" as PROB 11/289/177. It has not been imaged. Interregnum probate ran through the Prerogative Court alone, so it had to be findable there; whether it names Elizabeth's Gournay kin is the open question.[^crowe-will]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000103 -->

## The naming pattern: Peter and the missing Francis

Two of John's five children carry the naming test any parentage argument has to answer, and they cut in opposite directions.

Peter is the puzzle. Of twelve indexed Peter-Gurney baptisms across Britain in the 1632–1642 window, not one was fathered by a John Gurney, so the name did not reach John's youngest son through any indexed Gurney parallel. Peter is not absolutely absent from Norfolk Gurney households — a Peter Gurney, son of Peter, was christened at Smallburgh in February 1641 — but it enters John's own branch most plausibly through his wife Mary's family, where a Peter would sit unremarked. That makes the name a pointer to Mary's kin rather than to Francis, neither for nor against the parentage; recovering Mary's surname, still the standing open question of this research, would explain it whoever John's father was.[^peter-gap]

The harder point is a name that is missing. John named no child Francis. In a period when a first or early son was routinely named for the father, that absence is the single strongest naming-pattern argument against the identification, and it stands as written. John's roster runs Sarah, Mary, Richard, John Jr., and Peter, with no Francis among them. Four explanations have been offered — estrangement from a father who had lost everything, avoidance of a name attached to that ruin, maternal naming priority (Richard may honour Mary's kinsman Richard Ryvett of Gressenhall), or an earlier Francis who died young and left no record — but each is a hypothesis, and none is documented. The absence qualifies the parentage; it does not refute it.[^no-francis]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000115 -->

## What the identification carries, and what it does not

Four threads tie John to Francis's household, and none of them is a document naming him a son.

His trade is the first. Crafts passed from father to son in this period, and the colonial John is styled "John Gurney of Braintry Taylor in new England" in his own 1661/2 conveyance. His father was a Merchant Taylor. Neither the Merchant Taylors' books nor the Norwich registers record the transmission, so the match holds at the level of a household, not an indenture. The second is the corridor: roughly three in five Massachusetts Bay emigrants of the 1630s came from nine eastern counties, and under one in ten from London proper — and Francis's life ran between Norfolk and London for forty years.[^corridor] The third is motive: the 1634 sale left an adult elder son with nothing to expect at home. The fourth is the kindred just described, in the parish and the corridor at the right time.[^parentage-conclusion]

Against them stand four offsets. John's own 1652/3 deposition gives his age as "50 or thereabouts," a birth about 1602/3 and five to eight years earlier than Entry E reads — a tension that eases, but does not vanish, at the early edge of the entry's ±2-year margin (see [Origin — age and baptism](../origin/20-age-baptism.md)). He named no child Francis, the strongest naming-pattern argument against the identification. No record of his marriage to Mary, or of any of his English-born children's baptisms, has been found. And both formal trade-admission pathways return documented negatives. These four are why the estimate is not higher.

What removes the remaining doubt is not positive evidence for Francis but the absence of anyone else. Every other documented English John Gurney of the period has been traced to a life that rules him out — by continuing English residence, by a wife who is not Mary, by children who do not match, or by a death in England — including thirteen probate records at the Prerogative Court of Canterbury that fix seven distinct John Gurneys in England after 1637.[^elimination-corpus] Robert Charles Anderson's *Great Migration Directory* still records John's origin as "Unknown," and it is right to: no surviving record states it.[^anderson] The 1926 American report that the family's arms connected them with the Norfolk Gurneys is a family memory rather than evidence, and would only become evidence if an early American object or manuscript bearing the arms were found; the search for one has so far come up empty, and the reasoning is set out in [Origin — traditions](../origin/25-origin-traditions.md).[^arms]

The case file's own probability model puts Candidate B at roughly 65 percent against an unknown-corridor residue, and that model stays with the case file (§11) rather than being rebuilt here.[^probability] The [John Gurney fact sheet](../../../../../fact-sheets/g13-john-gurney-fact-sheet.md) now uses the same about-sixty-five-percent estimate.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000104 -->
