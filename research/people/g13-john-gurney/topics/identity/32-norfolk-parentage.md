# Identity — Candidate B: Norfolk parentage

**Candidate B is the proposition that John Gurney of Braintree was a son of Francis Gurney, a Merchant Taylor of West Barsham, Norwich and London, by his first wife Margaret Rybett. One primary record carries it: the Norwich marriage of 23 September 1611 that no earlier genealogist had found, which gives Francis a first wife and a first family in the right generation. Around it sit a trade that ran from father to son, an emigration corridor, a documented motive in the total land sale of 1634, and Gurney kindred living within five miles of the parish where Francis baptised his children. What the hypothesis does not have is a household. No document names John as Francis's son; no baptism of his has been found; no English marriage or child baptism of his own has been found; he named no son Francis; and his one self-reported age reads five to eight years older than the birth the hypothesis needs. Candidate B is the leading *named* candidate and stands at about eleven percent — behind three rows that describe the state of the record rather than a man.**

## The father: Francis Gurney of West Barsham, Norwich, and London

Francis Gurney was born 13 September 1581 at West Barsham Hall, Norfolk, twin with Anthony, the sixth son of Henry Gurnay, Esq., of Great Ellingham and West Barsham, by his wife Ellen Blennerhassett. Henry had twelve children. The family was old Norfolk gentry with an armigerous pedigree, but a sixth son had little to inherit and had to make his living.[^francis-parentage]

He made it in cloth. He was bound apprentice in London on 14 May 1599, aged about seventeen, to Henry Tryme of the Merchant Taylors' Company "near Ludgate," for a seven-year term beginning at Whitsun. On 3 February 1605 the Company court ordered him assigned over to William Smooth, Merchant Taylor of Lothbury, ahead of a documented journey north — the first concrete sign of his re-engagement with Norfolk, six years before he married there. The Company's binding book records his admission to the freedom on 30 June 1606.[^mt-apprenticeship]

Daniel Gurney, the nineteenth-century family historian, wrote that Francis's "commercial life began at Norwich," and the Hunstanton Hall account book bears him out: Francis acted as "a sort of agent, or banker" for the Lestranges, with payments running from January 1612 to May 1636.[^norwich-lestrange] The business went badly. A King's Lynn worsted-spinning venture, set up under an agreement of 17 October 1622 to employ the borough's poor children, collapsed and left Sir Hamon Lestrange to pay Francis's £100 bond.[^kings-lynn] On 11 July 1634 Francis sold every acre he held — all his Norfolk and Suffolk manors and lands, to Sir Owen Smith of Irmingland for £1,000, a forced liquidation conducted through the Court of Wards.[^1634-sale]

He was buried at St Botolph without Bishopsgate, London, on 9 January 1646/7, which corrects Boyd's misread 1641. Two qualifications belong with that date: the entry survives at transcript level rather than register level — FreeREG gives the register type as "Other Transcript," so nobody has seen the parish entry — and Ancestry's 18-million-record London parish collection does not carry it at all.[^francis-burial] His probate is negative in four jurisdictions — the Prerogative Court of Canterbury, the Commissary Court of London (which held St Benet Fink), the Norfolk church courts, and the Essex Record Office — with the Archdeaconry Court of London the one jurisdiction still unchecked. A man ruined by the 1634 sale may have left nothing worth proving, so the negative may be true rather than a gap; as it stands it is the state of the search.[^francis-probate]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000097 -->

## The first marriage nobody had found

On 23 September 1611, "Franc Gurny & Margaret Rybett" were married at St Martin at Palace, Norwich. The entry sits in the Norfolk Record Office parish register PD 12/1 and was located in March 2026.[^rybett-marriage]

It matters because it opens a family. The family historian Daniel Gurney spent decades on the Gurney records in the 1840s and 1850s and never found this marriage; nor did the genealogist Charles Bernau in 1913. Both knew Francis only through his second wife, Anne Browning, whose children begin at St Benet Fink, London, in 1619 — thirteen years after Francis's 1606 freedom, an unexplained gap in every account written before 2026. A first marriage in 1611 fills the gap and produces children born roughly 1612–1617. It also confirms one detail that had circulated only as family tradition — the Pease genealogy's report that Francis's first wife was a "Margaret Ryvett." What the same genealogy says next does not survive checking: its further claim of a son John born c.1610 who married Grizell Fletcher, which is to say the Braintree emigrant himself, carries no source of any kind, and the reasoning that once made it look corroborated was circular.[^pease-note]

**There is no earlier marriage on any searchable instrument.** The obvious rescue for the chronology — that Margaret is called Francis's *first* wife only because hers is the earliest marriage anyone had located — was searched and is not there. Five instruments were run in both the principal and the spouse direction across the full Gurn/Gorn/Gourn/Garn/Girn/Gern variant field: the Norfolk banns-and-marriages index (which carries marriage licences as well), the Norfolk Record Office's own county collection 1535–1812, the England-wide IGI-derived marriage set, the Bishop of London's marriage bonds and allegations, and the Norfolk probate-and-marriage-licence-bond index 1371–1858. Nothing anywhere between 1606 and 1611.[^no-earlier-marriage] What the negative cannot reach is the Norwich licence layer itself — the Consistory bonds, the Archdeaconry registers of licences granted 1590–1632, and the letters of matrimony 1590–1609 — whose only finding aid is Bolingbroke's calendars at the Norfolk Record Office, indexed on the searchroom shelves alone. No online name index reaches that window.[^licence-layer]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000098 -->

## Margaret's own family, two miles from East Dereham

Margaret is identifiable at register level as a **Rivet of Garveston** — either Margaret Rivet, baptised 21 May 1586, daughter of Francis Rivet, or, a live alternative, Margarett Rivet, baptised 1 February 1577/8, daughter of Robert Rivet. The full evidence, the weighing of the two candidates, and the two Garveston probates that fail to bridge the generations are on the [Rivett family of Garveston](../../../rivett-family-of-garveston.md) file. Garveston lies two miles from East Dereham and Gressenhall three, and the Rivett presence in both parishes is continuous. And Richard Ryvett of Gressenhall, who married Faith in 1602, is a documented source for the name John gave his eldest surviving son — Margaret's first cousin under the 1586 reading, her full brother under the 1577/8 one.[^rivett-cluster]

Two things about this family are commonly misstated, and both matter to the argument.

1. **Margaret's family are village copyholders, not gentry.** The Garveston Rivets held their land by copyhold — a customary tenancy recorded in the manor's own court roll — and are a different social class from the Suffolk gentry Ryvetts of Rishangles and Rattlesden, with whom they are often merged. Whether the two are related at all is unresolved, and the evidence points against it: the Suffolk Ryvetts held land directly of the Crown and so generated business in the Court of Wards, and no mid-Norfolk Rivett appears in that series at all. The spelling matters too. "Rybett" is the 1611 clerk's one-off; the family writes itself *Rivet*.[^rivett-status]
2. **The marriage parish is unexplained.** St Martin at Palace is sometimes described as the Rybett family's own church, on the strength of Rivett entries in that register between 1539 and 1603. Those are Norwich city Rivetts, and nothing connects them to the Garveston copyholders fifteen miles away. Francis and Margaret therefore married in a parish where neither family is otherwise documented — an open question, not a settled explanation.[^marriage-parish]

One further connection sometimes drawn to this family does not survive checking: a reported marriage between Myrable Ryvett and Sir John Heydon. The *Visitation of Suffolk* gives her husband as William Burd, a customs official of London. The Gurney–Heydon tie is real, but it runs through Anne Heydon's marriage to William Gurney V — a different link entirely.[^rybett-family]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000099 -->

## What the East Dereham register holds, and what it can never be asked

The East Dereham register is **PD 86/41, "Indented register bills, 1593–1641"** — loose annual certificates rather than a bound book, catalogued and digitised as 110 contiguous images. Read across all 110, the file is strictly chronological with no membrane out of sequence, and every annual return carries a contemporaneous in-parchment heading against which the archive's pencil years check out exactly.[^pd8641-structure]

Two windows of loss govern everything the register can be asked.

**The returns for 25 March 1611 to 25 March 1616 do not survive**, and no second witness ever existed. The Norfolk Record Office's own per-parish summary gives East Dereham as original registers c.1549–1596 (unfit for production), then nothing until 1679, with archdeacon's and bishop's transcripts only from 1698 — eighty-two years after the missing window. The loose bills of PD 86/41 are the only witness the parish ever had for 1593–1641. **This is a permanent evidentiary limit, not a pending pull.**[^pd8641-loss] A first child of the September 1611 marriage falls in roughly 1612–1615, so **that birth window is permanently unobservable**: Candidate B can never be confirmed or refuted from this register for those years, and the hypothesis may draw no more comfort from the silence than its rivals may draw damage from it.

**Between 1594 and 1608 the file holds four annual returns and is missing twelve** — 1593-4, 1595-6, 1601 and 1608-9 survive; nothing survives for 1594-5, for 1596-7 through 1600-1, or for 1602-3 through 1607-8. No Gurney-variant entry appears in any surviving pre-1610 return, with the confidence stated per return: 1593-4 and 1595-6 are clean, well-preserved negatives read line by line; 1608-9 was read at 4× line strips and is clean; **the 1601 return is a weak negative**, doubly flagged faded and soiled, with a heavy central stain and a minority of surnames unresolvable at any magnification.[^pre1610]

Six entries in the surviving membranes concern a Francis Gurney household. **Edward, son of ffrancis Gurney, baptised 27 May 1610**, is the firmest of them, fixed by the in-parchment "East Dereham 1610" heading rather than by an inferred page-year. **Marye, daughter of ffrancis Gurnoe, baptised 25 May 1618**, sits inside the 1618-19 return on the same footing. The others are a burial of a Marye in late 1615 or January 1616 whose relationship word does not read as "daughter" and who is likely a niece; the burial of Agnes, daughter of Francis Gurney, on 31 January 1616; and a probable burial of a son Francis on 8 November 1633.[^east-dereham-cluster]

**There is no baptism of a John in this register.** The entry indexed as "John the sonne of Nicholas Gorne," read in March 2026 as "John the sonne of ffrancis Gurnie" and carried thereafter as the identification's strongest single anchor, does not exist as described. The line adjudicated in March 2026 is on image `00722` and reads *"Amorye the daughter of Robart Enderby, Ianuarij 10"*; the record the index actually describes is on image `00726` and reads *"John the sonne off Nicholas Horne bapt — Janua 10"*, 10 January 1616/17.[^audit] Two further consequences follow for anything derived from the older reading. The May 2026 chronology lattice is superseded, because its interpolated rows assumed one page per annual return, where each return runs across several membranes and each membrane is photographed twice. And "page 00725 = the 1615 register year" was never available: images 00724 and 00725 are the marriages-and-burials membranes of the **1610** return, and no 1615 return exists at all.[^pd8641-structure]

Losing the baptism relieved a chronological strain rather than creating one. While it stood, John's birth had to be squeezed into c.1609/10 — before his father's September 1611 marriage, from a father bound a London apprentice until 1606, and awkwardly early against a deposed age of about fifty in 1653. Removing it lets Francis's own chronology run cleanly: Norwich from about 1606, married 1611, an East Dereham household 1610–1618, London from 1618-19. The friction now sits entirely on a different question — **where a son John born about 1603–1608 came from**.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000100 -->

## East Dereham had its own Gurney family, and it was not a humble one

Why East Dereham at all? Why should a sixth son of the West Barsham gentry baptise his children in a market town five miles from the family seat, in a parish no earlier account connected to the Gurneys?

The parish held a Gurney household three generations before Francis's children, and it was propertied. Three records assemble the household.

- **1543 — the marriage.** Michael Gurney of East Dereham married Marion Waggestar, a widow of the same parish, at St Stephen's, Norwich. The Norfolk antiquary Walter Rye's chronological list of Gurneys at Norwich carries the entry verbatim.[^rye-michael]
- **Before 1603 — the Chancery suit.** Michael's son **Arthur Gourney** sued Michael's widow **Margery**. Father and son had been *jointly seised of certain tenements in East Dereham* — that is, they held the freehold together — the son consented to his father selling them, and the father in return left him a legacy of **£100**.[^c2]
- **1584 and 1591 — Arthur's own standing.** By 1591 Arthur was styled **gentleman, of Yaxham**, two miles from East Dereham, buying a house in Pottergate Street in the Norwich parish of St Gregory. He was probably also the Arthur Gurney of Great Dunham who married Katherine Walpoole, a widow, by licence on 12 April 1584.[^rye-arthur]

Margery herself proved a will in 1602–03, and the manor court remembered her Ellingfield holding for decades afterwards: a court of 3 April 1627 records a neighbouring plot bounded on the west by *"terram nuper Margerie Gurney vid[ue]"* — land lately of Margery Gurney, widow — and a court of 10 October 1636 recites the same memory. A croft in the later rolls is still named **"Gurneys Croft."**[^margery-will-anf][^ellingfield-abuttals]

Every marker in that record says the family was substantial rather than humble.

- **"Jointly seised" is freehold language** — father and son held the property outright, not as tenants of a manor.
- **A £100 legacy in the 1590s is not a cottager's bequest.** It is several years' income for a prosperous yeoman.
- **Arthur is styled *gentleman*, married by licence rather than by banns, and bought a house in a Norwich city parish** — three separate status markers, none available to a village copyholder.

Margery's own will was proved in the Archdeaconry of Norfolk, the court for smaller estates, and that has been read as marking a humble household. The reading is sound for her own goods and wrong about the family. **The parish held a Gurney family of the right standing, in the right generation, to have produced a Francis.**

**That makes the East Dereham finding symmetric, and the symmetry is the point.** A propertied local Gurney family in the parish explains a Gurney child being baptised there just as well as Francis of West Barsham does — and what positively places Francis in the parish is only the children themselves. No record names him there. **Whether the "ffrancis Gurney" of the 1610 and 1618 baptisms is Francis G14 or the local family's man is an open question.** If it is the local family's, what Candidate B loses is its *Norfolk residence anchor* — not the candidate. Francis G14's own identity and geography are documented independently of East Dereham at every point: West Barsham 1581, London apprenticeship 1599–1606, Norwich from about 1606, the Norwich marriage of 1611, St Benet Fink from 1619. That would be a serious reduction and it is not a collapse.

The direct test is Arthur Gurney's household at Yaxham and Great Dunham: his 1584 marriage should be followed by children in the later 1580s and 1590s, and one of them either is or is not named Francis. **That test has never actually been run.** FindMyPast's Norfolk Baptisms returns no Gurney row at Yaxham — but the positive control returns 125 Smith rows there of which every one is 1806 or later, and a Smith query bounded to 1560–1640 returns zero. The set is the Norfolk FHS transcription, it is parish-selective, and it omits Francis Gurney's own East Dereham children. There is no coverage to make a negative out of.[^yaxham] Arthur is likewise absent from the Norfolk probate-and-marriage-licence-bond index across the whole 1371–1858 run, so Rye's 1584 licence is not reachable online and remains an offline pull in the Norwich licence layer.[^arthur-62679]

And the register cannot settle it either. The twelve missing returns between 1594 and 1608 include **1602-3, the year Margery Gurney's will was proved** — so her burial is unobservable in the only witness the parish ever had, exactly as the 1611–15 window is unobservable for Candidate B. **Both hypotheses are shielded by the same losses, and neither may draw comfort from the other's silence.**[^pre1610]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000125 -->

## Room for an older son

For John to be Francis's son he must be older than Roger, whom the 1633 Heralds' Visitation of London recorded as Francis's "eldest sonne." This is the sharpest documentary objection to the identification, and it stands as written.[^visitation]

It is not, however, a closed list. Daniel Gurney treated Dorothy as the eldest child "mentioned in" the St Benet Fink register — a careful phrase — and judged Roger, the eldest son of that London sequence, to have "probably died young." He also added, beyond the register, a son John of Maldon that Francis "seems" to have had and a George who was "probably another son." Bernau went further and warned outright that no complete record of the children of Francis and Anne Browning survives and that what is known is fragmentary. Neither compiler knew of the 1611 marriage, so no child of it could appear in their reckoning. A visitation recorded the children a man presented; a grown son of a first marriage, living independently or estranged, had no reason to be presented. Roger, baptised at St Benet Fink in December 1621, was the eldest son of the *second* marriage.[^child-lists-open]

The London register supports this reading rather than undercutting it. Seven children of Francis and Anne were baptised at St Peter le Poer with St Benet Fink between 1619 and 1637 — Dorithy 1619, Roger 1621, Frances 1625, Francis 1628, Lucretia 1630, Thomas 1636, Margaret 1637 — and there is no John among them. If John was Francis's son at all, he must belong to the first marriage.[^benet-fink-negative]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000101 -->

## The right Francis

Two other men named Francis Gurney were alive in the same parishes, and the identification collapses if the wrong one is picked up. The antiquary Walter Rye first separated them. Ours — Francis-A — is the West Barsham gentleman, Merchant Taylor, freed 1606, at East Dereham c.1608–1618 and at St Benet Fink 1619–1637, first wife Margaret Rybett, second wife Anne Browning, buried 1646/7. The other — Francis-B — is a Norwich plebeian laceweaver of St Peter Mountergate, later of St Giles Cripplegate 1638–1640, wife Mary, and the man behind the Costessey property that has occasionally been attached to the wrong household.

The Norwich corporate registers now separate them by simultaneous independent record rather than by trade and later parish alone. James Gurney, son of a Francis of St Peter Parmentergate — the laceweaver's Norwich parish — was bound apprentice at Michaelmas 1627 for seven years and admitted a freeman of Norwich on 8 January 1648. In 1627 Francis the Merchant Taylor was baptising children at St Benet Fink in London. Two Francis Gurney households, two cities, one year. The same registers contain no John Gurney freedom or enrolled apprenticeship anywhere in the emigrant window, which is why Candidate B's trade match has to run through informal or country training rather than a company book — the negative searches are set out in [Origin — trade and training](../origin/21-trade-training.md).[^two-francis]

A third man belongs on the same watch-list. A St Giles Cripplegate burial of December 1640 reads "John sonne of ffrancis Garney Joyner." The trade is joiner, not laceweaver, so this is neither Francis-A nor Francis-B, and the Garney spelling sits squarely inside the Gurney/Gurny/Gourney cluster. Note also that this Cripplegate John was buried two days after his christening, so he is not the emigrant either.[^cripplegate]

A fourth Francis Gurney household enters from Essex and belongs to the generation after: **Francis Gourney and Ann at Maldon, St Mary the Virgin**, with ten children baptised 1655–1667 — John 1655, Jane 1656, Francis 1657, George 1659, Thomas 1660, Charles 1662, Richard 1664, Francis 1665, Anna 1666, Henry 1667. That supplies at register level the John and the Thomas behind the bachelor John of Maldon who died in 1681 with administration to his brother Thomas, a claim that had rested on Daniel Gurney and Bernau alone. It starts in 1655 and therefore bears on Francis G14's descendants, not on the colonial John.[^maldon]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000102 -->

## The senior branch in the same corridor

If John was Francis's son, then the head of the family — the senior West Barsham line — was his uncle's and cousin's household, and that household was in the East Dereham neighbourhood in exactly his years.

The genealogist Joseph Muskett's *Suffolk Manorial Families* prints a Crowe-of-East-Bilney pedigree that names a Gurney daughter no Gurney source records: "Bozoune Crowe, of E. Bilney; mar. before 20 Dec. 1636 = Elizabeth, dau. of Thomas, sister of Edward Gournay, of West Barsham, Esq." Elizabeth was therefore a daughter of Thomas Gurney III and Martha Lewkenor, and a sister of the West Barsham heir Edward. East Bilney lies about five miles from East Dereham, in the same Launditch hundred. Under Candidate B, a first cousin of John's was keeping house within a morning's walk of the parish where Francis baptised his children.[^elizabeth-crowe]

The same volume settles the senior line's own confused terminal generation. Two Hovell pedigree charts record "Frances Howell maried to Edward Gurney of East Basham" and "Frances, wife of Edward Gurney of East Barsham, co. Norfolk, Esq., 1647," and the 1647 will of Mary Goodwin of St Giles, Norwich — a Hovell by birth — names "sister Frances Hovell of Hillington: Cozen Gurney" among her legatees. So Edward Gournay's widow was Frances Hovell of Hillington, the Hovells still acknowledged Gurney kin in November 1647, and the "Frances Hood" of the L'Estrange anecdotes is a garble of Hovell.[^hovell]

That places the branch geographically as well as socially. Edward Gournay died 6 August 1641, and the eighteenth-century Norfolk historian Francis Blomefield, quoting the inquisition directly, records that it was "taken at East-Dereham, October 13th in the 17th of Charles I" — the inquiry into the senior branch's own lands sat in the parish.[^ipm-east-dereham] The inquisition itself is C 142/613/60, read at catalogue level.[^c142] None of this names John. What it does is remove the objection that a Gurney child at East Dereham in this period would be a stranger there: the family was in and around that parish across the whole period, and the branch's affairs ran through the Court of Wards and, by 1627, through a letter from Brampton Gurdon to John Winthrop himself.[^winthrop-letter] One document could still convert this from geography into kinship — Bozoune Crowe's will, made 15 September 1658 and proved 11 March 1658/9, catalogued at The National Archives under the misrendered name "Bozann Crome" as PROB 11/289/177. It has not been imaged. Interregnum probate ran through the Prerogative Court alone, so it had to be findable there; whether it names Elizabeth's Gournay kin is the open question.[^crowe-will]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000103 -->

## The naming pattern: a missing Francis, a predicted Richard, and Peter

Three naming tests bear on this parentage, and they run in different directions.

**John named no child Francis.** In a period when a first or early son was routinely named for the father, that absence is the single strongest naming-pattern argument against the identification, and it stands as written. John's roster runs Sarah, Mary, Richard, John Jr., and Peter, with no Francis among them. Four explanations have been offered — estrangement from a father who had lost everything, avoidance of a name attached to that ruin, maternal naming priority, or an earlier Francis who died young and left no record — but each is a hypothesis, and none is documented. The absence qualifies the parentage; it does not refute it.[^no-francis]

**The naming custom read forwards predicts a paternal grandfather named Richard, and Candidate B's is Francis.** Richard is the eldest known son of the colonial household, and under the ordinary custom the eldest son took the paternal grandfather's name. That is a derived prediction rather than an independent observation, and it is conditional on Richard actually being the eldest son and on the custom being followed — it was followed loosely. But within the shape every candidate is scored against, Candidate B is the one row that attribute **contradicts** rather than merely misses.[^attribute-10] The rival explanation, that Richard honours Margaret's kinsman Richard Ryvett of Gressenhall, is structurally awkward as well: a maternal grandfather belongs in the second-son slot, not the first.

**Peter is not a lever at all, in either direction.** No Peter Gurney-variant baptism exists anywhere in Great Britain 1627–1647, and no Isaac 1632–1652, wildcarded across all parish-baptism collections; both men first appear in colonial records. Their absence from the English record is what the evidence expects rather than something a candidate must explain, and the rare-name discriminator therefore does not bear on the English search at all.[^peter-isaac] The corollary matters for the baseline: **the children born in England are Mary, Richard, John and possibly Sarah** — four at most, not the six the compiled genealogies list.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000115 -->

## What the identification carries, and what it does not

Four threads tie John to Francis's household, and none of them is a document naming him a son.

His trade is the first. Crafts passed from father to son in this period, and the colonial John is styled "John Gurney of Braintry Taylor in new England" in his own 1661/2 conveyance; his father was a Merchant Taylor. The label itself stands — it is primary and single-attested — but the inference chain built on it is softer than it looks. Neither the Merchant Taylors' books nor the Norwich registers record any transmission; no John Gurney is styled tailor anywhere in the pre-1700 full-text corpus; a Buckinghamshire Gurney tailoring trade runs across five testators between 1571 and 1633, so the trade is not distinctive of this family; and John's own 1663 inventory carries plough irons, oxen and grain and no tailoring tools.[^trade] The second thread is the corridor: roughly three in five Massachusetts Bay emigrants of the 1630s came from nine eastern counties, and under one in ten from London proper — and Francis's life ran between Norfolk and London for forty years.[^corridor] The third is motive: the 1634 sale left an adult elder son with nothing to expect at home, a documented push event inside the window. The fourth is the kindred just described, in the parish and the corridor at the right time.[^parentage-conclusion]

Against them stand five offsets.

- **The age.** John's own 1652/3 deposition gives his age as "50 or thereabouts," a birth about 1602/3. The hypothesis needs a son of a marriage of September 1611, or a pre-marital birth before it; either way the arithmetic is strained (see [Origin — age and baptism](../origin/20-age-baptism.md)).
- **No baptism.** No baptism of a John to this family survives anywhere, and the East Dereham window in which the hypothesis needs one is permanently unobservable.
- **No household.** No record of John's marriage to Mary, and none of any English-born child's baptism, has been found — on five instruments, England-wide, from both the father axis and the mother axis.
- **No Francis.** The naming argument above.
- **Both formal trade-admission pathways return documented negatives.**

**The structural point is that Candidate B is a context without a household.** Scored against the fifteen-attribute shape, B is strong on corridor, trade lineage and push event and scores zero on every attribute that describes an actual household — a father, a mother, and children with dates. That is not a peculiarity of this candidate; it is one half of the central structure of the whole problem, in which documented *men* and documented *households* form two disjoint sets and no candidate is in both.[^two-families]

**And Candidate B has no monopoly on an emigration link.** It is tempting to rank a hypothesised man above documented ones on the ground that only he has a documented reason and route to leave England. The colonial record does not support that.

- **Candidate B has an English-side corridor with no Gurney at the colonial end.** Francis's Norfolk-and-London geography, the 1634 liquidation, the Puritan uncle and the Coleman Street proximity all describe a departure environment. None of it puts a Gurney on a ship or in a New England town record.
- **The Buckinghamshire candidates have a colonial-side reception channel with no Gurney at the English end.** Two of John Gurney's own land-neighbours at Weymouth came from Wendover and Ashton Clinton — three to five miles from parishes where Gurneys demonstrably lived. Weymouth had a documented settler stream out of the Aylesbury Vale and no Norfolk stream at all. That is an arrival environment with no identified Gurney entering it.

**Neither side has the middle link.** The right statement is a symmetry, not an advantage.[^weymouth]

**The strongest remaining argument for B is the absence of anyone better, and that argument is weaker than it looks.** Every other *documented* English John Gurney of the period has been traced to a life that rules him out — by continuing English residence, by a wife who is not Mary, by children who do not match, or by a death in England, including thirteen Prerogative Court probate records that fix seven distinct John Gurneys in England after 1637.[^elimination-corpus] The catch is that the enumeration counts **households as reconstructed rather than men**, and every household tested for that defect has turned out to be more than one man. Five named candidates stand alongside B, and three rows describing the state of the record hold more probability between them than every named candidate combined.[^assessment]

The genealogist Robert Charles Anderson's *Great Migration Directory* records John's origin as "Unknown," and that is the correct entry: no surviving record states it.[^anderson] A 1926 American compilation reports that arms kept by American Gurneys connected them with the Norfolk Gurneys; that is a family memory rather than evidence, and it would become evidence only if an early American object or manuscript bearing the arms were found. The search for one has come up empty.[^arms]

Candidate B stands at about **eleven percent** — first among named candidates, and behind the record-state rows. It does not gain from the emptying of the searched corpus: an emptied corpus would benefit B only through its absence-explaining auxiliary, which is precisely the move this file's own method forbids.[^probability]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000104 -->
