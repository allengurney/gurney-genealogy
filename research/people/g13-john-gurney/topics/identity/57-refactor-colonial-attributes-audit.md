# The colonial template, audited — Sarah, Isaac, and the tailor label

Every candidate in this file is matched against a template drawn from the colonial records: a tailor
named John, wife Mary by about 1627, children Sarah, Mary, Richard, John, Peter and possibly Isaac.
The template has been treated as fixed while the English side was rebuilt twice. It is not uniformly
solid, and the differences matter, because two of the arguments now in play rest on its weakest
parts.

## What each element actually rests on

| Element | The document behind it | Standing |
|---|---|---|
| **Mary**, daughter | Married Daniel Shed at Braintree, 1647; seven Shed births in the town book 1647–1658[^shed] | **Primary. Solid.** |
| **Richard**, son | The direct-line G12, of Weymouth, with his own record trail | **Primary. Solid.** |
| **John Jr**, son | Witnessed his father's Braintree conveyance of 12 February 1661; Torrey carries his Mendon household, wife Ruth, children John, Samuel, Mary[^johnjr] | **Primary. Solid.** |
| **Peter**, son | "Peeter Gurnay" of Weymouth in Captain Isaac Johnson's company, Dedham muster 10 December 1675[^bodge] | **Primary as a man. Parentage by the compiled roster and elimination.** |
| **Isaac**, son | Isacke Gurney whipped at Scituate by the Plymouth court 1 March 1663/4; jailed at Boston under Daniel Gookin's warrant, June 1667[^isaac] | **Primary as a man. Parentage by elimination only — no record names his father.** |
| **Wife Mary** | Died at Braintree 20 September 1661; her marriage bounded before 1628 by daughter Mary's 1647 marriage[^wife] | **Primary for the death; the marriage date is an inference from the daughter.** |
| **Tailor** | "John Gurney of Braintry Taylor in new England" in the registry copy of his 12 February 1661/2 conveyance[^tailor] | **Primary, single attestation.** |
| **Sarah**, daughter | — | **No primary record of any kind.** |

## Sarah

Sarah is not simply the weakest name on the list. **She is the only member of the household with no
document attesting that she existed.** Every other name — including Isaac, whose parentage is
unproven — is attached to a record of a living person. Sarah is attached only to a list.

The list is Waldo Chamberlain Sprague's *Genealogies of the Families of Braintree*, which gives the
children as Sarah, Mary, Richard, John and Peter, Sarah first; the Weymouth town history carries the
same set, and Rigler's Gurney genealogy repeats it.[^roster] Three compilations, not three
witnesses: the later two follow the same tradition rather than testing it, and none cites a record
for Sarah.

**The negative is better bounded than "no record has been found."** Clarence Almon Torrey's *New
England Marriages Prior to 1700* is an exhaustive trawl of New England marriages across the whole
period, and its Gurney block carries John1 and both wives, and John Jr and Ruth at Mendon — but no
Sarah Gurney marriage anywhere.[^torrey] A daughter born around 1628 who reached adulthood in the
Braintree of the 1640s and 1650s and married in New England should be in Torrey. So if Sarah
existed, she died young, died unmarried, or married outside the compendium's reach.

The Braintree town book, which records her sister's marriage in 1647, her mother's death in 1661 and
her father's remarriage seven weeks later, records nothing of her. Her father's 1663 estate file
names no kin at all, so it neither confirms nor excludes her.[^probate] One nearby Sarah Gurney was
checked as a possible source of the tradition and does not serve: the Sarah Gurney of Providence,
Rhode Island who died in 1714 was a widow Field who married a Gurney, two generations later and in
another colony, with a living Gurney husband and five Field children.[^providence]

**Reading:** Sarah is best carried as **doubtful rather than probable** — a compiler's name with no
attestation, in a roster whose other members all have one. She should not be removed from the
published child list, because compiled tradition is not nothing and Sprague was a careful worker
from the Braintree records. But she should stop being weighed as though she were on a level with
Mary, Richard and John Jr.

## Sarah does not carry the weight the case file assigns her

The case file singles her out: "One colonial-side check now carries unusual weight … The naming
inference in §6.6 depends entirely on Sarah, the supposed eldest child, not existing. If she is
real and eldest, Richard moves to the second-son slot, the maternal-grandfather reading
returns."[^casefile]

**That is not how the custom counts.** The English naming convention runs son-slots and
daughter-slots separately — the first *son* takes the paternal grandfather's name, the first
*daughter* the maternal grandmother's — so a daughter, however senior in birth order, does not
occupy a son's slot and cannot displace Richard from first son to second. **Richard is the eldest
son whether or not Sarah existed.** The prediction that the colonial John's father was named Richard
does not depend on Sarah at all, and the Cheddington household reached by that prediction is
correspondingly more robust than the case file says.

What Sarah bears on is the **daughter** sequence, and there the effect is interesting rather than
destructive. If Sarah existed and was the eldest daughter, then under the custom Sarah takes the
maternal grandmother's name — the colonial John's wife Mary was a daughter of a **Sarah** — and Mary,
as second daughter, takes the paternal grandmother's name, so **the colonial John's own mother was
named Mary**. That is a mismatch for Margaret Rybett and a mismatch for Jane Founteine, so on a
strict reading Sarah's existence tells *against* both Candidate B and Cheddington equally, and
settles neither. If Sarah did not exist, Mary as eldest daughter most simply honours her own mother,
a common relaxation of the custom, and the daughter line yields nothing.

## Two predictions the convention has not been read for

Reading the son sequence all the way through, rather than stopping at the first slot, produces two
testable claims that no pass has stated.

**John Jr is the second son, and the second-son slot belongs to the maternal grandfather.** The case
file's unit treats him as "a later son carrying the father's own name, as the custom allows once the
grandfather slots are filled" — but that is the *third*-son rule, and John Jr is second.[^unit54] On
a strict reading, **the wife Mary's father was named John.** That is a search parameter: a marriage
of a John Gurney to a Mary, daughter of a John [surname], in the south Midlands or East Anglia
c.1626–1635.

**Peter is the third son, whose slot — the father's own name — was already spent on John Jr.** When
that happens the slot conventionally passes outward, most often to an uncle: the father's eldest
brother or the mother's. So the standing reading that Peter comes from the mother's kin is right in
direction but can be sharpened — **look for a Peter who is a brother of Mary, or a brother of John.**

Both readings are only as good as the assumption that this family followed the custom at all, which
is exactly what the missing Francis calls into question. They are offered as search heuristics, on
the same footing as the Richard prediction that produced the Cheddington household — cheap to test,
and worth nothing until tested.

## Isaac, and what the Cheddington argument leans on

The second naming signal behind the Cheddington household is Isaac: a name absent from every Norfolk
Gurney household surveyed, present in the Cheddington Gurney name-pool in the emigrant's own
generation.[^unit54]

That argument stands on a child whose membership in the family is itself an inference. **Isaac is not
in Sprague and not in Rigler.** He enters the roster because a young Isacke Gurney surfaces in the
Plymouth and Suffolk court records of 1663–1667, and because no other Gurney male in the
Braintree–Weymouth–Boston orbit was old enough to have fathered a man of his apparent age. That is a
reasonable identification and the dedicated subject file preserves him cautiously as a distinct
person pending a record that names his father.[^isaac]

The consequence for the Cheddington reading is modest but should be stated: **its second leg rests on
an unproven child, and so cannot be much stronger than that child's parentage.** The first leg — the
Richard prediction — is now firmer than the case file allows, and the second is softer. The two do
not compound.

## The tailor label stands; what it is allowed to prove does not

The 1663 inventory lists wearing clothes, bedding, pewter, a cupboard, a musket, plough irons, a
coulter, two axes, a mattock, a chisel, barrels and trays, wheat and Indian corn, a horse and a yoke
of oxen, sows and pigs, two young cattle, an old barrow and a pair of wheels — and **no tailoring
tools at all**: no shears, no pressing iron, no yard, no thread, no cloth in stock.[^inventory]

The direction to hold, and it is the right one: **John Gurney keeps the tailor label.** It is a
primary attestation in a registry-copied conveyance, it is what the compiled genealogies carry, and
there is no evidential basis to discount it. Farming and tailoring were not exclusive in a small
colonial town, and the absence of tools at death has an ordinary explanation: an ageing or infirm man
in a community where a tailor's kit was a scarce and valuable thing would be expected to have passed
it on, and he had already sold his Braintree freehold two years before he died, with an estate of
£55 carrying £37 of debt.

What the inventory does bear on is narrower and worth carrying: **the trade is attested once, at the
end of his life, in a document that had a reason to style him.** It is not corroborated by his own
goods, and no colonial record shows him practising. That does not weaken the fact; it weakens the
*inference chain* built on it — the argument that a Merchant Taylor father makes a tailor son more
likely, and the counter-argument that a Buckinghamshire Gurney tailoring trade does the same. Both
arguments are heavier than the single word they rest on. **Keep the label; hold the origin inference
loosely.**
