# Identity — assessment

John Gurney's parentage is settled by elimination and circumstantial fit, not by a document. No record made in his lifetime names his father, and none ever will unless a new one is found: the identification of him as the son of Francis Gurney, Merchant Taylor of West Barsham, Norwich and London, rests on the failure of every other documented John Gurney to fit, plus a set of independent signals — trade, corridor, motive, network, a baptism — that all point the same way and none of which is decisive alone.[^cb] The case file puts the parentage at roughly sixty-five percent, and that estimate belongs to the case file's own argument, not to this unit.[^s11-xref]

Two things make this elimination stronger than most colonial origin attributions. Every rival was actively disproved rather than left unexamined: Candidate A by continuous English residence to 1653, Candidate C by a family of the wrong shape, Candidate D by a 1662 London hearth-tax entry made in the year the colonial John lay dying at Boston, the Earsham testator by dying in England in 1638, and the 1636 Newgate apprentice by a mismatched family status and a chronology that will not bend.[^candidates] And the colonial record now shows exactly one adult Gurney household in Massachusetts before 1660 — the Dorchester "Gurneys" are Gurnells, the Providence John and Sarah are a later and unconnected pair, and there is no second emigrant John to confuse with ours.[^collaterals] The weakness is the mirror image of the strength: Candidate B predicts a man who would leave almost no English paper — a motherless son raised by his mother's kin, trained outside both the guild registers that would have recorded him — so the absence of a confirming document is exactly what the hypothesis expects, and cannot be counted against it.[^b-record-shape] Anderson's *Great Migration Directory*, working only from what the records state, still gives John's origin as "Unknown."[^anderson]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000109 -->

## Candidate C — Berkhamsted, Hertfordshire

Candidate C is a Berkhamsted John Gurney whose eight children were baptised there between 1610 and 1636: Henry (1610), Sara (1615), Jhon (1624), Richard (1626), Elizabeth (1629), Michael (1631), Sarah (1634), and Francis (1636).[^herts-baptisms] The Hertfordshire burial index records no John Gurney burial at Berkhamsted between 1640 and 1700; the only seventeenth-century Berkhamsted burials under the name are two entries for a Jhon Gourney, in 1612 and 1620, both too early to be this man.[^herts-burials]

The eight-child group eliminates him three times over. Henry's 1610 baptism puts the father's own birth no later than about 1585–1590, thirteen to eighteen years before the colonial John's c.1603. Three of the eight children — Henry, Michael, and Francis — have no counterpart in the colonial family, and the colonial John's Mary and Peter have no counterpart at Berkhamsted. The three shared names miss on date: Berkhamsted's John Jr. was baptised in 1624 against the colonial John Jr.'s c.1633, Richard in 1626 against c.1630, Sarah in 1634 against c.1628.[^herts-baptisms] Any one of the three grounds would be enough. Candidate C is the last of the principal alternatives to be cleared, and the only one whose working file the graph had not yet absorbed.[^c-file]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000105 -->

## What the eliminations do not clear

Clearing named households is not the same as clearing the record. The same-name sweeps turned up more than forty distinct John Gurney heads of household in England between 1600 and 1670, and English parish-register coverage for the period is patchy enough that others certainly existed unrecorded.[^same-name-sweep] The residual is not an abstraction. The London hearth-tax returns of 1662–1666 list eleven Gurney or Gurny households across the City, Westminster, and inner Middlesex — Walter Gurney at Greene Dragon Court in St Margaret's Westminster, William Gurny with seven hearths on the south side of Fleet Street, William Gurney at Cock and Key Alley in St Dunstan in the West, Ann Gurney with five hearths at Two Crane Court in the same parish, and seven more — a denser London Gurney population than any of the candidate searches had surfaced.[^hearth-tax] None of them is a candidate: they are all alive and in London after the colonial John was dead. What they show is how many Gurney households the record can hold without any of them being visible to a search built around a name, a wife, and a child set.

The case file carries that residual as two unknown-corridor scenarios — an undiscovered candidate in the dominant East Anglia-to-London emigrant corridor, and one outside it — and they are why the estimate for Candidate B sits in the sixties rather than the nineties.[^s11-xref]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000106 -->

## The emigrant's own family leaves no English trace

Clearing other men is not the same as producing John's own family in the record, and it has not. No indexed English parish-register cluster matches the colonial John's full family signature — a John Gurney with a wife Mary and children Sarah, Mary, Richard, John Jr., and Peter — anywhere in the target window of roughly 1620 to 1640, across more than twenty Gurney-surname baptisms reviewed in the FamilySearch, Findmypast, and Ancestry indexed collections. The nearest clusters are the candidate parishes already weighed — Berkhamsted, Aylesbury, Hitcham, Eythorne, Toddington, Ackworth — and every one of them is weak on the children's dates, on the mother's name, or on both. The colonial John's first three children, Sarah, Mary, and Richard, most plausibly sit in an unindexed eastern-England register set; their absence from the indexed collections is a coverage gap, not evidence that they were born anywhere in particular, and least of all in Massachusetts. It is the same silence, read in the baptism registers, that the missing marriage record shows in the marriage registers — and it is precisely the thin paper a motherless son raised outside the recording parishes would leave, which is what Candidate B predicts rather than what it has to explain away.[^children-matrix]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000116 -->

## The patronage question

The strongest network evidence in this case does not attach to John. In October 1627 Brampton Gurdon wrote to John Winthrop about the widowed "mrs. Gurny," whose son's wardship was moving through Winthrop's own office at the Court of Wards; the Gurneys of West Barsham, in the decade John emigrated, stood inside the personal circle of the men who founded Massachusetts Bay.[^gurdon] That is a fact about the senior branch of the family. Candidate B's John is two steps from it — a nephew of the ward line through his father Francis — and no record connects him to the network at all.[^wardship]

Whether John reached Massachusetts through that network, or simply out of the same county at the same time as thousands of others, is unresolved, and it matters: a documented patronage route would turn a plausible corridor into a mechanism. The test that would settle the chain is a patron-side search of the Norwich diocesan institution books for a 1627–28 presentation to the West Barsham vicarage made by right of the Gurney wardship. It cannot identify the emigrant. It can turn the Gurdon–Winthrop connection from strong construction into citable fact.[^cced]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000107 -->

## Anderson's "Boston"

Anderson's directory gives John's New England settlements as Boston and Braintree.[^anderson] No Boston record names him. The Boston attribution is most plausibly the same conflation that produced the family tradition of an 1615 birth in Southwark: the 1636 runaway apprentice named John Gurney, a distinct and younger man, was bound to John Newgate the feltmaker and lived in Newgate's Boston household — a servant's position that would generate neither church admission nor property record.[^newgate] A short unrecorded stay at Boston by the elder John, before he surfaces at Weymouth in June 1641, remains possible: the town was under a day from Weymouth and traded with it constantly. But a transient lodging is invisible in principle, so the possibility can neither be tested nor used.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000108 -->

## Where the number can go

Two end-states are realistic. A marriage record for John and Mary in the Rivett-orbit parishes around East Dereham, or a kinsman's mention of a Gurney "beyond the seas" in one of the unread Norfolk family wills of the 1650s, would lift the identification from probable to highly probable — somewhere near eighty-five to ninety percent. Failing that, continued negative searching leaves it where it is, a permanently caveated best identification in the sixty-five to seventy percent band, which is already better evidenced than most published colonial origin attributions.[^bottom-line] Three levers carry most of the remaining value: the Norfolk parish and probate images in the Rivett orbit, where the missing marriage should be if John married where he was raised; a professional examination of the East Dereham register at the Norfolk Record Office, which would settle whether the baptism entry reads "John the sonne of ffrancis Gurnie"; and a Y-DNA test of the one discriminating English-line kit, which would fix the American family's paternity against an English Gurney lineage independently of any record.[^entry-e][^ydna]

The published fact sheet now matches the case file's about-sixty-five-percent estimate.[^fs][^s11-xref] The figure remains a caveated synthesis of indirect evidence, not a substitute for the missing direct record.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000110 -->
