# Identity — assessment

**John Gurney's English origin is open.** No record made in his lifetime names his father, and the July 2026 work did not find one. What it did do was close the searchable index layer on three axes and change the shape of the answer: the leading named candidate now stands at about eleven percent, and **three rows that describe the state of the record rather than a man hold forty-four percent between them — more than every named candidate combined.**

That is not a retreat into "nothing is known." A great deal has been eliminated, and the eliminations are findings. What changed is that the elimination stopped producing a survivor.

## The structural finding

The candidates fall into **two disjoint families, and no candidate is in both.**

*Documented households with the wrong shape* — the younger Berkhamsted John, Hitcham, Ackworth, and the eliminated Eythorne — each have a father, sometimes a mother, and children with dates. What they lack is the right children. *Documented men with no household at all* — Candidate B, the Cheddington 1608 John, the 1603 Stewkley baptism, Candidate D and the Weston Turville head — are the best trade-and-corridor fit, the only candidate reached by prediction from the naming custom, the exact age match, the best trade match and the best Weymouth-proximity fit respectively, and **every one of them scores zero on all four child attributes.**

The emigrant needs a man of the right age *and* his household, and every candidate supplies one half. **The man's own baptism and his household's baptisms are probably not in the same searchable place** — one side is lost, unindexed, or in a class not yet opened. That is the strongest argument in the file for the record-state rows, and it is why they lead.[^shape]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000109 -->

## Reading the table

**No option exceeds twenty-two percent, and the leading rows are not people.** Three of them describe the state of the record rather than a man, and together they hold **forty-four percent**.

**Each of the four leading rows implies different work**, which is the practical value of separating them. The *unassembled* row is tested by re-sorting records already in hand — repeatedly productive through 2026, and now largely drained. The *not-yet-searched* row is tested by opening named classes that have never been opened: the south-Midlands licence series, the Suffolk gap, the Buckinghamshire wills. The *no-record* row is not tested by records at all, and Y-DNA is the only lever on it. Candidate B is tested by finding a marriage or a child in the Rivett-orbit parishes, or by a kinsman's will naming a Gurney beyond the seas.

**Why the *unassembled* row is smaller than the merge defects would suggest.** That row describes a man hiding in plain sight — records already searched, never joined up into a person. Merge defects of exactly that kind keep turning up: Candidate A, Stewkley, Berkhamsted, Denton, Toddington, Linslade, Kelham, Bishop's Stortford. **But each one was found *and* resolved, and a resolved defect drains this row rather than filling it.**

The residue is also now countable, which it was not while the father axis was incomplete. The completed sweep yields thirty-one localities for 1615–1649. Classified, the genuinely unattached single events in the colonial window that carry a colonial child forename number **one — Hitcham — and Hitcham already has a named row of its own.** Of the six "scattered single events" the row was once built on, four are assembled: Ivinghoe 1640 is the marriage of John Gurney and Rebecka Cooker on 22 October 1640, Great Kimble 1619 is the Owlswick father-and-son marriage settlement, and Weston Turville and Haddenham carry named rows. The two that remain, Aylesbury 1644 and 1669, fall outside the window entirely.[^assembled]

**Why Ackworth and Hitcham carry as much as they do.** Not because new supporting evidence appeared for either, but because the funnel closed around them: when every other household in the searched corpus resolves, the two that do not carry more of the weight. That is a legitimate inference and it is also a warning — both remain single-record candidates with real blocks, and neither should be read as rising toward identification.

**Why Candidate B does not gain from the emptying.** B would benefit from an emptied corpus, because its auxiliary predicts a man who would leave almost no English paper. **An auxiliary that explains away absence must not draw confidence from further absence.** That rule is what holds B at eleven.

**Falsifiability.** A probability table is exactly the kind of artefact that looks like rigour while resting on judgement. Two guards apply: each row names the evidence it turns on, and the numbers are stated so that they can be moved by a record rather than by a mood. Three specific records would move them materially — the Great Berkhamsted burial register image for 28 June 1638, the Ackworth parish register for 1636–37, and the Epping and North Weald registers at the Essex Record Office.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000106 -->

## What the eliminations do not clear

Clearing named households is not the same as clearing the record, and the counting was optimistic in a specific way: the older enumeration counted **households as reconstructed**, which repeatedly proves to be too few men. Candidate A was one household and is two; Candidate C was one and is two; Great Berkhamsted alone holds four contemporaneous Gurney households rather than the one it was carried as.

The residual is not an abstraction. The London hearth-tax returns of 1662–1666 list eleven Gurney or Gurny households across the City, Westminster and inner Middlesex — Walter Gurney at Greene Dragon Court in St Margaret's Westminster, William Gurny with seven hearths on the south side of Fleet Street, William Gurney at Cock and Key Alley in St Dunstan in the West, Ann Gurney with five hearths at Two Crane Court in the same parish, and seven more — a denser London Gurney population than any of the candidate searches had surfaced.[^hearth-tax] None of them is a candidate: they are all alive and in London after the colonial John was dead. What they show is how many Gurney households the record can hold without any of them being visible to a search built around a name, a wife and a child set.

## The emigrant's own family leaves no English trace

Clearing other men is not the same as producing John's own family in the record, and four independent sweeps have now failed to.

**No John-and-Mary Gurney household in England baptises a Mary, a Richard and a John between 1627 and 1634.** **No Gurney household anywhere in Britain 1620–1640 has a father in the John family and a mother in the Mary family** except Eythorne, Kent, which is eliminated on two burials — and one further household appears only when the surname is expanded to the outer edge of the variant scale, Gurley of Westminster, which fails on the sons. **Every John Gurney-cluster groom in England 1600–1660 is now named with his bride, and not one marries a Mary** — a negative sharper than "none found," because nine Gurney men married Marys in the same holdings, so it is specifically the Johns who have none. And the mother axis, swept for the first time, returns nothing new.[^four-negatives]

**Only four of John's children were born in England, at most.** Peter and Isaac were almost certainly born in New England: no Peter Gurney-variant baptism exists anywhere in Great Britain 1627–1647, and no Isaac 1632–1652. That leaves Mary, Richard, John and possibly Sarah — and Sarah has no primary record at all and does not appear in the compiler Torrey's survey of New England marriages before 1700.[^peter-isaac]

**Sarah's doubtful existence does not bear on the naming argument, despite appearances.** It is tempting to reason that the prediction of a paternal grandfather named Richard depends on Sarah not existing, because Richard has to be the eldest child for the custom to point at him. That confuses two separate sequences: the naming custom counts **son-slots and daughter-slots separately**, so a daughter — real or not — cannot displace Richard from the eldest-*son* position.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000116 -->

## The patronage question

The strongest network evidence in this case does not attach to John. In October 1627 Brampton Gurdon wrote to John Winthrop about the widowed "mrs. Gurny," whose son's wardship was moving through Winthrop's own office at the Court of Wards; the Gurneys of West Barsham, in the decade John emigrated, stood inside the personal circle of the men who founded Massachusetts Bay.[^gurdon] That is a fact about the senior branch. Candidate B's John is two steps from it — a nephew of the ward line through his father Francis — and no record connects him to the network at all.[^wardship]

Whether John reached Massachusetts through that network, or simply out of the same county at the same time as thousands of others, is unresolved, and it matters: a documented patronage route would turn a plausible corridor into a mechanism. The test that would settle the chain is a patron-side search of the Norwich diocesan institution books for a 1627–28 presentation to the West Barsham vicarage made by right of the Gurney wardship. It cannot identify the emigrant; it can turn the Gurdon–Winthrop connection from strong construction into citable fact.[^cced]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000107 -->

## Anderson's "Boston"

Anderson's directory gives John's New England settlements as Boston and Braintree, and his origin as "Unknown."[^anderson] No Boston record names him. The Boston attribution is most plausibly the same conflation that produced the family tradition of an 1615 birth in Southwark: the 1636 runaway apprentice named John Gurney, a distinct and younger man, was bound to John Newgate the feltmaker and lived in Newgate's Boston household — a servant's position that would generate neither church admission nor property record.[^newgate] A short unrecorded stay at Boston by the elder John, before he surfaces at Weymouth in June 1641, remains possible: the town was under a day from Weymouth and traded with it constantly. But a transient lodging is invisible in principle, so the possibility can neither be tested nor used.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000108 -->

## Where the number can go

Three levers carry most of the remaining value, and they attack different rows.

**Images, not indexes.** The index layer on births and marriages is close to exhausted, and what is left is register images with specific predictions attached: Epping and North Weald Bassett at the Essex Record Office, where a line alternating John and Richard across three generations sits in the Great Migration's own county and the index cannot settle whether an earlier John exists; the Ackworth register at the West Yorkshire Archive Service, where the 1637 baptism names no parents and the marriage gives no marital status; and Stewkley 1615, where one burial entry decides A(1603).

**Sources never opened.** The south-Midlands archdeaconry marriage-licence series across three archdeaconries in two dioceses; the Suffolk baptism gap; the Norwich licence bonds of 1606–11; the Buckinghamshire wills.

**Y-DNA.** The Braintree-line kit against the untested English-line kit in the FamilyTreeDNA Gurney project. It bypasses records entirely and is the only lever that bears on the *no-record* row at all. A match would fix surname-level paternity; it would not by itself confirm any particular household.[^ydna]

A marriage record for John and Mary in the Rivett-orbit parishes around East Dereham, or a kinsman's mention of a Gurney "beyond the seas" in one of the unread Norfolk family wills of the 1650s, would lift Candidate B materially. Failing that, continued negative searching leaves the identification where it is: **open, with a leading named candidate at eleven percent and no candidate near identification.**
