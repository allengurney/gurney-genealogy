# The naming convention read forwards — and a John Gurney son of Richard at Cheddington

The absence of a son named Francis has always been carried as an argument *against* Candidate B. Read
the other way round, the same convention is an argument *for* something: it names the grandfather the
colonial John actually honoured. Following that produced a new candidate.

## The inference

Under the standard English naming custom of the period the first son takes the **paternal grandfather's**
name, the second son the **maternal grandfather's**, and the third the father's own.

The colonial John's children are conventionally given as Sarah, Mary, Richard, John Jr., Peter, and
Isaac. **Sarah is the weak link in that list**: she rests on compiled tradition and no primary record of
her has been produced. Set her aside and the pattern resolves cleanly:

- **Mary** — eldest daughter, named for her mother Mary. A common variant of the custom, which strictly
  assigns the first daughter to the mother's mother.
- **Richard** — **eldest son**, and therefore the paternal-grandfather slot.
- **John Jr.** — a later son carrying the father's own name, as the custom allows once the grandfather
  slots are filled.
- **Peter** — unexplained in the Gurney lines and most plausibly from the mother's kin, as the case file
  already reasons.

**If the custom is being followed at all, John Gurney's father was named Richard.**

This also disposes of the explanation currently offered for Richard. The case file suggests he honours
Richard Ryvett of Gressenhall — Margaret Rybett's brother or first cousin. But a *maternal* Ryvett would
sit in the second-son slot, and there is no elder son to occupy the first. The Ryvett reading requires
Sarah's brother to exist; the paternal reading requires only that Sarah does not.

**How much weight this carries.** Naming customs were followed loosely, and any single family may
depart from them. What makes this worth acting on is that the inference is *cheap to test* and the test
returned something. It is a search heuristic that paid, not a proof.

## What the search found

Searching the Buckinghamshire church records for a John Gurney baptised c.1595–1615 with a father named
Richard returns one candidate, and its household reconstructs completely.[^fs-bucks]

**Richard Gurney married Jane Founteine at Cheddington, Buckinghamshire, on 10 August 1606.** Their
children were baptised there:

| Baptism | Child | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 8 March 1606/7 | Thomas | |
| **5 August 1608** | **Johannes (John)** | mother given as Jana in the index |
| 4 February 1609/10 | Fulke | |
| 20 August 1612 | Richard | |

A **Katherine Gurney married Edward Reeve at Cheddington on 9 February 1628**, plausibly a daughter of
the same household. **Richard Gurney of Cheddington left probate in 1626** — British Record Society
vol. 114, `Wf 26 100` — so the father died when this John was about eighteen.[^brs]

**John never appears in the Cheddington record again after his 1608 baptism.** The parish's later Gurney
entries belong to a different household — Isaac Gurney and Martha, children Jane 1636 and Martha 1641 —
and then nothing until 1706.

## How it tests against the profile

**In favour:**

- **The naming fit is exact and was predicted before the search, not after it.** Father Richard, eldest
  son Richard.
- **The birth year sits inside the window the case file derives from other constraints.** August 1608
  falls in the 1606–1608 sliver that Francis Gurney's apprenticeship forces on Candidate B — reached
  here by a completely independent route.
- **The father's death in 1626 removes the tie.** A younger son of a dead husbandman, aged eighteen,
  with two brothers, is exactly the profile that emigrates.
- **He is unaccounted for after 1608** — no marriage, no burial, no Prerogative Court will, and no
  Buckinghamshire archdeaconry probate, which is now a searched negative rather than an unsearched
  gap.[^brs]
- **The county has a documented Gurney tailoring trade** across four parishes 1571–1633, and the
  colonial John was a tailor.
- **A second naming signal, weaker than the first and worth stating carefully.** The colonial John's
  youngest son was **Isaac** — a name absent from every Norfolk Gurney household surveyed, which run to
  Edward, Marye, Agnes and Francis. Buckinghamshire Gurneys do use it, and one instance is
  contemporary: **Isaac Gurney and Martha were baptising children at Cheddington itself in 1636 and
  1641**, so the name was in that parish's Gurney pool in the emigrant's own generation. He is not among
  Richard and Jane's four indexed children, so he is a separate Cheddington household or married in
  rather than a demonstrated brother. A John Gurney and Mary also baptised a son Isaac at Cublington in
  **1664**, but that is a generation after the colonial naming and belongs to an already-eliminated
  household documented in England 1664–1672; it shows the combination recurs in the county, not that the
  name was available to the emigrant. **The Cheddington instance carries this point; the Cublington one
  does not.**

**Against, and these are real:**

- **The age fit is the weakest part.** Born August 1608, he would be **45** in 1653, deposing "aged 50 or
  thereabouts". That is a five-year stretch — tolerable under age-heaping, and no worse than Candidate B
  at the same date, but it is a stretch and it should not be smoothed over.
- **No wife Mary, and no marriage anywhere.** The Buckinghamshire and Hertfordshire index pass found no
  John Gurney and Mary household in either county before 1660. If this man is the emigrant he married
  outside the county or outside the indexed record — the same unevidenced step Candidate B requires.
- **No child set.** Nothing places him fathering children in England 1628–1635, the years the colonial
  John demonstrably was. This is the standing objection to every Buckinghamshire survivor and it applies
  here in full.
- **One baptism is one record.** A single indexed event with a matching father's forename is a lead, not
  a candidate profile. Cheddington is fifteen miles from Mursley, where a Richard Gurney tailor died in
  1631, and the county held five to seven simultaneous John Gurneys — coincidence of forenames in that
  environment is cheap.

## Where this leaves it

This is **a new named candidate at roughly the level of the other Buckinghamshire survivors**, arrived
at by a different and better route: a prediction from the naming custom that was then tested, rather
than a name-match noticed after the fact. It does not displace Candidate B, which still has the
circumstantial web, the verified marriage, and the documented emigration motive that this household
entirely lacks.

What would move it, in order of decisiveness:

1. **Richard Gurney of Cheddington's 1626 will** (Bucks Archives, `Wf 26 100`). A will naming children
   would confirm or destroy the reconstruction outright — and if it names a son John living in 1626,
   that John is the man. **This is now the single highest-value document in the Buckinghamshire file.**
2. **A Cheddington burial register search** for John Gurney, any date. His burial there closes the
   candidate immediately.
3. **A marriage of John Gurney to a Mary c.1626–1635** anywhere in the south Midlands.

## A note on Buckinghamshire apprenticeship records

The trade question cannot be worked the way it was worked for London and Norwich, and the reason is
structural rather than a gap in searching. **Buckinghamshire had no incorporated livery companies**, so
there is no county equivalent of the Merchant Taylors' binding and freedom books or the Norwich freemen
and enrolled-apprentice registers. The national apprenticeship registers — the Stamp Duty series, TNA
IR 1 — begin in **1710**, about eighty years after the relevant bindings, and FindMyPast's country
apprentice set is drawn from them. Pre-1710 Buckinghamshire apprenticeship evidence survives only in
borough court and freemen books for Buckingham, Chipping Wycombe and Aylesbury, in Quarter Sessions
pauper-apprenticeship indentures, and incidentally in wills — none of it name-indexed online.

So the honest position is that a Buckinghamshire apprenticeship search has not been run because there is
no online instrument to run it against, and that this is a *different kind of blank* from the London and
Norwich negatives, which were searches against complete registers. The nearest reachable substitute is
the wills: a husbandman's will often places a son with a master. That is another reason the 1626
Cheddington will is the document to get.
