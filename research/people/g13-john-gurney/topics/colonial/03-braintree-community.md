# Colonial Braintree community

**John Gurney lived at Braintree, Massachusetts, from about 1645 until his death in 1662/3 — his settled colonial home after leaving Weymouth.** His Braintree years are documented by the 1645 plantation petition, an imaged 1646 petition he signed alongside the town's founding cohort, a 1652/3 court deposition that names him "of Brayntree," a fifteen-year tenancy of William Tyng's farm (the future Adams family seat), his own Monatiquot-River freehold, and repeated service as a witness and appraiser for his neighbours. His principal Braintree landholding was leasehold, not freehold; the freehold he owned outright was a separate, smaller homestead he sold the year he died.

## Residence and the move from Weymouth

John's Weymouth parcels had already passed to other men by the 1643 property record, and he was established at Braintree by 1645, so the move falls c. 1642–1645; the earlier Weymouth phase is set out in [01-arrival-chronology.md](01-arrival-chronology.md). The firmest attestation that Braintree became his home is a Suffolk Court Files deposition of 17 March 1652/3 in *Wilson v. Faxon*, which describes "John Gurney of Brayntree aged 50 Yeares or thereabouts."[^nehgr-62-94] (The stated age bears on his English birth year and is weighed in the origin/age-baptism work, not here.)<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000001 -->

## The 1645 plantation petition

In May 1645 John signed the inhabitants' petition for the new plantation at Braintree (Mount Wollaston) — a marker of established standing in the town.[^braintree-petition] The signature is presently known through the secondary *History of Weymouth* report; the primary petition page has not yet been located, so the datum is carried at moderate confidence pending that pull.[^braintree-petition]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000002 -->

## The 1646 meadows petition

A year later John's name appears on a second Braintree petition, and this one survives as an imaged primary record. In 1646 he was among the inhabitants who petitioned the General Court at Boston against Thomas Gilbert — a tenant of a small upland patch who was invoking an "ancient order of Court" to graze "a great heard of cattle" across the petitioners' meadow lots, some hundred acres lying round about Gilbert's eight or ten acres of upland; the magistrates answered over Governor John Winthrop's endorsement, sending the petitioners back to an earlier order of 1643.[^arch45-petition] John signs shoulder to shoulder with the town's founding cohort — Martin Sanders (the lead signer), Peter and Richard Brackett, and Henry Adams, immigrant progenitor of the presidential family whose descendants would later hold the very Tyng farm John leased — among Samuel Bass, Samuel Adams, Benjamin Albee, John Dassett, James Penniman, and Thomas Meakins.[^arch45-petition] The signatures fix John in a documented 1646 act of town business, seventeen years before his death, beside three men — Peter Brackett, Richard Brackett, and John Dassett — who reappear as creditors in his own 1663 estate accounts.[^arch45-petition][^spr-338]

This meadows petition is a different document from the May 1645 plantation petition above, and the two should not be run together: the 1645 petition is still known only from the secondary *History of Weymouth* and remains unlocated in the primary record, while the 1646 meadows petition survives imaged at Massachusetts Archives volume 45.[^arch45-petition][^braintree-petition]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000112 -->

## The leased Tyng farm — the future Adams seat

In 1647 the Boston merchant William Tyng leased John a forty-five-acre Braintree farm for ten years, and John continued to lease it from Tyng's daughters Bethia and Mercy until 1662, so the tenancy ran continuously for about fifteen years.[^nps-clr] Tyng himself was a non-resident owner — he had taken up the Mount Wollaston lands as a Boston merchant-proprietor in 1639, among the large absentee allotments that shaped early Braintree landholding — which is why the working farm was leased to a resident like John rather than owned by him.[^adams-braintree][^adams-genesis] The same farm appears in the 1653 Tyng probate inventory — "48 Akers land at Braintree and Marsh in possession of John Gurney" (forty-five acres in the National Park Service / Ezekiel Sargent reading) — and again in the 28 March 1661 Tyng estate-division indenture, which names John as the sitting tenant.[^nehgr-30-432][^suffolk-deeds] The 1653 inventory and the 1661 indenture are two snapshots of one lease, not two separate tenancies. The northern portion of "the Gurney farm" later became the Adams family seat — the Old House (Peace field) in Quincy, acquired by the Adamses in 1787, beside the birthplaces of Presidents John Adams and John Quincy Adams.[^nps-clr]

This is leasehold, not ownership: John's principal Braintree land was a tenancy, and the farm was distinct both from the larger roughly five-hundred-acre Tyng holding (Salter's Farm / Mount Wollaston, leased to John Read and then Gregory Belcher) and from John's own Monatiquot freehold below.[^nps-clr][^suffolk-deeds]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000003 -->

## The Monatiquot freehold

John did own a Braintree homestead outright. On 12 February 1661/2 "John Gurney, tailor" conveyed to Richard Thayer, for fourteen pounds, a house and orchard, a five-acre parcel on the Monatiquot River, and an adjoining half-acre; possession was given on 16 April 1662, and the deed — signed by John's mark — was witnessed by Peter Brackett and John Rockwell.[^bates][^suffolk-deeds] The conveyance is the clearest single attestation of his trade ("tailor"), of a Braintree freehold he held in his own right, and of his close associates. The Suffolk registry copy of this same deed, recovered through a FamilySearch full-text search, independently carries the styling "John Gurney of Braintry Taylor" — a second manuscript-registry witness to the trade, alongside Bates's printed transcription and the printed *Suffolk Deeds Liber IV*.[^fts-deed] His final-years landholding therefore had two distinct parts: the leased Tyng farm and this separate Monatiquot homestead — the "Gurney farm" of later local memory and his personal freehold are not the same parcel.<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000004 -->

## Trade and livelihood

John's trade was tailoring — he is styled "John Gurney, tailor" in his own 1661/2 Monatiquot deed, and Sprague's compiled Braintree genealogy likewise enters him as a tailor.[^bates][^sprague] Yet the working economy of his last years was a husbandman's: his March 1663 estate inventory held not one tool of the tailoring trade, and its value lay in livestock, plough-gear, and grain — the material record read in full in [05-material-life.md](05-material-life.md). His occupation is best read as a trained trade overtaken in practice by mixed farming: a tailor by name, a husbandman by the measure of his goods.[^sprague]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000029 -->

## Standing in the community

John was repeatedly trusted with his neighbours' affairs. In 1659 he witnessed Gregory Baxter's Braintree will (with Moses Payne and Richard Brackett) and helped take the estate inventory on 7 July 1659.[^anderson-baxter] Together with the 1645 petition signature and the dense creditor network mapped by his own 1663 estate accounts — debts to Peter Brackett, Joseph Adams, and other Braintree men — the record shows a tradesman of modest means who nonetheless held recognised standing in the town.[^spr-338][^nehgr-12] Peter Brackett recurs throughout: he witnessed the 1661/2 Thayer deed, was a creditor of the 1663 estate, and was among the Braintree men tied to the later Mendon purchase.[^spr-338][^nehgr-12]<!-- graph-marker: G13-PM-000005 -->
