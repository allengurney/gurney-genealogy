# The Gurney family of Cawston and Booton, Norfolk (15th century–1750s)

Gurneys appear at Cawston and neighbouring Booton across three centuries — an **armigerous gentry Gurney** there in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries (a Booton deed of c. 1481 and a 1578 Cawston monument bearing the West Barsham arms differenced for a cadet), followed by a **copyhold Gurney family** documented through the seventeenth-century manorial courts and into the eighteenth. Whether the later copyhold family descends from the earlier armigerous one is an open question, but the continuity of the surname in one small pair of parishes across so long a span makes Cawston/Booton one of the more persistent non-West-Barsham Gurney reservoirs in Norfolk.

## The earlier armigerous Gurney at Cawston and Booton (15th–16th c.)

**A William Gurnay of Booton stood among the parties to a Cawston property grant c. 1481.** Ancient Deed B.991 records a "Grant by Robert Mallard [and others], citizens of Norwich, to Robert Skynner of Causton, **William Gurnay of Boton** [Booton], and Thomas Halys of Causton, of a messuage at Kyrkgate … in Causton," dated 21 Edward IV (1481).[^booton-b991] Booton adjoins Cawston immediately to the south; the deed places a Gurnay of enough standing to act as a Norwich-citizen grantee's transferee in the Cawston–Booton parishes a full century before the earliest copyhold-family courts below.

**A gentry Gurney was buried under arms at Cawston in 1578.** Blomefield transcribes a monument in Cawston church: "Here lieth the Body of **William Gurney, Gent.** March 10 1578, and **Anne his Wife** 19 January 1596, they had 1 Son and 3 Daughters," accompanied by two coats — "**Gurney impaling Waytes** of Norfolk, az. a fess or between three fishes naiant arg." and "**Gurnay, arg. a cross ingrailed gul. with a crescent** impaling Wayte."[^cawston-monument-1578] The heraldry is the decisive detail: *argent, a cross engrailed gules* is the West Barsham Gurney coat (the direct-line arms Burke gives as "Argent, a cross engrailed gules"), and the **crescent is the mark of cadency for a second son**. William Gurney, gent., of Cawston, married to Anne Wayte, was therefore an armigerous **cadet of the West Barsham line** — a recognized junior branch seated at Cawston in the mid-sixteenth century, not an unconnected same-name townsman.

Taken together the 1481 Booton grant and the 1578 armigerous burial argue for a Gurney gentry presence at Cawston/Booton spanning the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, plausibly a single cadet line, and a natural reservoir out of which the seventeenth-century copyhold family below (and later "unplaced" Norfolk Gurneys) could have descended by the ordinary downward drift of a cadet branch into the yeomanry.<!-- discovery trail: lead L-198; round-5 dump §2.7 / §3.6.3 --> The descent from William the cadet (d. 1578) to William-and-Ann of the 1648 courts is not documented and is carried as an open question, not a proven line.

## The copyhold family, 1640s–1750s

A sustained Gurney family appears throughout the digitised Cawston manorial records 1641–1685 (with recitals running into the eighteenth century): **William and Ann Gurney** in the 1648–1650 courts, **Emme Gurney** 1655–1695, **Agnes Gurney** 1659–1664, a Gurney death presented c. 1640–1664, an **Anne Gurney** in 1729, and "land late of the said Ann Gurney" recited in 1757. Cawston is 11 miles north of Norwich — a different orbit from both the West Barsham gentry line and the East Dereham corridor.

## Reading

A copyhold family at Cawston across at least three generations: William & Ann (1648–50, with William's land in abuttal recitals by 1649/57 "land late of … Gurney"), the long widow-or-heiress tenures of Emme (1655–1695) and Agnes (1659–64, the latter in a "Libertate" entry worth reading), death presentments in the 1640s–60s, and an Anne Gurney still in the rolls in 1729 with her land recited in 1757. The 19th-century Hugh Gurney Barclay cards are the Norwich banking family as lords/mortgagees, not the local line.

**Case relevance.** Comparator only at present — no John in the visible cards, and no link to the emigrant's window. But a documented mid-17th-century Gurney family 11 miles north of Norwich thickens the map of plebeian Norfolk Gurney households against which any origin hypothesis must discriminate, and the 1640s–60s death presentments are exactly the kind of entries that name relicts and heirs.
