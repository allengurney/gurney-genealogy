## Why this place matters historically

Saxthorpe is not a primary family seat in the way that West Barsham, Harpley, or Hardingham are. Its importance is narrower and more local. It preserves a **named holding — Loundhall — within the wider later-medieval Norfolk estate world of the family**. That makes it a useful place file precisely because it captures one of the smaller but still meaningful local properties that surrounded the larger manorial centres. [Edmund G23 companion]

In other words, Saxthorpe helps show the family not only as holders of a few famous manors, but as participants in a broader mosaic of Norfolk property, exchanges, and local estate management. [Edmund G23 companion]

## The 1472 manor-court confrontation — the family's attempt to reclaim Saxthorpe

Saxthorpe is also the setting of the single most concrete contemporary episode in the whole pre-1500 Gurney record. Saxthorpe had been one of the old Heylesdon–Gurney holdings; after Sir John Gurney V's death in 1408 his widow Alice Heylesdon sold it to John Wynter, and it passed into the Fastolf estates. In 1471, within months of inheriting the West Barsham seat, **William Gurney IV (G19)** moved to reassert Gurney lordship over the manor — bringing the Pastons, who claimed it through the Fastolf inheritance, into direct conflict with him.

In January 1472 William Gurnay entered Saxthorpe and began keeping a manor court, having the tenants attorn to him as lord. John Paston III walked in with a single man, charged the tenants to proceed no further, and when the court resumed sat down beside the steward and **blotted his book with his finger** as he wrote, calling the tenants to witness that the court had been interrupted in Sir John Paston's right. William tried again on Holy Rood Day (3 May) 1472: **Henry Heydon** — son of John Heydon of Baconsthorpe, the Gurney family ally — had "reysyd as many men as he kowd mak in barneys" (raised as many men-at-arms as he could muster) to support Gurnay, but John Paston defused the second court by quiet persuasion and the men were sent home. Paston reported that "my Lord of Norffolks men wyll be with hym ayenst me," and proposed that **Roger Townshend** arbitrate. Within weeks Henry Heydon bought Saxthorpe and Titchwell outright from Bishop Waynflete of Winchester, sidelining both families; Margaret Paston wrote on 5 June 1472, "We beat the bushes, and have the loss and the disworship, and other men have the birds."[^saxthorpe-1472-paston]

The episode fixes Saxthorpe as more than a passive property record: it is where the working Gurney–Heydon military alliance is caught in the field (Heydon's men mustered for Gurnay), and where the Gurney–Heydon–Townshend triangle first appears — the same Heydon and Townshend in whose estates William Gurney IV would sit as a trustee two decades later. The fuller treatment, with the underlying letters, is on the [G19 William Gurney IV companion](../people/g19-william-gurney-iv-fact-sheet.research.md).
