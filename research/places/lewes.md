# Lewes, Sussex

Town in East Sussex, England. Coordinates: **50.874, 0.009**.

Site of the **Battle of Lewes, 14 May 1264** — the decisive engagement of the Second Barons' War, in which the rebel barons under Simon de Montfort defeated King Henry III and captured the king and his son Prince Edward (the future Edward I). **Sir John de Gournay I (G27)** fought on the rebel side at Lewes — an event that defines his career and classifies him as a Montfortian baron at the critical political moment of his generation.

## The 1264 context

The Battle of Lewes was the defining event of Montfort's brief ascendancy. Rebels who fought at Lewes and survived the subsequent defeat at Evesham (4 August 1265) typically lost lands by the Dictum of Kenilworth or other royal sanctions; only a handful — those who reconciled quickly with the Crown — retained full estates.

Sir John I's loss was limited: he **forfeited only South Wootton manor, Norfolk**, retaining Harpley and the other principal holdings. This relative leniency is consistent with his rapid reconciliation — by 1270 he was accompanying the now-reconciled Prince Edward on **Crusade**, with letters of protection issued under Rot. Patent 54 Hen. III, m.15 d. (transcribed in DG Supplement pp. 785–786).

## The Gournay engrailed-cross arms

The Crusade journey (1270) is the traditional origin of the Gurney family coat of arms — the engrailed cross — though Daniel Gurney discusses multiple alternative origins (Ufford influence, Baconsthorpe coheir marriage). See DG-I pp. 339–341 and DG Supplement pp. 785–786 for the full discussion. The earliest surviving physical impression of the arms is on William III's (G26) 1294 conveyance deed.
