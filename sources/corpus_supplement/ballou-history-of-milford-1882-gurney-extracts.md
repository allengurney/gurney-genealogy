## Summary and explanation

This Milford history is more expansive than the short Mendon proprietors’ references because Ballou pauses to narrate the **institutional creation of the plantation itself**. He is not merely listing proprietors or abutters; he is explaining how the Braintree/Weymouth project moved from petition, to General Court approval, to commissioner-made settlement rules, to Indian purchase, and then to incorporation.

Within the requested page range, the critical John Gurney point appears on **PDF page 30**. There he is named among the persons **“accepted to Allottments”** at the plantation granted at Netmooke in 1662, and Ballou explicitly places him in the **Braintree** contingent rather than the Weymouth contingent. That is valuable because it situates John Gurney among the recognized early participants before the plantation had yet become an incorporated town.

The surrounding pages matter because they show what that acceptance meant. The commissioners were not simply listing casual associates. They were establishing the rules for division of land, public charges, local management, ministerial settlement, and the deadline by which accepted allottees were to be **settled at the plantation with their persons and estates**. Read in context, John Gurney’s appearance is evidence of intended settlement status and recognized proprietary standing in the formative stage of Mendon.

The Indian deed on PDF pages **31–32** then shows the legal foundation of the tract itself: the eight miles square bought by Moses Payne and Peter Brackett, later assigned to the town. This does not name John Gurney individually, but it is the documentary land-base under which his accepted allotment would have operated.

The immediately following pages, though outside your requested span, are especially useful. On **PDF page 34**, John Gurney appears again among those sharing in the **1667 division of the meadows**. On **PDF page 35**, he appears yet again in the list of persons to whom the town had granted land **before King Philip’s War**. Those two supplementary references reinforce the implication of the 1662 allotment list: John Gurney was not a fleeting or merely nominal name in the project; he remained among the recognized grantees of the early Mendon plantation.

## Research takeaway

Taken together, this Milford account strengthens the case that John Gurney belonged to the **founding / first-generation proprietary body of Mendon**, beginning with accepted allotment status in 1662 and continuing into the town’s actual land distributions and pre-war grant structure. It is therefore a stronger narrative source than a bare index or isolated land-list entry, because it embeds his name in the sequence of official acts that brought the plantation into being.
