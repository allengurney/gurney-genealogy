# Weymouth land system — Charles Francis Adams, "The Genesis of the Massachusetts Town" (1892)

Charles Francis Adams, "The Genesis of the Massachusetts Town, and the Development of Town-Meeting Government," *Proceedings of the Massachusetts Historical Society*, 2nd series, vol. 7 (1891–1892), pp. 172–263; the Weymouth treatment is at printed pp. 190–192. Internet Archive item `proceedingsofmass2v7mass`. Source ID: `adams-genesis-massachusetts-town-1892` (the companion *History of Braintree* passage below is `adams-history-of-braintree-1891`). No Gurney is named in the paper; it is general context for the Weymouth land-grant and proprietorship system within which John Gurney's parcels sit.

## The Weymouth property records are undated (printed p. 190)

> The formal record of Weymouth does not begin until December, 1641, although there are a very few items, evidently of an earlier date, which are undoubtedly transcribed from other sources by a later hand, and a mass of property records which are undated, but which, from external and internal evidence, should appear a year or two later.

> Unlike Dedham, therefore, Weymouth was not settled as a plantation by a definite body of proprietors, nor did the town organize itself as a business company.

Adams's note adds that "the Weymouth town records have been copied, but never printed. The earlier church records have been lost" (citing Nash, *Sketch of Weymouth*, p. 8). The implication is that the conventional "1643" date attached to the Weymouth property list is an external/internal inference, not a date recorded with the grants.

## The 6 April 1646 order restricting non-inhabitant landholding (printed p. 191)

A vote prepared by the Weymouth townsmen, dated 6 April 1646 and approved by the whole town:

> Whereas we find by sad experience the great inconvenience that many times it comes to pass by the permitting of strangers to come into the plantation pretending only to sojourne for a season, but afterwards they have continued awhile account themselves inhabitants with us, and so challeng to themselves all such priveledges and immunitys as others doe enjoy ... we the townsmen ... have thought good to present ... the insuing order ...
>
> First that no inhabitant within this plantation shall presume to take into his house as an inmate, or servant, any person or persons, unless he shall give sufficient bonds ... Also it is further agreed ... that no person or persons within this plantation shall lett or sell any house, or land, to any person or persons that is not an inhabitant amongst us, untill he hath first made a tender of it to the Towne, at a trayning or some lecture day or other public meeting.

## Non-resident proprietors held rights but were excluded from inhabitant privileges (printed pp. 245–46 region)

> Of freemen, there were, indeed, some who were non-resident; but, so far from being reckoned inhabitants, these were expressly excluded from the basis of population upon which the number of deputies from a given town was estimated ... These non-resident freemen proprietors were frequently of controlling influence, — indeed, not rarely they were the original joint purchasers of the township, yet, not having their residence with the actual settlers, they were not entitled to certain exclusive privileges granted to inhabitants, and eventually were deprived of all political control.

## Companion volume — non-resident ownership at Mount Wollaston

Charles Francis Adams, *History of Braintree, Massachusetts (1639–1708)* (Cambridge: Riverside Press, 1891), p. 5, on the early Mount Wollaston allotments:

> even subsequently to 1637, large allotments, indicative of a non-resident ownership, appear to have been made at "the Mount."

The same volume (p. 109) records that William Tyng, the Boston merchant, bought the greater part of the Mount Wollaston allotment in 1639 from Coddington, and that the farm passed by inheritance in 1661 to Tyng's daughter (wife of Thomas Shepard), thence to the Quincy and Adams families. No Gurney is named in the volume, which treats the North Precinct / Quincy; John Gurney's own Braintree freehold lay in the South Precinct on the Monatiquot.
