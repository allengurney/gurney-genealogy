# Ralph de Diceto on the capture of Hugh de Gournay, 1173

**Source:** William Stubbs, ed., *Radulfi de Diceto Decani Lundoniensis Opera Historica: The Historical Works of Master Ralph de Diceto, Dean of London*, vol. 1, Rolls Series 68 (London: Longman & Co., 1876), pp. 369, 371, 373-374.  
**Intake file:** `sources/intake/new/pdfs/Rerum_Britannicarum_Medii_aevi_scriptore.pdf`  
**External reference:** <https://www.google.com/books/edition/Rerum_Britannicarum_Medii_aevi_scriptore/NxdnAAAAMAAJ>  
**Proposed source ID:** `diceto-opera-historica-vol1-stubbs-1876`

## Research value

This passage gives a compact independent chronicle notice for the 1173 rebellion against Henry II: both father and son named Hugh de Gournay were captured by the Young King, with eighty knights, after being taken by stratagem rather than battlefield force. It is useful because it explicitly distinguishes the elder and younger Hugh in the same event and because Stubbs records a manuscript spelling variant, `Gurnai`, under the printed `Gornai` form.

The passage should be treated as chronicle evidence, not as a charter record. It supports chronology and political alignment, while the fuller documentary work still belongs with charter and act evidence from the French calendar and other primary-source editions.

## Extract

### p. 369, A.D. 1173

> Hugo de Gornai, tam pater quam filius, et quater xx. milites cum eis, non tam inimicorum virtute quam insidiis intercepti, capti sunt a rege filio. Castrum succensum est; burgenses ad redemptionem compulsi.

Stubbs footnote: `Gornai] Gurnai, D.`

### Working translation

Hugh de Gournay, both father and son, and eighty knights with them, were intercepted not so much by the strength of their enemies as by ambush or stratagem, and were captured by the king's son. The castle was burned, and the townsmen were compelled to ransom.

## Immediate chronicle context

Diceto places the notice inside the 1173 conflict between Henry II and the Young King. Nearby entries describe the wider rebellion, including the adherence of Robert earl of Leicester and William the chamberlain of Tancarville to the Young King's side, the participation of other Norman and cross-Channel nobles, and the involvement of the counts of Flanders and Boulogne. The following pages also describe military pressure in Normandy, including Aumale and Driencourt, the count of Boulogne's mortal wound, the count of Flanders withdrawing by way of Eu, Henry II at Rouen, and the gathering of forces toward Verneuil.

## Observations

- The phrase `tam pater quam filius` is the key genealogical value: Diceto does not merely name one Hugh de Gournay, but explicitly marks father and son.
- The manuscript variant `Gurnai` belongs in future searches alongside `Gornai`, `Gornaco`, `Gornaio`, `Gournayo`, and `Gurnay`.
- The text describes capture by the Young King, not by Henry II. Any later summary should preserve that direction of capture.
- The burned castle is unnamed in the short notice and should not be identified without support from another source.
- The source is a printed scholarly edition of a medieval chronicle. It is suitable as supporting evidence but should be paired with charter evidence for property, kinship, and office claims.
