# John Gurney in *The Bartletts of Weymouth*

**Full book citation:** Bartlett, Thomas Edward. *The Bartletts: Ancestral, Genealogical, Biographical, Historical: Comprising an Account of the American Progenitors of the Bartlett Family, with Special Reference to the Descendants of John Bartlett, of Weymouth and Cumberland.* New Haven, Conn.: Press of the Stafford Printing Co., 1892. Reprint, Provo, Utah: J. Grant Stevenson, 1973.

**Filename:** TN-244870 The Bartletts of Weymouth

## Full text of the applicable John Gurney section

**Printed pp. 14–15**

> The original grant for Mendon was for eight miles square, but the grantees took possession of nearly double that extent of territory. At a General Court holden in Boston, October 16, 1660: “in further answer to Braintry's petition, the Court declare they judge meete and proper to grant a plantation of eight miles square and that the persons named have liberty to enter upon and make a beginning thereat.”
>
> This tract was purchased from the Indians for “twenty-four pound sterling,” and a deed was given “Anawassanauk alias John Quashoamait alias William, of Blue Hills; Great John Namsconont alias Peter, and Upannbohqueen alias Jacob of Natic,” to Moses Payne and Peter Brackett, “both of Brantre.” The deed was dated April 22, 1662, and witnessed by John Elliot, Sr., John Elliot, Jr., and Daniel Weld, Sr. At the time of the above grant the Court ordered “that Major Atherton, Lieut. Roger Clapp, Elezur Lusher and Deacon Parke, or any three of them, shall be and are hereby empowered to make a valid act there.” These were denominated the Committee of Nipmug, which was the original name of the town.
>
> This was the Country of the Nipmug (sometimes called Nipnet and Nipmuck), Indians whose headquarters was said to have been at what is now Oxford, Mass. October 30, 1663, this committee ordered that all persons who had accepted allotments of land in the plantation should remove there with their families by the middle of November, 1664, “upon penaltie of forfeiture of all the grants there.” The first settlers from Braintree were, John Moore, George Aldrich, Daniel Lovett, Josiah Chapin, Ferdinando Thayer, John Scammell, Nathaniel Haseman, Alexander Plumley, Matthias Puffer, John Woodland, John Hasber, Joseph Penniman and **John Gurney**. Those from Weymouth were, Goodman King, Sr., Walter Cook, William Holbrook, Joseph White, Goodman Thomson, Goodman John Raynes, Goodman Bolter, Sr., Abraham Staples, Samuel Pratt and Thomas Bolter, all of which had land allotted them prior to their removal to the town.

## Researcher’s explanation

This passage is not a biographical sketch of John Gurney. It is a contextual settlement passage inside a Bartlett genealogy, and John Gurney appears only as one name in a list of the “first settlers from Braintree” associated with the Mendon/Nipmug plantation.

The passage is useful because it places John Gurney within a specifically **Braintree-based** group tied to the inland plantation that became Mendon. That fits your background understanding that John was in Massachusetts by 1641, later lived at Braintree, and remained there until his death in 1662 or early 1663. In other words, this source is consistent with John Gurney being part of the Braintree community involved in the Mendon venture.

At the same time, the chronology requires caution. The passage first references the 1660 court action on “Braintry's petition,” then the 1662 Indian deed, and then an order of **30 October 1663** directing persons who had accepted allotments to remove with their families by November 1664. If your working chronology is correct that John Gurney died in 1662 or early 1663, then this passage should probably **not** be read literally as proof that he personally removed to Mendon after the October 1663 order. A more careful reading is that he belonged to the Braintree group connected with the grant and settlement enterprise—possibly as an original proprietor, intended settler, or allottee—before his death.

That distinction matters genealogically. The wording “The first settlers from Braintree were … John Gurney” is helpful, but this book is a **late secondary compilation** rather than a contemporaneous town record. In the opening pages the author states that he compiled from town records, deeds, wills, histories, biographies, and genealogies, but in this specific passage he does not identify the exact primary record from which each listed name was drawn. As evidence, it is therefore best used as a **supporting secondary source**, not as stand-alone proof that John Gurney physically settled at Mendon.

The strongest genealogical value of the passage is narrower and more defensible:

1. It independently associates **John Gurney with Braintree**.
2. It associates him with the **Braintree petition / Mendon plantation movement**.
3. It suggests he was regarded by a 19th-century compiler as one of the men whose name belonged in the earliest Braintree cohort connected with that settlement.

So, for research purposes, this source is best cited as evidence that John Gurney was remembered in local-genealogical tradition as a **Braintree man linked to the early Mendon grant**, while the question of whether he actually removed there before death remains unresolved and should be tested against primary records.

## Suggested research use

A careful citation note for your case file could characterize the passage this way:

> Thomas Edward Bartlett, in his history of the Bartlett family of Weymouth, Mendon, and Cumberland, listed John Gurney among the “first settlers from Braintree” connected with the early Mendon plantation. Because the passage is a late compiled secondary account and appears in connection with events extending into late 1663, it is best read as evidence of Gurney’s association with the Braintree-to-Mendon venture, not as conclusive proof that he personally removed to Mendon before his death.
