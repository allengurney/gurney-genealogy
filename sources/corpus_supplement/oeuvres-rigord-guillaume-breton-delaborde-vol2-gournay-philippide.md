# Guillaume le Breton, *Philippide*, book VI (Delaborde ed., vol. 2) — the fall of Gournay, 1202

Source: Henri-François Delaborde, ed., *Œuvres de Rigord et de Guillaume le Breton, historiens de Philippe-Auguste*, vol. 2 (Société de l'histoire de France; Paris: Renouard, 1885). Internet Archive identifier: `uvresderigordetd02rigouoft`. Source ID: `oeuvres-rigord-guillaume-breton-delaborde`.

The Gournay set-piece stands in book VI of the *Philippide* (printed pp. 160-162; verse lines 210-262 of the book). Guillaume le Breton describes the town's triple-walled, moated defences, names Hugh de Gournay as its commander, and recounts Philip Augustus taking it in 1202 by having the earthen dam of a great pond above the town broken so that the released flood overwhelmed the walls. Rigord's prose chronicle §138 and the *Chronicon* c. 112-113 parallel the account. OCR from the Internet Archive text is retained; printed marginal verse-numbers ("2i10" = 210, etc.) survive as scan noise.

Working translation (selective): *"Not far from here [Philip's campaign] a town proud with its teeming people, full of diverse riches and famed by report, set on level open country, fortified with a **triple wall** — a most delightful and handsome valley held it, Gournay by name, impregnable by the ground of its site even had no defender been within; over which, and many other places, **Hugh de Gournay presided**. Its ditches were exceedingly broad and deep, which the [river] Epte had so filled with its stream that no approach to the walls could be had through them; yet the king undid it by such a stratagem. Not far from the walls a very great pond swelled, whose water — banked like a standing sea — an earthen dam, packed with squared stones and much turf, forced to stand in its winding lake. This the king has broken through the middle; thence pours an immense deluge, and in a sudden chasm the whole valley turns to the likeness of the sea… The town, mighty in arms, walled and manned, fearing capture by no art or force, is taken by an unlooked-for flood; the watery assaults throw down the ramparts, a brief wave overturns the citadel that feared no siege-engines or weapons. When the king had thus brought Gournay under his own rule, recalling all the inhabitants to their homes, [he gave] peace…"*

The verse "munitum triplice muro … inexpugnabile" is the passage Daniel Gurney cited (as *Record* Part I, p. 24, "munitum triplice muro … inexpugnabilis," locating it at "Lib. xi"); in Delaborde's edition it stands in **book VI**, not book XI.
