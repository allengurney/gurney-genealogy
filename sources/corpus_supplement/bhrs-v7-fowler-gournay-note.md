Herbert Fowler's apparatus to the Bedfordshire pipe-roll/barony material; the account of the lords of Houghton Regis). Full text: `sources/corpus/bhrs-v7-1926-fulltext.txt` (archive.org `publicationsofbe07bedf`). This is the "Beds. Hist. Rec. Soc., vii, pp. 153-7" account Clay cites in EYC viii p. 6 n. 8 as a standard modern treatment of the family. Extracted 2026-07-14.

## Scope and generational scheme

Fowler traces the senior baronial line only, as holders of Houghton Regis and Wendover: Hugh I (retired to Bec c. 1082; wife Basile; St. Anselm's letter to Basile noted) → Gerard ("His wife was Edith daughter of William de Warenne Earl of Surrey"; crusader 1096, "presumably died abroad, either on Crusade or on a later pilgrimage") → Hugh II (minor at his father's death, lands administered by step-father Dreux de Monceux; brought up by Henry I; 1118 revolt; sister Gundred betrothed to Nigel de Albini) → Hugh III (Fowler's own split: the lord marcher of 1135, leader of the Norman walk-out from Stephen's camp 1137, "hot-brained young seigneur," d. c. 1178) → Hugh IV (relief 1179, third crusade at Acre, lost Gournay to Philip Augustus 1202-4, pardoned 1206, sheriff of Bucks and Beds 1214, homage of "Gerald his heir and first-born" accepted 1214, died in Poitou; m. Millicent daughter of Thomas de Maria [recte de Coucy tradition]).

Note Fowler's numbering runs one behind Daniel Gurney's for the later lords (Fowler's Hugh II + Hugh III together correspond to DG's Hugh IV; Fowler's Hugh IV = DG's Hugh V). Fowler explicitly flags the difficulty: "As Hugh is from now the dominant name in the family for 125 years, it is not always possible to separate certainly those who bore it."

## Bearing on the Walter question

Fowler names no children of Gerard and Edith other than Hugh and Gundred. His scope is the baronial succession to the Bedfordshire manors; younger sons without Bedfordshire interests fall outside it, so the omission of Walter is silence, not rejection.

## Verbatim, closing assessment (p. 157)

> The impression of repeated disloyalty which the family history at first suggests is softened by closer consideration; they stood always by Normandy, when possible by England. But they were between the upper and the nether millstone, and exposed to constant duress in the seizure of their castles and lands by the French. Their real sympathies are shown by their electing to be English at the final severance of Normandy about 1206.
