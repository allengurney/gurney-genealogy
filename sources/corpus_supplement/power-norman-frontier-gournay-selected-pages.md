## Scope and names

Power writes principally about the **senior lords of Gournay-en-Bray** and their lordship, not the Norfolk junior branch. The supplied pages do not name Walter de Gournay, do not identify a father for him, and do not connect Montigny-sur-Andelle to him. The genealogy at pp. 504--505 likewise diagrams only the senior baronial succession.

The book's ordinal sequence is independent of this repository's generation labels. In the pages below, Power's Hugh II is Gerard's son and Power's Hugh III is the senior lord who died in 1214--15.

## Early Normandy and the limit of the Eudes/Rollo tradition

### pp. 11--12 -- no fixed early territorial settlement

Power cautions against treating the early duchy as a territory with a settled 911 boundary. He says the traditional assertion, derived from Dudo, that Rollo received all land from the Epte to the sea "has been disproved"; Rollo's actual power was initially concentrated around Rouen, and wider Scandinavian control developed gradually. He likewise says there was no single established Norman frontier either in 911 or in 933, and that ducal authority at the nominal limits of Normandy fluctuated substantially even after 1100.

> "There was no single, established Norman frontier from either 911 ... or 933." (p. 11)

> "The Norman counts extended their power ... in a piecemeal fashion." (p. 12)

This is **not** evidence against Eudes as an individual, whose existence remains a local-tradition question. It is independent modern support for the existing caution that neither Dudo's territorial language nor the later Gournay tradition can establish a personal grant of Gournay to Eudes by Rollo.

## Frontier service and the Gournay inheritance

### p. 27 -- *estagium*

Power distinguishes frontier service from ordinary ducal service: the knight-service of some marcher magnates "was specifically to be performed *ad marchiam*." At Gournay-en-Bray, he records that castle-guard was called *estagium*, a term otherwise unknown in Normandy but common in adjoining Beauvaisis and elsewhere in France. He adds that the lord of Gournay and his knights had numerous lands there. The Gournay lordship therefore belongs in a cross-border service environment, not a self-contained Norman one.

> "At Gournay-en-Bray in the early thirteenth century, castle-guard was called *estagium*: this term was otherwise unknown in Normandy, but it was common in other parts of France including the adjoining Beauvaisis, where the lord of Gournay and his knights had numerous lands." (p. 27)

### pp. 188--191 -- the *Conquests* and the limits of a parage comparison

Power identifies the *Conquests Hue de Gournay* as twenty-four villages and hamlets north and east of Gournay. They belonged to the Norman customary-law sphere in general while retaining some Beauvaisis practices. This directly supports the repository's treatment of the Conquests as an aggregate frontier territory and customary-law zone, rather than a single manor.

> "The Conquests Hue de Gournay were twenty-four villages and hamlets north and east of Gournay." (p. 188)

Power describes a local inheritance rule for the *Conquests*: the eldest son received two-thirds of fiefs (besides the seigneurial manor) and the remaining third was shared by siblings. He expressly contrasts this custom with the *parage* followed in most of Normandy and with the primogeniture of Caux and Eu. The *Conquests* material concerns the senior Gournay lordship east of the Epte; it is **not** an account of Walter's claimed junior share or of Montigny-sur-Andelle.

> "Unlike in Normandy proper, the eldest son was entitled to two-thirds of the fiefs besides the seigneurial manor, while the remaining third was divided as hereditary property between his brothers and sisters. Inheritance customs in the *Conquests* therefore contrasted with the *parage* adhered to in most of Normandy." (p. 189)

Power warns that the familiar dating of the *Conquests* is uncertain. Their association with an earlier Hugh, or with the later Hugh III (d. 1214--15), is ambiguous. Before 1204, the best evidence concerns the three castles of Gournay, La Ferté-en-Bray, and Gaillefontaine, west of the Epte. By contrast, the eastern holdings are less well documented, though he finds evidence for villages east of the Epte and up the Thérain toward Gerberoy. He concludes that the lordship expanded "by enfeoffment, purchase and exchange rather than by grand political events" (p. 191).

> "Almost all the evidence for the lordship of Gournay before 1204 relates to the land pertaining to the three castles of Gournay, La Ferté-en-Bray and Gaillefontaine, which lay west of the River Epte and in the diocese of Rouen." (p. 190)

## Senior lordship, c. 1089--1215

### pp. 162 and 233 -- Hugh, Gerberoy, and the cross-frontier marriage network

Power associates the first appearance of the Conquests in the evidence with the period before the 1078--79 war in which Gournay and Hugh de Gournay suffered defeat near Gerberoy: the Gournay men appear to have seized Beauvaisis parishes that later bore the Conquests name. He immediately cautions that the dates of both the early acquisition and the later judicial evidence are open to challenge. The passage is therefore support for the existing territorial association of Hugh III and the Conquests, **not** proof of a precisely dated seizure.

On the later senior line, Power treats Gerard's widow's marriage to Dreux de Mouchy and her son's Coucy marriage as part of a continuing web of connections among north-eastern Normandy, Picardy, and northern Champagne. This reinforces the independently documented Edith--Dreux remarriage and the senior-line Coucy connection without replacing their charter and chronicle evidence.

> "The dates of both events are open to challenge." (p. 162)

### pp. 355--359 -- political setting

Power opens by cautioning against the stereotype of the Gournay lords as "turncoat marchers." He describes their acquisitions in the dioceses of Amiens and Beauvais and farther away in Laonnais through Hugh II's marriage to Melisende de Coucy, while noting their continuing interest in English lands, including Wendover, Bledlow, and Houghton Regis (p. 355).

> "In north-eastern Normandy, the lords of Gournay-en-Bray have often been taken as the very epitome of turncoat marchers; Hugh III de Gournay (d. 1214--15) was the butt of contemporary contempt and derision for precisely this fault. Such opinions paid scant attention to the previous history of Gournay." (p. 355)

For the senior lordship in the twelfth century, Power records that Hugh II supported Stephen at Shrewsbury in 1138 and received Wendover and Houghton Regis. His participation in the Second Crusade led to friction with Henry of Anjou; in the Young King's revolt, Hugh II and his surviving son Hugh III were captured, Gournay was burned, and their ransoms and related seizures damaged their English position (p. 356). Hugh III later exchanged Bledlow for Bec land around Gournay and Gaillefontaine and granted Houghton as his daughter's dower (p. 357).

Power's territorial formulation is particularly useful: in the twelfth century, "Bray" was more or less synonymous with the land of Hugh de Gournay. He says the lordship had no ducal revenues and that its military service was undefined in the marches, but that the lord's *dominatio* and *potestas* made the position clear (p. 357). The orientation toward Francia, Beauvaisis, and Vermandois was both strength and weakness: the Gournays held fiefs east of the Epte from those powers, while the French Vexin and Beauvaisis mattered greatly to them (p. 358).

> "The orientation towards Francia, the Beauvaisis and Vermandois was both the strength and weakness of the Gournays' power." (p. 358)

After Hugh III's 1203 break with King John, Power says that the Laonnais lands inherited through Melisende de Coucy served as a refuge until the 1206 reconciliation. His conclusion is that Hugh III was "no mere trimmer, but a powerful yet struggling head of a great lordship overwhelmed by irreconcilable external pressures" (p. 359).

### pp. 365, 368--369, 469 -- the three-castle lordship and frontier authority

Power repeatedly treats Gournay, La Ferté-en-Bray, and Gaillefontaine as a connected three-castle lordship (p. 365). For 1089, he identifies Gerard de Gournay among the five most powerful men of the north-east frontier who submitted to William Rufus. He reads Gerard's conflict with William de Breteuil as both a struggle for the Andelle valley and part of the larger contest between frontier lords, Robert Curthose, Rufus, and Count William of Évreux (p. 368).

> "Orderic Vitalis depicts the submission of the five most powerful men there to Rufus in 1089: first Count Stephen of Aumale and Gerard de Gournay, followed by Count Robert of Eu, Walter Giffard and Ralph de Mortemer." (p. 368)

The conclusion states that around Aumale and Gournay there was little territorial definition of ducal authority: its effective extent depended primarily on the duke's relationship with the lords of those towns. The Gournays' landholding extended into Beauvaisis (p. 469).

> "There is little or no sign that the limits of ducal authority were defined in territorial terms around Aumale and Gournay; it was primarily the duke's relationship with the lords of those towns that determined the extent of ducal authority in the district." (p. 469)

### pp. 53, 91, 105, 118, and 219 -- frontier jurisdiction, Capetian pressure, and Gournay lordship

Across several chapters, Power supplies mutually reinforcing place-level context. He treats the judicial frontier near Aumale and Gournay as blurred, with local nobility exercising much of the practical judicial authority (p. 53). He places Gournay, La Ferte, and Gaillefontaine among Norman lordships west of Beauvaisis whose revenues French officers collected during the 1202--03 advance (p. 91), and explains that Capetian acquisitions around Gournay and Gaillefontaine affected adjacent Beauvaisis territory (p. 105). Gournay confirmation-charters, moreover, reveal uncertainty about ducal authority in parishes situated in the dioceses of Amiens and Beauvais (p. 118).

For the later senior lordship, Power notes an act styling a Hugh de Gournay *Dei permissione dominus Gorniaci* and calls the Gournay lords among Normandy's most powerful nobles (p. 219). That is valuable context for the senior barony, but the source's Hugh numbering must not be silently substituted for the repository's generational labels.

## Genealogy diagram -- pp. 504--505

The diagram headed "18. Gournay" gives this senior-line sequence:

- Hugh I de la Ferté; Hugh II de la Ferté; then Hugh I de Gournay (fl. 1066 x 1086) and Basilea, daughter of Gerard Flaitel.
- Gerard (d. c. 1104) married Edeva (Edith) de Warenne; after Gerard's death, Edith married Dreux de Mouchy (fl. c. 1101--30).
- Gerard and Edith's shown children include Ralph I, Hugh II (d. 1178--79), and Gundrada. Hugh II is shown with Beatrice and then Melisende de Coucy; their shown children include Hugh, Edeva, Gerard, and Hugh III (d. 1214--15), husband of Juliana.
- Hugh III's shown children include Hugh (d. c. 1202), Melisende, Gerard, and Hugh IV (d. 1239).

The diagram does **not** include Walter de Gournay or purport to list every child of Gerard and Edith. Its note records that the parentage of Hugh III's Juliana is unknown and rejects the later tradition that she was a sister of Count Renaud of Boulogne (p. 505).

## French conquest of the lordship in 1202

### pp. 408, 414--415, 424, 532--538 and Map IX

Power treats the 1202 sequence cautiously because the narrative sources and chancery material cannot be fully reconciled (p. 532). His reconstruction nonetheless establishes several points in the supplied pages:

- King John was at Gournay on 27 May 1202, then retreated through Lyons toward Orival; the dated itinerary places the surrounding Andelle valley under Norman control at that point (p. 535).
- The map places Gournay as a major fortress and identifies 5 July as the date by which it was in French hands (Map IX, p. 536).
- Gournay had fallen by 5 July, its garrison having fled; Power calls it "probably the one major fortress between Eu and Gisors not yet in [Philip's] control" (p. 538). The footnote notes a French garrison from 5 July and French accounting of the prévôté from 4 July.
- By mid-July, Philip's authority covered eastern Normandy as far as Arques, including Lyons, Argueil, and Hugh de Gournay's castles. John failed to relieve Gournay after recovering Radepont; Power judges that its fall deprived him of his last main garrison in the eastern marches and opened the route to Arques (p. 424).

> "[John] was still confident enough to visit Gournay (letter dated 27 May), only to retreat via Lyons to Orival on the Seine again." (p. 535)

> "[Gournay] had fallen by 5 July, the garrison having fled." (p. 538)

> "[The] failure signally to relieve Gournay ... deprived John of his last main garrison in the eastern marches and opened the way for a French advance upon the great ducal castle of Arques." (p. 424)

The source therefore supplies a detailed history of the senior lordship's 1202 fall. It does not supply evidence about Walter's parentage, Montigny, or the mechanism of a junior-branch partition.
