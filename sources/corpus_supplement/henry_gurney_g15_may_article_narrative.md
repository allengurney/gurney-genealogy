# Henry Gurney (G15) — Narrative and Research Findings from Steven W. May’s Article

**Source ID:** `may-henry-gurney-spenser-2005`

_Source reviewed: Steven W. May, “Henry Gurney, A Norfolk Farmer, Reads Spenser and Others,” in **Spenser Studies: A Renaissance Poetry Annual**, vol. XX, pp. 183–223, uploaded PDF._[^may-source]

## The ancestor who turns out not to be “just” a Norfolk farmer

Steven W. May’s article sharply changes the feel of Henry Gurney (G15). The current fact sheet already recognizes him as a poet, bookman, and landholder, but May’s full article makes him much more vivid: Henry emerges as a **working estate lord, late-life poet, polemicist, amateur literary critic, lender and borrower of books, organizer of a rural intellectual network, frustrated husband, increasingly deaf gentleman, Protestant loyalist, and father whose own learned son criticized his poetry with painful bluntness**.

May’s title, “a Norfolk Farmer,” is intentionally understated. Henry was not a ploughman-farmer; he was a **gentleman estate manager**, lord of Great Ellingham and connected to West Barsham, Hingham, Harpley, Irstead, and the older Gurney landholding world. But the “farmer” word does capture something important: his intellectual life was not detached from fields, fences, rents, tenants, crops, hunters, dogs, geese, cheese, grafting, timber, lawsuits, and parish affairs. The manuscript at the center of the article — Bodleian MS Tanner 175 — began as an estate and account book and then became a private literary world.

That transformation is the central story: **an account book became a self-portrait**.

Henry first used the manuscript in the 1570s for practical estate business: rentals, leases, indentures, debts, family property notes, earlier estate records from his grandfather Anthony’s time, a pedigree, mercers’ bills, a list of debts, and a record of burials in the church and chapel of Great Ellingham. Around 1592, when Henry was about forty-three, the book became primarily literary. He later wrote of himself that “till his age of forty thre a verse did never wright.” After that, he filled the book with roughly **628 poems**, most by himself, along with book inventories, poetic “censures” or verse reviews, and records of manuscript circulation.[^may-tanner]

The result is unusually intimate. For many sixteenth-century ancestors we get pedigrees, land deeds, wills, and perhaps church register events. For Henry we get something closer to a personality.

## The physical manuscript: Henry’s hand, Henry’s system, Henry’s afterlife

Tanner 175 was not a casual notebook. May describes it as a small folio, about **200 × 304 mm**, containing two preliminary leaves and **239 folios**, though its own pagination implies it once extended to at least **244 folios**. Nearly all the blank space was used. Much is in Henry’s handwriting, much of it now badly faded. He added rubricated titles and dates to many poems, created an index of “speciall matters,” and even tracked where in the manuscript particular topics could be found.[^may-ms-physical]

Several details are unusually human:

- A half-leaf was tipped into the book between folios 82 and 83; Henry wrote a poem on it with the paper flat against the following leaf, and one marginal line trailed onto the next page.
- Folio 103 was also tipped in.
- Two leaves were lost between present folios 71 and 72.
- Henry himself explained one missing leaf: “the leafe following I cutt out my self.”
- A parchment slip, probably cut from the original binding, reads: **“Edward Gurnay his booke”** — evidence that the manuscript passed to Henry’s grandson Edward Gurney, born 1608 and died 1641.[^may-ms-provenance]

This is worth adding to the G15 research companion because it gives the manuscript a family afterlife. It was not merely Henry’s notebook later discovered by scholars; it appears to have been preserved within the Gurney family at least into the next generation. May further suggests that later Cambridge connections may explain how it eventually reached the Tanner collection, possibly through Henry’s great-grandson Henry at Caius and William Sancroft of Emmanuel College.[^may-ms-provenance]

## Childhood inside the Howard orbit — and the darker irony of Anthony Gurney

The family-history section contains one of the strongest new narrative moments.

May says Henry’s family retained “viable social ties” with the Howards, the greatest family in Norfolk: dukes of Norfolk and earls of Surrey. Henry was sponsored at baptism in 1549 by **Lady Catherine Howard**, daughter of Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey. Henry’s sister Frances had Lady Jane Howard as godmother in 1547, and Henry’s brother Anthony had the widowed Lady Frances Howard, Countess of Surrey, as godmother in 1550.[^may-howards]

The dramatic irony is severe: **Henry’s grandfather Anthony Gurney had been foreman of the grand jury that condemned Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, to death for high treason in 1547.** Yet within the same period, the Howard women continued acting as godmothers to Gurney children.[^may-howards]

That detail deserves special treatment in the G15 narrative because it gives the family’s social world real texture. These were not distant heraldic associations. The Gurneys were close enough to the Howards for baptismal sponsorship, yet also implicated in the judicial machinery that destroyed one of the family’s most famous men. This suggests a Norfolk gentry world where obligation, faction, patronage, and survival could coexist uneasily.

## A chronology correction: Henry was an eight-year-old heir, not a twenty-one-year-old heir

This is the most important conflict with the current project narrative.

May states that Henry’s father Francis predeceased Anthony; Anthony then died in **1557**, leaving Henry, the male heir, as an **eight-year-old minor**. Henry matriculated at Cambridge in October 1564, apparently did not take a degree, came of age in **1570**, and married Helen Blenerhasset on **10 June 1571**.[^may-chronology]

The current fact sheet says Henry was born 21 January 1548/49 but also says he inherited as grandson and heir of Anthony, **“aged 21 at his grandfather’s death in January 1555/6.”** That cannot be right as written: a person born in January 1548/49 could not be twenty-one in January 1555/56. The current research companion already flags death-date and burial issues, but this specific age-at-inheritance contradiction appears to need review.[^current-g15]

Recommended correction direction:

> Henry was born in 1548/49; his father Francis died before Anthony; Anthony died c. 1557; Henry inherited as an eight-year-old minor; he came of age in 1570 and then settled into estate management.

The “aged twenty-one” language may be a misread, a Blomefield transcription issue, or a confusion with Henry’s coming of age in 1570. The article’s chronology is internally coherent.

## Education: a small but real source tension

May states on page 186 that Henry matriculated pensioner from **Christ’s College, Cambridge** in October 1564 and apparently did not take a degree.[^may-chronology] Later, however, May’s discussion appears to refer to Henry entering **Corpus Christi** in 1564 when comparing him with Thomas Blenerhasset’s Cambridge enrollment; he also describes Anthony Drury as having matriculated from Corpus Christi about seven years before Henry’s admission to the same college.[^may-blenerhasset]

This should be treated carefully. The article itself seems to contain a tension: **Christ’s College vs. Corpus Christi College**. Before updating the repo, verify Henry in Venn’s *Alumni Cantabrigienses*. For now, the safest phrasing is:

> Henry matriculated at Cambridge in October 1564, apparently without taking a degree; May identifies the college as Christ’s College in the main biographical passage, though later discussion appears to connect him with Corpus Christi, requiring verification against Venn.

## Marriage and children: May gives a different count at 1593

May says Henry’s poem “The match & petigre of Gurnay & his wife their childrens number sex and age” shows that he married **Helen, daughter of John Blenerhasset**, and that **eleven of their thirteen children were still alive when he composed the verses in 1593**. Henry’s prose notes say the marriage took place **10 June 1571**.[^may-marriage]

This introduces a useful nuance against the current “13 children / 12 surviving” epitaph language. The current fact sheet uses the epitaph reading “He 13 children gat, seven sons and daughters five in life,” treating it as twelve surviving children.[^current-g15] May’s article says eleven of thirteen were alive in 1593. These can possibly be reconciled, but not automatically. Options:

1. May’s “eleven alive in 1593” is correct, and the epitaph wording has been overread.
2. Another child was born or another count changed after the 1593 poem.
3. The epitaph’s “seven sons and daughters five in life” may mean something subtler than “twelve living at Henry’s death.”
4. One source is simply wrong or has a transcription issue.

Recommended repo note:

> The child count should preserve both witnesses: Henry’s 1593 poem, per May, had eleven of thirteen children alive; the later epitaph tradition appears to count seven sons and five daughters “in life.” Reconciliation remains open.

## Day-to-day Henry: fences, hounds, corn, cheese, geese, deafness, lawyers, and marital accounting

This is where the article is most valuable for humanizing G15.

Henry’s poems were often about practical life. He wrote in praise of **dogs, geese, and properly constituted cheese**. May notes that he also composed verses on agricultural practice in imitation of Thomas Tusser’s *Five Hundred Points of Good Husbandry*, a book of verse advice for managing farm and household.[^may-farm-life]

The most vivid estate-management anecdote concerns hunters. Henry complains that a neighbor, “goodman G B,” along with “two or thre of clownes” and “stinking howndes,” invaded his closes, broke fences and gates, and trampled his corn:

> My neybor goodman G B  
> with two or thre of clownes  
> his followers that be,  
> all following stinking howndes  
> himself vppon his lade  
> & they vppon their feet  
> My closes do invade  
> & thinke yt not vnmeet  
>  
> to breake my fence & gates  
> & trample on my Come  
> presuming that such states  
> should therfore be forborne[^may-hunters]

In plain language: Henry is furious that a neighbor and his hunting party treat his enclosed land as if it were open sport. This is not just quaint local color. It shows Henry as a property-conscious estate lord in a world where hunting privilege, local status, and agricultural damage collided. It also suggests why his poetry often values order, rule, measure, and proper boundaries.

His deafness is another major human detail. By about **1597**, Henry was growing deaf. He wrote multiple poems about it. One complaint is legal and practical: when he consulted lawyers, they had to speak loudly, which risked revealing his legal strategy to nearby enemies:

> whileas my case wth Councellors I treate  
> their speech wth peyne they very lowd must sounde  
> wherby no less even dangers very greate  
> vnto my cause, do oftentimes Redound  
> when enimys, who standeth neere at hand  
> the secretes of my case may vnderstand[^may-deafness]

But the same deafness also pushed him inward toward writing. He describes poetry as a way to “shorten” time, “wast the night,” please himself and friends, and compensate for lost social ease:

> Who can Inioy no company  
> nor be of them Inioyd  
> by reason of Infirmity  
> nor otherwise Imploid  
> to shorten way to wast the night  
> to please him self & ffrend  
> If honest poemes he Indite  
> who may it discommend[^may-deafness]

This may be the interpretive key to Henry. Poetry was not only vanity or hobby. It was a social technology. As hearing failed, writing let him remain present in the world: correcting books, sending poems, receiving replies, and participating in a circle that could still “hear” him on the page.

The marital passage is equally revealing. After twenty-eight years of marriage, Henry says his wife accused him daily of wasteful spending:

> Whi should his wife in teeth him daily cast  
> with vayne expence & of exceeding wast?

He protests that he gave her a strict accounting of every expense and that she could detect no actual waste, yet still accused him:

> he to hir, a Reconing euer streight  
> did yield of all expence that he did make  
> although she can no wast in him detect  
> yet still much waste to him she doth obiect.

His conclusion is bleak:

> for greater griefe the world can not afford  
> then wiues contempt in gesture, deede & woord.  
> most rare it is for man a wife to find  
> that every wey shall well content his minde[^may-wife]

This should be used carefully. It is Henry’s side only, not a neutral account of Helen. But it is deeply valuable as first-person evidence: the household was not just a pedigree machine. It had budget arguments, emotional grievance, and the intimacy of daily reproach. It also reinforces that Henry thought like an accountant: he kept records, gave reckonings, indexed his poems, tracked loans, and wanted faults itemized by “aucthor, Rule, or reason.”

## The religious man: anti-Catholic, loyal Protestant, but not Puritan

Henry’s religious position is more nuanced than simply “last Gurney born Catholic; became Protestant.”

May’s Henry is fiercely anti-Catholic. His major poetic work, **“The Anatomy of Popery,”** ran to more than **2,000 lines**, not counting multiple drafted additions. Other poem titles include “of popish heathen Sottishnes” and “Catholique popish churche.” He translated anti-Catholic passages from Mantuan’s Latin *Eclogues* and wrote against image worship, pro-Catholic tracts, William Alabaster’s Catholic “Motives,” and other Catholic or Catholic-adjacent works.[^may-religion]

Yet May emphasizes that Henry was **not a Puritan**. His 1614 will left legacies to younger sons only if “none hould any fantasticall or erronious opinions, so adjudged by our bishop or civil lawes.” That formula places him inside the established Church of England, suspicious of extremes on both sides. He detested Rome, but he also trusted bishops and civil law as boundaries of religious order.[^may-coterie-religion]

This fits the current fact sheet’s interpretation, but May gives it stronger evidence. Henry’s circle did include people with puritanical leanings — for example Parson Trendle of Ovington, who was cited in Bishop Redman’s 1597 visitation for failure to wear the surplice — but Henry himself was more conformist than radical.[^may-trendle]

His anti-Catholicism also does not mean he was intellectually incapable of fairness. His response to the Jesuit Robert Southwell is the key example.

## The surprising fairness toward Robert Southwell

Of all the poetry Henry reviewed, he reserved his highest praise for Robert Southwell’s *Maeoniae*, a collection published shortly after Southwell’s execution in 1595. Henry knew Southwell was a Jesuit. He guarded against treating Southwell’s poetic merit as proof of Catholic truth. But he still admired the poems:

> For matter form Choise woordes and fittest phrase  
> Deepe Style or arte, or godly meditation  
> as présidentes thes poemes may we blase  
> & compt for woorkes of speciall estimation  
> my Censure saith to truth or Conscience debtor  
> for all respectes scarse redd I ever better[^may-southwell]

May rightly says this is remarkable and to Henry’s credit. A man who wrote thousands of anti-Catholic lines could still read a Jesuit poet and say, in effect: **as poetry, this is excellent**. He drew the doctrinal boundary, but he did not let the boundary erase aesthetic judgment.

That matters for Henry’s character. He was rigid, but not stupid. Polemical, but not blind. Rule-bound, but not wholly closed.

## The loyal Elizabethan: queen, Burghley, Essex, Spain, and Hakluyt

Henry was deeply loyal to Elizabeth I and to the late Elizabethan Protestant regime. He wrote multiple poems praising the queen and Lord Burghley. During the Essex rebellion of 1601, he showed no sympathy for Essex; his verse review of William Barlow’s authorized account began: “this bookes contents wch cannot be butt true.”[^may-elizabeth]

May uses Henry as a corrective to the claim that Elizabeth’s subjects were generally weary of her by the 1590s. Henry’s manuscript praises were private, not printed court flattery. That makes them stronger evidence of sincere regime loyalty.[^may-elizabeth-conclusion]

His response to Hakluyt’s *Voyages* is also important. He read English navigation and colonization through the lens of Elizabethan Protestant prosperity:

> Through Goverment most sage of noble queen  
> such peace & welth do to hir Subiectes flowe  
> as speciall meanes to noble myndes have been  
> Realmes most remote to serch therin to sow  
> The Christian faith & Civill good behavior  
> wth tradeing for ech others welth & favor[^may-hakluyt]

This is an unusually direct window into how a rural Norfolk gentleman interpreted overseas expansion: not merely profit, but a blend of peace at home, wealth, trade, Protestant mission, and “civill good behavior.” It is idealized and nationalistic, but extremely useful as a mental-world detail.

## Henry’s theory of poetry: order, rule, memory, smoothness, usefulness

Henry’s poetic standards explain both his appeal and his limitations. He believed good verse should be regular, clear, useful, memorable, and technically correct.

His fullest statement says a “perfect verse” must contain much matter, use fit and known words, avoid forced or vain words, keep meter/time/feet aligned, and run “Roundly smoth & sweet” on the tongue:

> A perfect verse much matter must conteine  
> fit woordes & knowne, none forced nor in vayne  
> A word alike in meeter tyme & feete  
> And on the tongue Ronn Roundly smoth & sweet[^may-poetics]

He also saw verse as a tool for memory:

> Of verse it is the chiefest property  
> to Stoare the head & helpe the memory.[^may-poetics]

This is a key interpretive point. Henry did not primarily think of poetry as imaginative invention. He thought of it as **ordered, memorable, moralized knowledge**. His preferred topics were “Divine” philosophy first, then mathematical, moral, and natural matters.[^may-poetics]

This helps explain why his estate book became a poetry book. For Henry, putting something into verse was a way to sort it, store it, judge it, and circulate it.

It also explains why he disliked Spenser.

## Henry versus Spenser: he respected fame but rejected innovation

Henry is one of the earliest known readers of *The Faerie Queene* to record a critical response. He read the 1590 first edition, describing it precisely as a quarto of 589 pages. He copied the epigraph and the first five stanzas, even marking the rhyme scheme as “1.3 / 2.4.5.7 / 6.8.9.” His interest was technical.[^may-spenser]

He knew Spenser had “great fame,” but he disliked the poem:

> ould outworne woordes, this aucthor doth observe  
> And vsuall staff, or measure, doth reiect  
> if praise or blame, therfor he do deserve  
> I cannot ludge, although I may suspect  
> that when a woorke is sträng & new devised  
> the aucthor is Invyed, or dispised[^may-spenser]

Plain translation: Spenser uses old-fashioned words and rejects normal stanza/meter. Henry cannot fully judge whether this deserves praise or blame, but he suspects that strange new works invite envy or contempt.

May’s reading is that Henry failed to appreciate Spenser’s technical brilliance. But from Henry’s point of view, Spenser violated two core rules: use known words and keep conventional measure. Henry did not read poetry as modern literary scholars do. He read like an estate manager: **clarity, measure, rule, and utility first**.

The irony is that Henry, a militant Protestant, probably would have loved much of *The Faerie Queene* had he continued into its anti-Catholic allegory. But Spenser lost him at the level of form.

## “Mother Hubberds Tale”: Henry missed the political bite

Henry also censured Spenser’s “Mother Hubberds Tale,” likely in early 1596. May notes that he may have borrowed a manuscript copy rather than the printed *Complaints* volume. Many readers circulated the poem cautiously because of its alleged satire of Lord Burghley and Robert Cecil.[^may-mother]

Henry did not seem to notice the political satire. He found the theme obscure, the rhyme and feet faulty, and the verse harsh:

> No point of praise ther soundeth in myn eare  
> in poem wch such speciall fame doth bear  
> yt halteth oft aswell in Ryme as feet  
> hath Theame obscure & verse more harsh then sweet  
> myn Ignorance I rather yet Suspect  
> then dare avouch therin but one defect  
> for who that once hath gott him self a fame  
> his after faltes bear but reprovers blame[^may-mother]

The humility is interesting: “I suspect my own ignorance before I dare claim one defect.” But the judgment is still negative.

May uses this to show a divide between politically sensitive elite readers and “average, educated” provincial readers. Henry was literate, connected, and politically loyal, but he did not automatically read poetry as court satire. For the G15 narrative, this matters: Henry was not isolated, but he was still provincial in political-literary decoding.

## The book coterie: a rural information network around Great Ellingham

May’s reconstruction of Henry’s coterie is one of the article’s most genealogically valuable sections.

Henry loaned books, borrowed books, returned books with verse “censures,” and distributed copies of his own poems. The exchanges took place mainly between about **1595 and 1603**. May identifies around **twenty-one named members**, plus unnamed participants, expanding the circle to about **twenty-five**. It was centered within a dozen miles of Great Ellingham, with one known reach to the Cornwallis estate at Earl Soham, Suffolk, about thirty miles away.[^may-coterie]

Named or likely participants include:

| Person | Relationship / role | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Anthony Drury of Besthorpe** | Henry’s first cousin | Borrowed Hakluyt’s *Voyages* in 1595; received Henry’s “Medley of Conceits”; lived about two miles from Great Ellingham. |
| **Robert Thornton of Hingham** | Neighboring gentleman | Borrowed Hakluyt after Drury returned it; Hingham about two miles north. |
| **John Cornwallis of Earl Soham** | Brother-in-law | Husband of Katherine Blenerhasset, Helen’s sister; received “the best” copy of Henry’s *Medley*; perhaps wealthiest identifiable member. |
| **Ralph Furness** | Clergyman, probable cousin | Borrowed Saravia books defending English church government. |
| **George Copping / Coppin** | Vicar of Besthorpe | Received part of the *Medley*. |
| **John Trendle, rector of Ovington** | Clergyman | Borrowed Gregory’s *Decretals*; cited in 1597 for failure to wear the surplice. |
| **Henry Womack** | Clergyman | Borrowed several books; later united by Henry to Great Ellingham church in 1614. |
| **John Poynton** | Rector of Kilverstone | Borrowed Racster’s anti-Alabaster book. |
| **Bassingbourne Throckmorton** | Nephew | Received Blagrave’s *Mathematical Jewel*; loaned Henry *The Passions of the Spirit*. |
| **Mary Throckmorton Rawley** | Niece; only woman explicitly in Henry’s circle | Received a copy of the *Medley*. |
| **William Rawley** | Mary’s husband, likely “Mr Rauly” | Borrowed Thomas Playfere’s sermon. |
| **Miles Hobart** | Cousin by marriage | Borrowed Gratian’s *Decretum*. |
| **Edward Berthelet / “Mr Barties”** | Probable lawyer and JP | Borrowed a New Testament; loaned Henry the *Scanderbeg* history; received the *Anatomy*. |

The social pattern is revealing. Henry’s network was **family-heavy, clerical, local, Cambridge-touched, Protestant, and manuscript-aware**. At least a third of the members had enrolled at Cambridge, though only the clergy took degrees.[^may-coterie]

The only woman explicitly named is Mary Throckmorton Rawley. That should be added as a small but meaningful finding: Henry’s literary circulation was not exclusively male, though it was overwhelmingly so.

## Henry as manuscript publisher

May calls Henry a significant publisher of manuscript verse. He prepared at least **four copies** of the *Anatomy of Popery* and at least **four copies** of the *Medley of Conceits*. These were not printed books, but handwritten circulation copies.[^may-manuscript-publisher]

This is a useful framing for the repo: Henry was not merely writing privately. He was **publishing within a manuscript economy**.

May also connects Henry to Chetham’s Library MS Mun. A.4.15. That manuscript, dating from about 1603 to 1626 or later, contains **seventeen poems** totaling about **660 lines** that May identifies as Henry’s or nearly all traceable to Tanner 175. They appear near poems by John Donne, Ben Jonson, Sir John Davies, and Sir Walter Ralegh. May calls their presence “striking proof” that Henry’s poems circulated in manuscript, perhaps after his death.[^may-chetham]

This deserves more prominence in the G15 research companion. It means Henry’s poetry escaped Great Ellingham. Even if May thinks the verse is pedestrian, the circulation history is important.

## The pain of being criticized — especially by Edmund

Henry criticized others constantly, but May shows that he also received criticism.

One friend praised his *Anatomy of Popery*, writing of Henry’s “golden penn.” Henry replied in verse, one of the clearest examples of original verse exchange within the circle.[^may-criticism]

But other readers were harsh. Henry complained that his “nerest frendes” derided works he had written to please them, and did so before his wife, child, servant, foe, or friend. He said strangers applauded the poems, while those closest to him mocked them without specifying the faults.[^may-criticism]

The sharpest blow came from his learned son **Edmund Gurney**, the future divine. Edmund wrote a verse “censure” of Henry’s own poem “of Poesy rules.” May says Henry must have copied Edmund’s criticism “with a very reluctant hand.” Edmund mocked his father’s verse as rhyming prose and concluded:

> much better wert, you made no verse at all  
> then from ye Rules therof so much to fall[^may-edmund]

This is one of the most human moments in the entire article. Henry, who judged Spenser, Weever, Southwell, Breton, Foxe, Hakluyt, and others, had to preserve in his own notebook the fact that his scholar-son thought he had failed at poetry by his own standards.

For the family narrative, this is excellent: it shows an intellectually active father-son household, but not a sentimental one. Edmund was formed in Henry’s world of books, polemic, and rules; he also surpassed and challenged it.

## The library: what Henry’s books say about him

Henry’s 1595 inventory contains **eighty-eight titles**, including books “in other mens handes.” May says sixty-six were first published after 1557 and were likely acquired by Henry himself, while some older books may have come from his father or grandfather. Ten titles are marked “to Edm” or “giue Ed,” apparently books intended for his scholarly third son Edmund; in his 1614 draft will, Henry left all his Latin books to Edmund.[^may-library]

### A Protestant working library, not just devotional reading

The library is heavily religious: five Bibles, three New Testaments, and more than twenty Reformation-polemic entries. He owned or used works by Bilson, Charke, Jewel, Fulke, Nowell, Whitaker, Knewstub, Musculus, Marbecke, Chemnitz, Saravia, and others. He also owned Catholic or Catholic-reference works: de Burgo’s *Pupilla oculi*, canon law, Gratian, Gregory’s *Decretals*, *Sacerdotale Tridentinum*, *Summa Angelica*, Platina’s papal lives, and Cardinal Allen’s defense of English Catholics.[^may-appendix]

The impression is not of Catholic sympathy. It is of a polemicist who needed enemy texts, inherited older Catholic books, and preserved enough of the old learned apparatus to argue against it. His phrase “an ould popish book” for de Burgo’s *Pupilla oculi* is especially useful: he labels the inherited Catholic past from a Protestant present.[^may-library]

### A legal and estate-management shelf

His law and administration books include Pulton’s statutes, Rastell’s statutes, Lambarde’s *Eirenarcha* on justices of peace, Littleton’s *Tenures*, Kitchen on court leet, Perkins on English law, Fitzherbert-like office material, and canon-law books. This fits the practical Henry of fences, debts, leases, litigation, advowsons, and loud lawyers.[^may-appendix]

The landholding implication: Henry’s household probably had a working “office” culture. Books were tools for managing property, parish rights, litigation risk, and local authority.

### Husbandry, natural knowledge, and practical science

The inventory includes Leonard Mascall on grafting and planting, Digges’s *Pantometria*, Blagrave’s *Mathematical Jewel*, Actuarius on urine, Lyte’s herbal, Lemnius on occult natural marvels, Heresbach on agriculture, and Lycosthenes on prodigies.[^may-appendix]

This combination is very Henry: practical land use, measurement, medicine, natural signs, and mathematical curiosity. The “Mathematical Jewel” was given to Bassingbourne Throckmorton, indicating that mathematical and practical-scientific books circulated within the family network.[^may-coterie-family]

### History, geography, and national imagination

Henry owned or borrowed Grafton, Holinshed, Hakluyt, Muenster’s *Cosmography*, Livy, Valerius Maximus, Barnaby Rich’s *Allarme to England*, and the Queen’s speeches about Mary Queen of Scots. This is the reading profile of a man who viewed England historically, providentially, and internationally.[^may-appendix]

Hakluyt, especially, shows him thinking beyond Norfolk. But even there, he interprets global exploration through Elizabethan Protestant order.

### Poetry and literary taste

His literary books include Ascham’s *Scholemaster*, Thomas Blenerhasset’s *Second Part of the Mirror for Magistrates*, Churchyard on the wars in Flanders, Nashe’s *Pierce Penilesse*, Barclay’s *Ship of Fools*, Barnaby Rich, Sidney’s *Defence of Poesie*, Livy, and possibly Ariosto/Harington indirectly through his poetic references.[^may-literary-books]

The Blenerhasset connection is especially important. May argues that the “Tho Blenerhassit” associated with *The Mirror for Magistrates* was probably Henry’s brother-in-law Thomas Blenerhasset, not the usually identified Norwich man of the same name. This same Thomas stood godfather to Henry’s first son Thomas in 1572. May speculates that Henry may have met him at Cambridge, and that friendship may even explain Henry’s Suffolk marriage.[^may-blenerhasset]

That is a valuable genealogical-social inference, though it should be marked as May’s argument rather than proven fact.

## What Henry’s book reviews tell us about his mind

Henry’s “censures” were not casual marginal notes. They were often verse responses written into books or sent back with them. His review of the *Scanderbeg* history explains the practice:

> Brief Censure of this volumes larg content  
> with my Retorne & thankes I have you sent  
> yet if your booke, or fancy yt offend  
> you may both sone & safli out it rend[^may-scanderbeg]

This is a gift-exchange ritual: borrow book, read book, compose judgment, return book with thanks, and permit the owner to tear out the verse if unwelcome.

His reviews show a mind that valued:

- technical regularity;
- scriptural proof;
- Protestant truth;
- Elizabethan loyalty;
- practical usefulness;
- decorum, meaning the right style for the right speaker;
- and plainness over obscurity.

He disliked John Weever’s *Epigrammes*, apparently accessed through Edmund, because Weever violated rules of poetry. Henry criticized “dark” warp, “harsh” woof, omitted conjunctions, causals, relatives, and verbs — essentially saying Weever forced readers to supply missing syntax.[^may-weever]

He liked Foxe’s *Acts and Monuments*, writing seventy-eight lines in praise of “the first Tom of ye booke of Mártires.” His praise of Foxe suggests he may have known something of Foxe’s life:

> a fox he was by name & skill but yet a sheepe for mind  
> all fraudes he clarkely doth disclose yet was to none inclined[^may-foxe]

He read a book on women by a French author and objected that it failed to give proper honor to Elizabeth, “our true & noble english quene.” He read about Siamese twins born in Flanders, though May could not identify the exact printed account. He read Henry Lyte’s herbal and joked, in effect, that if its remedies all worked, physicians would suppress it because they would lose business.[^may-diverse]

This is the “color” to preserve: Henry was opinionated about everything — theology, Spain, women, herbal medicine, monstrous births, meter, printers, famous poets, and local legal secrecy.

## Interpretive portrait: Henry as a man of rule under pressure

My interpretive read, clearly marked as interpretation: Henry’s personality seems to be organized around **order under pressure**.

He inherited as a child after the death of father and grandfather. He lived through religious reversal, aristocratic danger, and Norfolk patronage politics. He managed multiple estates and legal obligations. He had many children. His hearing failed. His wife challenged his spending. Neighbors broke fences. Friends mocked his poems. His son publicly defeated him at his own poetic rules.

In response, Henry made systems:

- estate headings;
- family pedigrees;
- book inventories;
- borrower lists;
- poem indexes;
- subject tables;
- verse rules;
- colored-ink variants;
- polemical arguments;
- manuscript copies distributed through trusted networks.

His literary conservatism may not be mere provincial dullness. It may express the same temperament that made him an estate manager: **known words, sound fences, correct measures, accounted expenses, indexed topics, lawful doctrine, and poems that “run roundly smooth and sweet.”**

That is why Spenser troubled him. Spenser’s poetic world was imaginative, archaic, allegorical, and technically innovative. Henry’s world was measured, moral, juridical, scriptural, and domestic. He could respect fame, but he wanted art to submit to rule.

## Suggested concise repo-ready finding

Henry Gurney’s Bodleian manuscript, Tanner 175, should be treated as the richest self-authored source for any pre-Quaker Gurney ancestor. It began as an estate book for Hingham, West Barsham, Great Ellingham, and related property business, but from about 1592 became Henry’s literary archive: more than six hundred poems, a 1595 library inventory, verse “censures” of borrowed books, family records, borrower lists, and records of manuscript circulation. The manuscript shows Henry not simply as a Norfolk landholder but as a late-life poet, Protestant controversialist, estate manager, book lender, book borrower, rural literary organizer, and opinionated critic whose household and network can be reconstructed in unusual detail. His poems preserve concrete life details — hunters breaking fences and trampling corn, praise of dogs/geese/cheese, deafness during legal consultations, domestic conflict over spending, loyalty to Elizabeth, hostility to Spain and Catholicism, and the sting of criticism from friends and from his son Edmund. May’s article also corrects or complicates several project details: Henry appears to have inherited as an eight-year-old minor, not as a twenty-one-year-old; his child count in 1593 was eleven living of thirteen; and the attached PDF confirms the May article citation as *Spenser Studies* vol. XX (2005), pp. 183–223.
