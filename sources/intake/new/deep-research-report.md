## Bottom line

For your current direct medieval line, this book is **important but mostly collateral**. The key reason is that Anderson’s “Gournay” material is not primarily the old Norman/Norfolk **de Gournay** line currently tracked in your project through Walter de Gournay, Gerard de Gournay, Hugh de Gournay, Renaud, and the traditional Eudes line centered on entity["city","Gournay-en-Bray","Seine-Maritime, France"] in entity["place","Normandy","France"]. Instead, Anderson repeatedly says that the “Gournay” line in *this* book descends from **John de Harpetre**, a younger son of Ascelin Gouel de Perceval, whose descendants later **assumed the name Gournay**. In other words, the 1742 book treats a **Harpetre-Gournay cadet branch of the Yvery/Perceval house**, not the older de Gournay seigneurs of Gournay-en-Bray who belong to your direct line as presently outlined in the ancestor table. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5search1

That makes this source valuable in three specific ways. First, it is an **early printed witness**, predating entity["people","Daniel Gurney","genealogist 1848"], for how an eighteenth-century genealogist understood the Harpetre-Gournay branch. Second, it preserves a **Somerset place-cluster** around Harpetre, Kary, Weston, Stawell, and related holdings that may matter for collateral mapping and surname-formation history. Third, it contains several **anecdotal and atmospheric passages**—feuds, castle warfare, baronial rebellion, and surname formation—that are excellent narrative material for research companions, even where they are not direct-line proof. fileciteturn0file0

My strongest conclusion is therefore this: **the book does not appear to add new evidence for the early direct de Gournay barons of Gournay-en-Bray and Norfolk**, but it **does** add useful collateral evidence about the separate Harpetre-Gournay branch, including an explicit statement that additional sons of Ascelin may have existed, and a useful list of landed places that deserve follow-up. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5search1

## Cleaned transcript extracts

### Extract on the origin of the Harpetre-Gournay branch

**Cleaned transcript from PDF p. 28, printed p. 4:**

> “He left seven sons, and a daughter, married to Radulfus Rufus, a Norman nobleman. Of the sons we have recovered only the names of Robert, William and John. Of which the youngest, John, was portion’d by his father in the manor of Harpetree, of which he assumed the name, which he afterwards changed to Gournay; and from him descended the Barons of Harpetree-Gournay before-mentioned.”

This is the single most important passage in the volume for your purposes. It is explicit, succinct, and repeated elsewhere in the book: Anderson’s Gournay line is **not** the old de Gournay/Braiois line, but a cadet line beginning with **John de Harpetre**. It is therefore relevant chiefly as **collateral surname evidence**, not as direct-line ancestry for the Gournay-en-Bray to Norfolk sequence. fileciteturn0file0

### Extract on surname formation

**Cleaned transcript from PDF p. 107, printed p. 21:**

> “Ascelin, son of this Robert, is known under several titles in ancient history: by that of Yvery, that of Gouel, and that of Percheval; and himself and his son by that also of Lupus, or Lupellus; and a younger son by that of Harpetre, from a manor of that name, in the county of Somerset, which he had for his portion, and which his descendants changed to Gournay.”

This passage matters because it gives the surname transition in the clearest genealogical form: **place-name portion → Harpetre identity → later Gournay identity**. For your working library, this is a good intake note under “surname caution”: Anderson is documenting a **secondary medieval adoption of Gournay**, not the original Norman place-name origin of the de Gournays of Gournay-en-Bray. fileciteturn0file0

### Extract on place-cluster in Somerset

**Cleaned transcript from PDF p. 164, printed p. 78:**

> “Among others, the following lands appear to have been given to Robert de Yvery and Ascelin his son, in the county of Somerset … Kary, Ferentone, Brismartin, Easton, Weston, [Laneverei?], the two Harpetres, Hetune, Tillebere, Biscopewide, Millescot, Babington, Stacewell, and Estide, besides a portion of land of Yvil, now known by the name of Ilchester.”

This is one of the best practical finds in the volume. Even though the line is collateral, the passage preserves a **bundle of Somerset place-names** that can be chased in Domesday, charter, and topographical sources. Some of those forms almost certainly need modernization and reconciliation, but this page is exactly the sort of intake material worth preserving verbatim. fileciteturn0file0

### Extract on the children of Ascelin

**Cleaned transcript from PDF pp. 277–279, printed pp. 191–193:**

> “Children of Ascelin Gouel de Perceval, Earl of Yvery, and of Isabella de Breteuil, his wife.  
> I. Robert, eldest son, Lord of Yvery.  
> II. William, called also Gouel de Perceval, surnamed Lupellus.  
> III. Roger, the third son, surnamed Balbus.  
> … John, surnamed de Harpetre, from whom the Barons of Harpetre descended, who at length assumed the name of Gournay.  
> Three sons more issued from this marriage of Ascelin with Isabella de Breteuil … but of these we do not certainly know the names. It is only presumed, that John de Yvery … was one of them. Hugh de Yvery … is likewise supposed to be another of them.”

This is the most important **child-count** passage in the book. It does not help the direct de Gournay line, but it absolutely does matter for the collateral Harpetre-Gournay line because Anderson is saying the couple had **more sons than the named core list**, and he even floats two conjectural identities: **John de Yvery** and **Hugh de Yvery**. Those are not conclusions; they are eighteenth-century hypotheses. But they are exactly the kind of hypotheses that deserve preservation in a research appendix. fileciteturn0file0

### Extract on the Stephen-period castle warfare

**Cleaned transcript from PDF pp. 287–288, printed pp. 199–200:**

> “This William Lupellus, and William, son of John de Harpetreu … each manned his castle, the one of Kary, the other of Harpetre … from whence, for a time, they ravaged the neighbouring country … [King Stephen] advanced against the castle of Kary … After which he became master of the castle of Harpetre, partly by surprise, and partly by assault.”

This is excellent narrative material. It places the Harpetre branch in the violent political context of King Stephen’s reign and shows Harpetre as no mere naming manor, but as a **defended castle-site tied to rebellion, siege, and local devastation**. That gives the Harpetre-Gournay branch social texture and atmosphere that can enrich a companion note even if it remains collateral to the direct line. fileciteturn0file0

## What this book actually adds

The most concrete genealogical addition is **not** “new proof for the direct Gournay line,” but rather a firmer eighteenth-century statement of the **collateral Harpetre-Gournay derivation**. Anderson says this in the epitome, in the surname chapter, in the child list, and in the pedigree table. The repetition makes it less likely to be a passing slip and more likely to be a central organizing assumption of the whole work. fileciteturn0file0

The second real addition is the **child problem** around Ascelin and Isabella de Breteuil. Anderson gives a named set—Robert, William/Lupellus, Roger Balbus, John de Harpetre, and a daughter married to Radulphus Rufus—but then explicitly says **three more sons existed whose names are not certainly known**, and that **John de Yvery** and **Hugh de Yvery** are only presumed candidates. This is useful because it preserves an older tradition of an **expanded sibling set**. That does not make the conjecture correct, but it does make it historically worth retaining as a lead. fileciteturn0file0

The third addition is geographical. The book ties the Harpetre/Gournay cadet branch to a broad Somerset holding pattern under the Yvery/Perceval umbrella, not just to Harpetre alone. That makes the branch feel less like a single-place offshoot and more like part of a regional baronial complex with Kary, Weston, Stawell, Ferentone, and other manors moving in the same orbit. fileciteturn0file0

## Place-name leads

Several of Anderson’s forms look strongly like names still visible in the Domesday and Somerset place index, though I would treat these as **research leads rather than final identifications**. The strongest ones are **Stacewell → Stawell**, **Yvil → Ilchester**, **Babington → Babington**, and likely **Ferentone → Farrington** and **Biscopewide → Bishopsworth**, with the “two Harpetres” very likely pointing to the later East and West Harptree pair. The modern Domesday index also shows places such as Stawell, Farrington [Gurney], Bishopsworth, Babington, Easton, Timsbury, and Ilchester in Somerset, which makes Anderson’s cluster feel geographically plausible even where exact form-to-form matching remains tentative. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn9search0turn9search1

For intake purposes, I would separate these into two buckets. The **stronger leads** are Kary/Cary, Weston, Stawell, Babington, Yvil/Ilchester, and Harpetre. The **weaker or unresolved forms** are Ferentone, Brismartin, Hetune, Tillebere, Biscopewide, and Estide, where the phonetic match is suggestive but not yet secure. The correct next step for those weak forms is Domesday folio comparison, not narrative reuse. fileciteturn0file0

One especially interesting collateral lead is the possibility that **Ferentone** may relate to **Farrington [Gurney]** in later naming history. I would not assert that as fact from this source alone, but it is exactly the sort of place-name resonance that deserves a carefully marked follow-up note in the Somerset geography appendix. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn9search1

## Narrative and atmosphere

Anderson’s tone is aristocratic, martial, and very much concerned with feudal dignity, but the underlying stories are vivid. Ascelin is described as “Lupus,” the Wolf, for the violence of his temper; his brother William’s assault on a woman at Pacey triggers a major Norman feud; Ascelin withstands a two-month siege at Breherval and wins Isabella de Breteuil in marriage; and the next generation appears amid rebellion, castle defense, and siege warfare in the reign of King Stephen. Even when the genealogy is dubious in places, the book is rich in the **feel** of baronial frontier life. fileciteturn0file0

That narrative value is real, but it needs strict labeling. These stories belong to the **Yvery/Perceval/Harpetre-Gournay world**, not automatically to the direct medieval de Gournays of Gournay-en-Bray and Norfolk. In your future fact-sheet companions, this material should therefore be tagged as **collateral context** unless you independently connect a specific event, person, or place to the direct line. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5search1

## Intake conclusions

For later fact-sheet population, I would preserve this source under a heading such as **“Collateral pre-Daniel printed witness: Anderson 1742”**. The reportable takeaways are straightforward. The book is strong evidence that an eighteenth-century genealogist understood **Harpetre-Gournay** as a cadet branch of the Yvery/Perceval house, descending from **John de Harpetre**, a younger son of Ascelin. It is also useful for the Somerset place-cluster and for the note that **additional sons of Ascelin were believed to have existed, though unnamed or conjectural**. It is **not**, on present reading, a source that materially advances the direct early de Gournay line now tracked in your project from Walter and Gerard back to the Gournay-en-Bray tradition. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5search1turn8search0

If I were turning this into research-companion intake notes, I would mark the source with four labels: **collateral line**, **surname-transition evidence**, **Somerset place leads**, and **Stephen-period narrative context**. I would also explicitly flag **John de Yvery** and **Hugh de Yvery** as Anderson-era conjectures only, and I would keep all Harpetre-to-Gournay material clearly separated from the older direct de Gournay line centered on Gournay-en-Bray, Norfolk, and the Norman baronage. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn5search1
