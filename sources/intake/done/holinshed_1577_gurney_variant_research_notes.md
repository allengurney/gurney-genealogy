## Method and limitations

The Internet Archive copy is the correct target, but it is a difficult scan for OCR: the IA metadata notes that this is **volume 2 only**, that it **lacks L3 of the index**, and that pages are **torn and tightly bound with some loss of text**. Its OCR was generated by Tesseract and detected as Fraktur/blackletter-script material. Because the user specifically warned that the stylized type makes OCR questionable, I treated IA full-text searching as insufficient by itself and used the Oxford Holinshed Project’s keyed 1577 transcription as the primary searchable text layer.

I searched broad permutations and near-permutations, then separated true/candidate Gurney-family/place hits from false positives.

# Detailed extracts and summaries

## 1. Norman Conquest lists: Hue de Gourney / Gournay; Gurnay and Gurney in Battle Abbey Roll

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 291  
**Forms:** `Hue de Gourney`, `Geneuay`, `Hue erle of Gournay`, `Gurnay`, `Gurney`  
**Type:** Conquest-era name list / Battle Abbey Roll tradition  

### Text extract

Holinshed introduces a catalogue of the noble captains and gentlemen who, according to William Tailleur’s book, assisted Duke William in the conquest. Within that list appears:

> “Hue de Gourney, alias Geneuay.”

A little later in the same catalogue appears:

> “Hue erle of Gournay.”

In the following Battle Abbey Roll section, under the letter G, the forms include:

> “Gurnay”  
> “Gurney”

The same list nearby also has `Gurry` and `Gurley`, but those are not treated here as Gurney variants without additional corroboration.

### Modern plain-English summary

Holinshed preserves two overlapping name traditions connected to the Norman Conquest. First, he lists a **Hugh/Hue de Gourney**, with an apparent alias rendered `Geneuay`. He also lists a **Hue, Earl of Gournay**. Then, in the Battle Abbey Roll material, both **Gurnay** and **Gurney** appear as separate or variant entries under G.

For Gurney/Gournay research, this is not direct evidence of a specific descent line, but it is useful evidence that Holinshed’s 1577 historical tradition associated the Gournay/Gurney forms with Norman Conquest-era aristocratic or military names.

### Research note

This is a useful citation for name-form continuity: `Gourney`, `Gournay`, `Gurnay`, and `Gurney` all appear in the same broad Conquest-list context. The alias `Geneuay` is unusual and should be checked against other editions/manuscript or printed-list traditions; it may reflect a transcription, typographic, or source corruption.

## 2. Hugh de Gourney’s castle burned by Henry, Duke of Normandy

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 364  
**Form:** `Hugh de Gourney`  
**Type:** Person / Norman territorial-military episode  

### Text extract

The surrounding story concerns Henry, Duke of Normandy, after marrying Eleanor, Duchess of Aquitaine. The French king makes war on Henry, and Henry responds militarily in Normandy and adjoining lands:

> “After this, Duke Henry entred into the territories of King Stephan, and brent certeine Castels that belonged to the sayde King, and also a Castell that belonged to Hugh de Gourney called Fert, but the French king tooke Vernon…”

### Modern plain-English summary

After Henry, Duke of Normandy, married Eleanor of Aquitaine, the French king opposed him and began military action. Henry retaliated by burning several castles, including one belonging to **Hugh de Gourney**, named `Fert` in the text. The French king, however, captured Vernon.

This is a territorial and political reference to Hugh de Gourney in the Angevin/Capetian conflict environment. It places Hugh’s property in the military geography of Normandy and adjacent contested territory.

### Research note

`Fert` may correspond to a “Ferté” place-name, but the exact identification should be checked against other editions or medieval Normandy studies before assigning it to a modern location.

## 3. Hugh de Gourney listed among famous Holy Land warriors under Richard I

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 473  
**Form:** `Hugh de Gourney`  
**Type:** Person / crusade reputation list  

### Text extract

After describing Richard I’s successful seizure of a caravan during the Third Crusade, Holinshed adds a list of men whose exploits in the Holy Land were considered famous:

> “Famous men for theyr valiancie. The famous actes of these and other the like valiants, which they atchieved in those holy lande warres, deserue of righte to be registred in bookes of perpetuall memorie…”

The list includes:

> “Hugh de Gourney”

### Modern plain-English summary

Holinshed groups Hugh de Gourney among the notable men whose valor in the Holy Land wars deserved lasting remembrance. This is not a detailed biographical account, but it is a reputational mention: Hugh is treated as part of the notable crusading-warrior class associated with Richard I’s campaign.

### Research note

This is potentially valuable if building a Gournay/Gurney narrative around the crusading generation, but it should be corroborated in more specialized sources on Richard I’s crusade and Norman/Breton/English contingents.

## 4. Robert Earl of Leicester captured in the lands of Hugh Gourney

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 473  
**Form:** `Hugh Gourney`  
**Type:** Person/place marker / Richard I’s Normandy war  

### Text extract

In a later Richard I context, after the king came into Normandy, the Earl of Leicester is described as moving imprudently through Hugh Gourney’s territory:

> “In the same season Robert Erle of Leicester, with a small companye of Souldiers, came unto the Castel of Passey, which Castel hee wanne… and as he adventured himselfe somewhat unadvysedly in the landes of Hugh Gourney, he was taken prisoner…”

### Modern plain-English summary

Robert, Earl of Leicester, captures or gains control of Passy, then rides with too small a force into the lands of **Hugh Gourney**. He is captured there. King Richard later pursues the French king near Vendôme.

Here, `Hugh Gourney` functions as a territorial marker. The passage implies Hugh’s lands were a recognized zone of military movement during Richard I’s conflict with France.

### Research note

This should be cross-checked against the 1587 Holinshed edition and French/Norman campaign chronologies, because the passage may help place Hugh Gourney’s territorial role in late-12th-century Normandy politics.

## 5. `Gorney` taken by Breton forces in King John’s conflict

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 541  
**Form:** `Gorney`  
**Type:** Place  

### Text extract

The surrounding narrative concerns King John, French King Philip, and Arthur of Brittany. In one campaign, Breton forces take several towns:

> “In an other part, an army of Britaines with great diligence wanne the Townes of Gorney, Butenant and Gensolin…”

The text then says the victorious forces went on toward Angers and took the city.

### Modern plain-English summary

During the early King John conflict involving Arthur of Brittany and King Philip of France, Breton forces captured several towns, including **Gorney**. This is almost certainly a place-name reference rather than a person.

### Research note

Because Holinshed also uses `Gourney` as a Norman place-name elsewhere, this `Gorney` spelling may be an alternate or corrupt spelling of a related place, but it should not be assumed to mean Gournay-en-Bray without geographical corroboration.

## 6. French king wins Gourney in the war against King John

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 541/553 region  
**Form:** `Gourney`  
**Type:** Place  

### Text extract

In the King John war narrative, after the French king takes or besieges several Norman places, Holinshed states:

> “After this… the French K. wan Gourney, and then returning to the Citie of Mans, he appoynted Arthur duke of Britaine, to go into Poictou…”

### Modern plain-English summary

King Philip’s campaign against John continues through Normandy and adjacent territories. After securing other places, the French king captures **Gourney**, then turns his attention toward Le Mans and sends Arthur of Brittany into Poitou.

This is a place hit and is important because Gournay/Gourney appears in the geography of John’s loss of continental territory.

### Research note

This likely relates to Gournay-en-Bray or a similarly named Norman/Vexin place. It should be mapped against the sequence of French gains in Normandy during 1202–1204.

## 7. Hugh de Gourney defends Castle Galliard, then revolts from King John

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 557 region  
**Forms:** `Hugh de Gourney`, `Hugh Gourney`  
**Type:** Person / military captain / political defection  

### Text extract

The French king besieges Castle Galliard. Holinshed credits Hugh de Gourney with a strong defense:

> “...he went to Castel Galiard, which he besieged; and though by the high valiancie of Hugh de Gourney the Captayne there, the French men were manfully beaten backe, and kept out for a month and more…”

Despite this defense, the castle is eventually delivered to the French king. Holinshed then adds:

> “Hugh de Gourney revolteth from king John. And in the ende the sayde Hugh Gourney reuolted from his obedience, delivering also the Castell of Mountforte unto the Frenche King…”

### Modern plain-English summary

Hugh de Gourney is described as captain of **Castle Galliard** during a French siege. He defends it vigorously and keeps the French out for more than a month. Eventually, however, the fortress falls. Holinshed then says Hugh Gourney ultimately abandons King John’s side and delivers **Mountfort** to the French king.

This is one of the most important Gourney references in the document because it gives a compact narrative arc: Hugh is a capable military defender, but later defects from John to the French.

### Research note

This passage is highly relevant to any narrative about the Gournay family’s position during the collapse of John’s continental power. It should be compared with sources on Château Gaillard and the Anglo-Norman baronage during 1202–1204.

## 8. Thomas Gourney and the imprisonment/murder of Edward II

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 846  
**Form:** `Thomas Gourney`  
**Type:** Person / Edward II deposition and murder narrative  

### Text extract

After Edward II’s deposition, he is kept under guard. Holinshed says Berkeley treated him too kindly, so Berkeley was removed and Thomas Gourney substituted:

> “But for so much as the Lord Berkeley used the King more courteously than his adversaries wished him to do… they removed him from the custodie of his persone, and put others in his place, who studied to deale more roughly with him…”

Holinshed then names the replacement:

> “...sir Thomas Gourney appointed in his stead…”

Gourney and Matreuers move Edward secretly from place to place and back to Berkeley. Holinshed then narrates the murder by night at Berkeley Castle. Afterward, he says the Queen, the Bishop, and others attempted to conceal the crime:

> “...proclaimed with all speede, that no man upon paine of death should be so hardy, as to accuse any of them that were knowen to be the doers thereof…”

Thomas Gourney flees:

> “Thomas Gourney flying into Marseilles, there remained as a banished man…”

He is later captured and killed before he can expose higher responsibility.

### Modern plain-English summary

Thomas Gourney appears in Holinshed’s account of Edward II’s final captivity and murder. Berkeley is removed because he treats the deposed king too gently. Thomas Gourney replaces him as one of the harsher keepers. Along with Lord Matreuers, he moves Edward from place to place and ultimately brings him back to Berkeley, where Edward is murdered at night.

Holinshed treats Gourney as one of the direct instruments of the murder, while also implying that higher-ranking people wanted the truth suppressed. Gourney flees to Marseille, is eventually captured, and is killed at sea on the way back to England so that he cannot accuse the chief instigators.

### Research note

This is a major historical Thomas Gourney reference, but it is politically charged and should not be treated as neutral fact without comparison to chronicles and modern Edward II historiography. The spelling `Gourney` is clear in this Holinshed witness.

## 9. Sir Mathew Gourney commands a battle division in Brittany

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 884  
**Form:** `sir Mathew Gourney`  
**Type:** Person / military commander

In a Brittany campaign involving Mountford and Charles of Blois, the forces are divided into several “battles,” or divisions. The second division is assigned to three commanders:

> “The second battaile was led by Sir Oliver de Clisson, Sir Eustace Daubreticourt, and sir Mathew Gourney.”

The first is under Sir Robert Knolles; the third under Sir John Chandos; Mountford keeps a rear guard.

### Modern plain-English summary

Sir Mathew Gourney is one of three named commanders leading the second division of Mountford’s army in Brittany. The passage places him among important English-aligned military figures such as Knolles, Clisson, Daubreticourt, and Chandos.

### Research note

This likely refers to the Battle of Auray context in the Breton succession conflict. It is a useful military-career reference for Sir Matthew Gournay/Gurney.

## 10. Sir Mathew Gourney sent to Portugal

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 1003  
**Form:** `sir Mathew Gourney`  
**Type:** Person / expeditionary force  

### Text extract

Holinshed describes an English force sent to support the King of Portugal against the King of Castile:

> “The Earle of Cambridge, the Lorde William de Beauchampe, the Lorde Botreux, and sir Mathew Gourney, were sent into Portugall with fiue hundred armed men, and fiue hundred Archers, to aide the king of Portugall against the king of Castile…”

The passage ties this to the Duke of Lancaster’s claim through his wife Constance, daughter of Peter, King of Castile and León.

### Modern plain-English summary

Sir Mathew Gourney is named as one of the English leaders sent with 500 men-at-arms and 500 archers to aid Portugal against Castile. The broader political context is John of Gaunt’s claim to Castile and León through his wife Constance.

### Research note

This is another useful military-service reference for Sir Matthew Gournay/Gurney, placing him in Iberian operations connected to John of Gaunt’s dynastic ambitions.

## 11. Sir Mathew Gourney in a procedural/legal role before the king

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 1118  
**Form:** `Sir Mathew Gourney`  
**Type:** Person / parliamentary or judicial proceeding  

### Text extract

In a proceeding involving the Earl of Salisbury and Lord Morley, Salisbury tries to have a protestation entered. Lord Morley objects that no such protestation was included in Salisbury’s first answer. Holinshed then has Sir Mathew Gourney speak:

> “Sir Mathew Gourney sitting under the king, said to the Erle of Salisbury, that sith hee had made no such protestation in his first answer… he was now past the aduantage thereof…”

Gourney asks whether Salisbury has anything else to say.

### Modern plain-English summary

Sir Mathew Gourney appears not as a battlefield figure but as someone participating in a formal legal or parliamentary procedure. He tells the Earl of Salisbury that he cannot retroactively add a protestation that was not included in his initial answer. In modern terms, Salisbury has missed the procedural opportunity to preserve that point.

### Research note

This passage suggests Sir Matthew had standing or authority in a high-level proceeding. It may be useful for understanding his later-life status and public role beyond military service.

## 12. Gourney yields to Henry V’s English obedience; Sir Gilbert Umfreville made captain

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 1164  
**Form:** `Gourney`  
**Type:** Place / Henry V Normandy campaign  

### Text extract

During Henry V’s Normandy campaign, after the fall or submission of Gisors and Castle Galliard, other towns and castles in the Vexin yield:

> “...divers other Townes and Castels within the Countrey of Veulquessine yielded themselves to the obedience of the Englishmen, as Gourney, Chaumount, Neaufie, Dangu…”

Holinshed then notes that of Gourney:

> “...sir Gilbert Umfrevile was made Captaine…”

### Modern plain-English summary

In the aftermath of Henry V’s Normandy advances, Gourney submits to English control along with several other places in the Vexin region. Sir Gilbert Umfreville is appointed captain of Gourney.

### Research note

This is a place reference rather than a Gurney-family reference. It is useful for mapping the later medieval military significance of Gourney/Gournay in Normandy/Vexin.

## 13. Sir Thomas Kiriell departs from Gourney in Normandy and defeats French forces

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 1219  
**Form:** `Gourney in Normandie`  
**Type:** Place / English military base  

### Text extract

During Henry VI-era operations, Sir Thomas Kiriell leaves Gourney with English troops:

> “Sir Thomas Kiriell departed from Gourney in Normandie, with four hundred Englishmen… and came before the Towne of Cleremont…”

The surrounding account says he encountered French forces under the Earl of Clermont. The English archers repelled the French, many were killed or captured, and Clermont withdrew.

### Modern plain-English summary

Gourney functions as an English-held base or staging point in Normandy. Sir Thomas Kiriell marches from Gourney with 400 Englishmen, raids enemy-held territory near Beauvais and Clermont, and defeats a larger French force through effective archery and field tactics.

### Research note

This is another valuable Gourney place reference for the English occupation of Normandy after Henry V and during Henry VI’s reign.

## 14. Later Normandy campaign references: Warwick before Gourney; Arundel comes to Gourney before Gerberoy

**Locus:** 1577 Holinshed, Vol. 4, p. 1219  
**Form:** `Gourney`  
**Type:** Place / military operations  

### Text extract

Holinshed records a skirmish involving the Earl of Warwick:

> “Richard Beauchampe Earle of Warwicke… had a great skyrmishe before the towne of Gourney…”

He defeats or repulses the enemy and captures many horsemen.

Later, after the Earl of Arundel captures several places, he proceeds to Gourney:

> “...the Erle of Arundel… came to Gourney, where hee heard tell how there was a Castell newly fortified at Gerboroy…”

The following narrative explains that Arundel attacks Gerberoy, faces stronger resistance than expected, is wounded by artillery, captured, and later dies at Beauvais.

### Modern plain-English summary

Gourney appears repeatedly as a military location in the English-French conflict in Normandy. Warwick fights successfully near the town. Later, Arundel comes to Gourney and learns about the newly fortified Gerberoy. That intelligence leads him into the Gerberoy campaign, where he is wounded and captured, then dies.

### Research note

This cluster is important for a place-history narrative of Gourney/Gournay during the later Hundred Years’ War. It does not directly identify a Gurney-family person, but it reinforces the strategic importance of the place-name.

# Consolidated plain-English research summary

The Holinshed 1577 target contains two broad kinds of Gurney/Gourney/Gournay material:

1. **People in medieval military and political history**
   - `Hue/Hugh de Gourney` appears in Conquest-list tradition, Norman-Angevin conflict, crusade reputation material, and King John-era Normandy war.
   - `Thomas Gourney` appears prominently in the Edward II murder narrative.
   - `Sir Mathew Gourney` appears as a significant later medieval military figure and, in one passage, as a person acting in a procedural/legal capacity near the king.

2. **Places named Gourney/Gorney/Gournay-like forms**
   - `Gorney` / `Gourney` appears in the King John continental-war geography.
   - `Gourney` appears repeatedly in Henry V/Henry VI-era Normandy and Vexin campaign contexts.

For genealogy purposes, Holinshed does **not** provide a direct line of descent to later English or American Gurneys. Its value is contextual and historiographical: it confirms that by 1577, the printed English chronicle tradition preserved multiple spellings of the Gourney/Gournay/Gurney name cluster across Norman, crusading, royal, and Hundred Years’ War contexts.

The strongest family-name/person hits are:

- **Hue/Hugh de Gourney / Gournay** — Conquest-list and 12th–13th-century Norman-war references.
- **Thomas Gourney** — Edward II murder narrative.
- **Sir Mathew Gourney** — 14th-century military and legal/procedural references.

The strongest place-name hits are:

- **Gourney/Gorney** in King John-era Normandy/Brittany conflict.
- **Gourney** in Henry V/Henry VI-era Normandy/Vexin campaigns.
