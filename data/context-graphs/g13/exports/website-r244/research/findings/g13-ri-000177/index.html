The identification is reached by elimination and circumstantial fit, and predicts its own thin record

John Gurney's parentage is settled by elimination and circumstantial fit, not by a document: no record made in his lifetime names his father. Two things make this elimination stronger than most colonial origin attributions. Every rival was actively disproved rather than left unexamined - Candidate A by continuous English residence to 1653, Candidate C by a family of the wrong shape, Candidate D by a 1662 London hearth-tax entry made in the year the colonial John lay dying, the Earsham testator by dying in England in 1638, and the 1636 Newgate apprentice by mismatched family status and an unbendable chronology. And the colonial record shows exactly one adult Gurney household in Massachusetts before 1660: the Dorchester "Gurneys" are Gurnells, the Providence John and Sarah are a later unconnected pair, and there is no second emigrant John. The weakness mirrors the strength. Candidate B predicts a man who would leave almost no English paper - a motherless son raised by his mother's kin, trained outside both guild registers that would have recorded him - so the absence of a confirming document is what the hypothesis expects and cannot be counted against it.
