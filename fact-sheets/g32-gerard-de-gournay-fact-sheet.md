<section class="fact-panel fact-panel-highlights" id="highlights">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Highlights</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Joined the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Crusade">First Crusade</a> — survived it — then died on a second pilgrimage.</strong> Gerard sailed from Normandy in September 1096 with <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Curthose">Robert Curthose</a>'s contingent, which included <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odo_of_Bayeux">Bishop Odo of Bayeux</a> and <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_II,_Count_of_Blois">Stephen de Blois</a>. They wintered in Calabria with <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemond_I_of_Antioch">Bohemond</a>, who inspected their heraldic badges — an early glimpse of the emerging system of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry">arms</a>. Gerard fought at the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Nicaea">siege of Nicaea</a> (June 1097), survived the terrible march across "<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrygia">Burnt Phrygia</a>" where men and hawks died of thirst, and was present at the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(1099)">fall of Jerusalem</a> in July 1099. He returned home around 1100, but later set out again with his wife Edith — and died *en route*: "*Hierosolymam petens in ipso itinere mortuus est*." <sup class="fn"><a href="#n5" id="ref-5">5</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>Married the daughter of England's wealthiest earl — and his seal survives.</strong> The Warenne marriage brought Norfolk manors and the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caister-on-Sea">Caister-on-Sea</a> barony into the Gournay family. Gerard's own seal — "Signum Girardi de Gornaco" — is preserved in the Cartulary of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Trinity_Abbey,_Rouen">La Trinité de Rouen</a>, tangible physical evidence of his authority as a Norman lord. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n4" id="ref-4b">4</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>His daughter *la belle Gondrée* married into the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mowbray_family">Mowbray</a> line.</strong> Gundred de Gournay married <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_d%27Aubigny">Nigel de Albini</a> in 1119 (the wedding arranged by <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_I_of_England">Henry I</a> himself). Through Nigel and Gondrée, the Gournay blood entered the Mowbray family — the future <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Norfolk">Dukes of Norfolk</a>. Gondrée also patronised <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byland_Abbey">Byland Abbey</a> and <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rievaulx_Abbey">Rievaulx Abbey</a>, whose noble ruins still stand in Yorkshire. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n7" id="ref-7">7</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>A formidable lord — "an unsafe man to meddle with."</strong> Gerard secured his castles for <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_II_of_England">William Rufus</a> against Robert Curthose: in 1089–90, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orderic_Vitalis">Orderic Vitalis</a> records that Gerard delivered "Gornacum et Firmitatem et Goisleni Fontem" — Gournay, La Ferté-en-Bray, and Gaillefontaine — to the king in a coordinated frontier-triad action commanding the head of the Bresle valley. When the Count of Évreux tried to claim one of his residences, Hannay noted, Gerard's "power and valour made him an unsafe man to meddle with" — and the Count backed down. A surviving charter from the Abbey of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint-Wandrille">Saint-Wandrille</a> shows him exercising seigneurial authority: nothing could be granted from his lordship without his consent. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6">6</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>JUNCTION POINT of the two lines.</strong> Gerard's eldest son <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_IV_de_Gournay">Hugh IV</a> continued the main Norman baron line — the most powerful branch, which held Gournay-en-Bray until it went extinct in 1235. Gerard's younger son Walter (G31) became the ancestor of the Norfolk junior branch, from which all subsequent English Gurneys — including the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurney_family">banking Gurneys</a> — and through Francis Gurney's son John Gurney-1, the American Gurneys, descend. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n8" id="ref-8">8</a></sup></li>
</ul>
</section>

<section class="fact-section fact-narrative" id="narrative">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Narrative</h2>

Gerard de Gournay was born into a world already transformed by the Conquest that his father and grandfather had helped achieve. By c. 1040, the Gournays were lords of a frontier fortress in Normandy with English manors attached — a dual-realm family of the kind that the Conquest had created wholesale. Gerard first appears in 1082, witnessing the charter by which <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Conqueror">the Conqueror</a> and <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matilda_of_Flanders">Matilda</a> established the nunnery of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbaye_aux_Dames,_Caen">the Holy Trinity at Caen</a> — probably very young, but already present in the political world.

His marriage to Edith de Warenne was a union of two of the most powerful Norman families. The <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Warenne_family">Warennes</a> had arrived with the Conqueror and received lands so extensive that William de Warenne, 1st Earl of Surrey, was reckoned the wealthiest layman in England after the king. With Edith came Norfolk manors, and the barony centred on <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caister-on-Sea">Caister-by-the-Sea</a> became the Gournay *caput baroniae* in England. Old shields bearing *pure sable* — the ancient Gournay arms — were visible in St. Nicholas's church at <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Yarmouth">Great Yarmouth</a> for centuries.

Gerard was a formidable local lord as well as a warrior. A charter from the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_of_Saint-Wandrille">Abbey of Saint-Wandrille</a> preserves his formal consent (*annuente Girardo de Gournai*) for a land grant — nothing could leave his lordship without his approval. When <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_de_Breteuil">William de Breteuil</a>, lord of Pont-Saint-Pierre, sheltered one of Gerard's rebellious knights, the resulting dispute escalated until the townspeople feared their homes would be burned and began moving their possessions to safety. Gerard played power politics at the Norman regional level as vigorously as he did on the international stage.

Then, in September 1096, Gerard departed for the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land">Holy Land</a> with Robert Curthose's contingent. The company that gathered was extraordinary: <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odo_of_Bayeux">Bishop Odo of Bayeux</a>, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_II,_Count_of_Blois">Stephen de Blois</a>, and a host of Norman lords. Wintering in <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calabria">Calabria</a>, they fell in with <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemond_I_of_Antioch">Bohemond of Taranto</a>, who inspected their heraldic blazons — an episode that places the Gournay name in one of the earliest documented discussions of the nascent system of arms. Gerard fought at the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Nicaea">siege of Nicaea</a>, survived the murderous march through <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrygia">Phrygia</a>, and was present when <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(1099)">Jerusalem fell</a> to the Crusaders on 15 July 1099.

He returned home around 1100, but the pilgrimage impulse drew him back. He and Edith set out together for Jerusalem — and Gerard died on the journey: "*Hierosolymam petens in ipso itinere mortuus est.*" A common genealogical confusion arises here: Gerard's eldest son, also named Gerard, died *vitae patris* — during his father's lifetime — possibly at the siege of Nicaea in 1097. Some secondary sources conflate the two men's deaths, assigning the younger Gerard's 1097 date to the father. Daniel Gurney's chronology, which has the elder Gerard surviving to return and then dying before 1104 on a second pilgrimage, is internally consistent and should be preferred.

Gerard's death left the great fief divided. His eldest surviving son Hugh IV inherited the senior barony — Gournay-en-Bray, the Norman honour, the English estates — and continued the main baronial line. His youngest son Walter received a younger son's portion of the Norfolk and Suffolk manors. From this seemingly minor partition, all subsequent English and American Gurneys descend. Gerard is the last direct-line ancestor to hold Gournay-en-Bray itself.
</section>
