<section class="fact-panel fact-panel-highlights" id="highlights">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Highlights</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>He won West Barsham — and made it the family's home for nearly three centuries.</strong> Edmund married into the Wauncy family, who had held the manor of West Barsham for some three hundred years, since the time of the Domesday survey of 1086. When his wife's brother and infant nephew both died within a few years, the whole estate passed to Edmund. From his death in 1387 until the family's male line died out in 1661, the Gurneys were the Gurneys of West Barsham; the hall still standing there — its surviving Tudor wing now a protected historic building — is the house his descendants lived in and enlarged. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6">6</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>He ran East Anglia for the most powerful man in England.</strong> John of Gaunt — Duke of Lancaster, son of one king and father of the next — kept Edmund on as steward of his estates across East Anglia from 1372 until Edmund's death. Edmund belonged to the small circle of lawyers through whom the Duke governed his Norfolk lands, and his services were prized widely enough that even the great Broadland abbey of St Benet's Holme paid him a fee. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n7" id="ref-7">7</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n16" id="ref-16">16</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>Two towns paid to keep him on retainer at once.</strong> Both Norwich and Bishop's Lynn (today King's Lynn) hired Edmund as their standing legal counsel — Norwich paying him a yearly fee alongside his colleague Edmund de Clipesby. Few men in East Anglia were more sought-after for legal advice. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n8" id="ref-8">8</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>His coat of arms still survives in stained glass.</strong> Edmund bore the family arms — a red scalloped (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engrailed">engrailed</a>) cross on silver — and after his marriage combined them with his wife's arms on a single shield split down the middle: his cross on one half, her three silver gloves on a red field on the other, the customary way a married couple's two families were shown together. That combined shield could still be seen, centuries later, in the windows of the family's house in Norwich and of Denton church in Norfolk. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n9" id="ref-9">9</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>He turns up again and again in the king's records.</strong> Edmund appears across at least fifteen separate royal commissions and inquiries — keeping the peace as a justice, investigating customs fraud and piracy off the Norfolk coast, arbitrating between rival religious houses, serving as a special commissioner for the queen's manor, and more. He is one of the most thoroughly documented people in the whole family line. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n10" id="ref-10">10</a></sup></li>
</ul>
</section>

<section class="fact-section fact-narrative" id="narrative">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Narrative</h2>

Edmund Gournay transformed the family's standing in Norfolk. His forebears at Harpley had been respectable minor gentry — country knights and squires of modest means. Edmund became something far larger: the most sought-after lawyer in the county. Norwich kept him on a yearly retainer — its treasurers paid him twenty shillings a year, listed in the same breath as his colleague Edmund de Clipesby — the town of Bishop's Lynn sought his counsel too, and, most striking of all, John of Gaunt himself made him steward of the Duke's estates across East Anglia, a post he held almost without a break from 1372 until he died.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n8" id="ref-8b">8</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n7" id="ref-7b">7</a></sup>

That tie to Gaunt is the key to Edmund's rise. In the 1370s and 1380s the Duke of Lancaster — uncle to the young King Richard II — was the strongest subject in the kingdom, and helping run his Norfolk lands placed Edmund among the handful of lawyers and stewards who made that vast machine work. He worked shoulder to shoulder with men like John Winter and his fellow Norwich counsel Edmund de Clipesby — the professional class on whom dukes, bishops, and towns alike leaned.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n7" id="ref-7c">7</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n16" id="ref-16b">16</a></sup>

That trust showed in the work that came to him. The crown named him to its commissions again and again — to keep the peace as a justice, to root out customs fraud, to settle a quarrel between the prior of Norwich and the prioress of Carrow, to look into piracy in Norfolk waters.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n10" id="ref-10b">10</a></sup> And when the county's leading families needed someone they could trust to hold their lands — a common arrangement, where a man held property in another's interest — they turned to Edmund. He held the manors of Feltwell and Mundford for Sir John de Plays;<sup class="fn"><a href="#n19" id="ref-19">19</a></sup> he was one of the powerful de la Pole family's trustees for a manor near Norwich;<sup class="fn"><a href="#n18" id="ref-18">18</a></sup> he joined a circle of knights — the Calthorpes, Haleses, and Sheltons — to grant land to Walsingham Priory;<sup class="fn"><a href="#n20" id="ref-20">20</a></sup> and he helped settle the great manor of Hingham on the Morley lords.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n21" id="ref-21">21</a></sup>

His most important personal step was his marriage to Katherine de Wauncy. Her family had held West Barsham for some three hundred years, and had once moved in royal circles — an earlier Robert de Wauncy witnessed charters for Richard the Lionheart at the great castle of Château-Gaillard in 1198 and was trusted with the care of two royal princesses at Rouen.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n17" id="ref-17">17</a></sup> Then came the run of deaths that changed Edmund's fortunes: Katherine's brother Sir Edmund de Wauncy died in 1372, leaving a seven-year-old son who did not long outlive him, and the whole West Barsham estate passed to Edmund through his wife. He moved the family's main seat there from Harpley. Her father had already settled a hundred marks a year on the couple in 1357, and by 1375 Edmund was granting long leases of the manor under his own seal — the red scalloped cross.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n5" id="ref-5b">5</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6b">6</a></sup>

Edmund died in 1387 and was buried, as he asked, in the church of the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin at West Barsham. His will paints a vivid scene: thirteen poor men robed in white were to stand around his body holding torches, the family in mourning black beside them, and on the day of the funeral the gentry and "good men" of the surrounding country were to gather at West Barsham to be "honourably fed" — a public show of the family's new standing. The will also carries a striking clause of conscience: he directed his heirs to make amends to anyone he had wronged — anyone he had cheated of land, injured, or extorted — perhaps the kind of reckoning a powerful lawyer expected to owe.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n15" id="ref-15">15</a></sup> His eldest son, Sir John V, succeeded him and built on his legal foundations; his second son, Robert — the direct ancestor — would inherit West Barsham in his turn, when Sir John's own line died out.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n11" id="ref-11b">11</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n12" id="ref-12b">12</a></sup>
</section>
