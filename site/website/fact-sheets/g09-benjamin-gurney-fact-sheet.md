<section class="fact-panel fact-panel-highlights" id="highlights">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Highlights</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Born of an unmarried liaison and raised by an aunt.</strong> His father Benjamin G10 fathered him by Jane Harden before later marrying Sarah Morse. Benjamin was raised by his mother's sister and named in his maternal grandfather John Harden's will of 1751 — a will that places him concretely in the Plymouth County extended family. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n5" id="ref-5">5</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>Sold Abington land and bought into a frontier town in 1770.</strong> In June 1770 Benjamin sold his Abington land and, by Springfield, Massachusetts records of 5 November 1770, purchased land in "Town No. 5" — soon to be incorporated as Cummington — with Silas Reed. The move opens the Cummington chapter of the family. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6">6</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>Exchanged farms with Philip Shaw in 1787.</strong> Foster &amp; Streeter's <em>"Only One Cummington"</em> (1974, p. 390) records Benjamin's 1787 farm exchange with Philip Shaw — a small but specific transaction that places him concretely in the town's land record. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n7" id="ref-7">7</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>The 1790 census shows a working farmstead at full extent.</strong> Six dependents in the household (3 males under 16, 3 females) is consistent with a household of small children and a wife — exactly what is expected in his late fifties with a young second family. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n3" id="ref-3b">3</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>Buried in Dawes Cemetery, Cummington.</strong> One of the few direct-line ancestors in the Massachusetts colonial generations whose burial place is firmly recorded. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n2" id="ref-2c">2</a></sup></li>
</ul>

<section class="fact-section fact-narrative" id="narrative">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Narrative</h2>

<p>Benjamin Gurney is the man who moved the family from old Plymouth County into the Massachusetts hill country. He was baptized at Abington on 30 May 1730 — the son of his father's pre-marital liaison with Jane Harden — and was raised by his maternal aunt rather than in his father's later household with Sarah Morse. His maternal grandfather John Harden, who knew of the boy's situation, named him in a 1751 will, the first surviving documentary fix on his identity.</p>

<p>The decisive year of his life was 1770. In June he sold his Abington land. By 5 November he had bought into "Town No. 5," soon to be incorporated as Cummington, with Silas Reed — the Springfield, Massachusetts records preserve the transaction. Cummington at that date was barely a settled place: the Bryant family, who would produce the poet William Cullen Bryant, were establishing themselves nearby in the same period. Benjamin took up farming there and remained for the rest of his life. In 1787 he exchanged farms with Philip Shaw — a small detail preserved by Foster &amp; Streeter's <em>"Only One Cummington"</em> — and in the 1790 federal census he appears as head of a six-person household, structure 3-0-3.</p>

<p>He married twice — first Elizabeth Harden, then Mercy Noyes — and his son Amos (G8), the bridge to the New York chapter, was born in 1770, almost exactly at the moment of the Cummington move. Benjamin died at Cummington on 28 September 1805 and was buried in Dawes Cemetery, where his marker survives. He is the last firmly-documented Cummington-resident generation in the direct line: his son Amos remained in Cummington only to about 1802, and the family's western Massachusetts chapter effectively ends with Benjamin's death.</p>
</section>
