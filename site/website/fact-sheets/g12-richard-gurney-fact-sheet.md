<section class="fact-panel fact-panel-highlights" id="highlights">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Highlights</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>A six-acre homestead by Great Pond.</strong> In 1683 the Weymouth town meeting voted Richard six acres of common land on the west side of Great Pond "to build a house &amp; fence to dwell thereon with his family" — the lot recorded at his death as "a dwelling house &amp; barn with about six acres of land adjoining," set with the plain goods of a working farmer. The family's earlier Weymouth land — "the East field," "the mill field," "the east side of Great Pond" — had been his father's, granted in 1651–52 and soon passed to others. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n5" id="ref-5">5</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n10" id="ref-10">10</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>King Philip's War struck the wider family hard.</strong> Richard's brother John was among the dead in the Mendon massacre of 14 July 1675 — the first major bloodshed of the war — and a second brother, Peter, a soldier in Captain Isaac Johnson's company, was killed in the fighting late in 1676. Richard himself stayed at Weymouth; his teenage son Zachariah was caught up in the 1676 musters but survived, and all his children outlived the war. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6">6</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n9" id="ref-9b">9</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>Admitted Freeman in 1681.</strong> Becoming a Freeman of Massachusetts Bay required formal church membership and entitled the holder to vote and hold public office. Richard's admission on 12 October 1681, after some four decades in Weymouth, marks his place in the community as a recognized adult member of the New England covenant. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n3" id="ref-3b">3</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>The record that fixed his death date.</strong> Richard died without a will in the autumn of 1691, and the Boston court's grant of authority over his estate to his sons Richard and Zachariah — the eldest son, John, declining the task — is the document that finally separated him from the October 1719 death long misattributed to him from his same-named son. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n2" id="ref-2c">2</a></sup></li>
</ul>
</section>

<section class="fact-section fact-narrative" id="narrative">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Narrative</h2>

<p>Richard Gurney is the second-generation American Gurney — brought from England as a small child by his father, John Gurney (G13), the colonial emigrant first recorded at Weymouth in 1641. Richard's adult life unfolded entirely at Weymouth, on the south shore of Massachusetts Bay. In 1681 he was admitted a Freeman of Massachusetts Bay, and in 1683 the town meeting granted him six acres on the west side of Great Pond "to build a house &amp; fence" — a homestead local tradition placed toward East Street and Old Spain, running down to Back River. He married Rebecca Taylor — named in her father's will, proved 1688 — and the family lived through the convulsive decades of King Philip's War and its aftermath. The family's earliest Weymouth land was not his but his father's: the "East field," "mill field," and "east side of Great Pond" grants of 1651–52, which John Gurney never settled and which passed to others.</p>

<p>The most personal mark of those years on the family is bleak. His brother John was among the dead at the Mendon massacre of 14 July 1675 — the first major violence of King Philip's War, when Nipmuc fighters attacked the small frontier town of Mendon and killed several inhabitants — and another brother, Peter, a soldier in Captain Isaac Johnson's company, was killed in the war late in 1676.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6b">6</a></sup> The war reached the next generation too: Richard's teenage son Zachariah was caught up in the 1676 musters — credited for service under Captain Johnson's company, and listed among the Weymouth men who failed to answer Captain John Holbrooke's call to muster at Concord that April — but he came home.<sup class="fn"><a href="#n9" id="ref-9c">9</a></sup> Richard himself remained at Weymouth through it all, neither soldier nor displaced. He died intestate in October 1691, his estate administered by two of his sons, Richard and Zachariah, after the eldest son John declined the duty — the very record that finally untangles Richard from the 1719 death date long misattributed to him from his same-named son.</p>

<p>Richard is the hinge between the family's first foothold at Weymouth and the Plymouth County farming country where the line settled for the next century. His youngest son, Benjamin (G11), carried the Gurney name south to the farms along the Abington–Bridgewater line and on to Middleboro; from Benjamin the line ran through Cummington, into New York, and eventually further west. The land Benjamin worked there he bought for himself a generation later — it was never Richard's — but without Richard's line of descent none of those later American chapters would exist. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n8" id="ref-8b">8</a></sup></p>
</section>
