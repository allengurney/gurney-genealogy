<section class="fact-panel fact-panel-highlights" id="highlights">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Highlights</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>One of the hardest ancestors to identify.</strong> The genealogist Daniel Gurney could only write that Edmund Gournay had "a second son, whom we believe was named Robert," resting on a single herald's pedigree drawn up in 1622. Yet a modern review of Norfolk's own land and court records reveals a Robert Gurnay around Little Cressingham — in a 1405 land settlement and on five local juries between 1400 and 1406 — the only man of that uncommon name in the county in his generation, and in his family's own region. On the convergence of name, date, and place, the pedigree's careful research stands behind a real and locally-rooted man. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n5" id="ref-5">5</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n12" id="ref-12">12</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n18" id="ref-18">18</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n19" id="ref-19">19</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>A second son — which is why he is lightly recorded.</strong> In Robert's England the eldest son took the land, the title, the offices, and the paperwork they generated. His elder brother John got West Barsham and a public career; Robert, the younger, was provided for quietly and left only a faint trail. The thinness of his record is exactly what you would expect of a gentry second son — not a gap in the story, but the normal shape of the evidence. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n3" id="ref-3">3</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>The whole later family descends through him — by an accident of death.</strong> His brother Sir John Gurney V seemed to carry the line: sheriff of Norfolk and Suffolk, member of Parliament, husband of a rich London heiress. But Sir John's only son died as a boy, the senior line failed, and the entire estate passed sideways to Robert's son Thomas. A man who left only a faint record of his own became the hinge on which the family turned. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6">6</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n13" id="ref-13">13</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>The world he moved in is documented in brilliant detail.</strong> His father Edmund was a leading lawyer retained by John of Gaunt — drawing a fee even from St Benet's Holme abbey — and the Norfolk steward of the earls of Arundel; his brother served Gaunt in Aquitaine in 1394; his son rode in John Holland's retinue at Agincourt in 1415. Robert lived his whole life inside that web of Lancaster-and-Arundel connection, Norwich and Lynn business, and family soldiering. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n9" id="ref-9">9</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n10" id="ref-10">10</a></sup></li>
  <li><strong>The family's red cross runs straight through him.</strong> The Gurney arms — a red <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engrailed">engrailed</a> cross on silver — were borne by his crusader ancestor, sealed on a document by his grandfather, and displayed by his father joined with his mother's Wauncy coat; his son carried them on and added a gurnard fish as a pun on the name. No seal of Robert's own survives, yet the heraldic thread is unbroken on both sides of him — a visible token of the continuity the pedigree records. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n11" id="ref-11">11</a></sup></li>
</ul>

</section>

<section class="fact-section fact-narrative" id="narrative">
<h2 class="unnumbered">Narrative</h2>

Robert Gournay was the second son of Edmund Gournay, and in the England of his day that single fact explains why he is so lightly recorded. Land, title, public office, and the records they threw off all passed to the eldest son — here, Robert's brother John. A younger son was settled more modestly and left a far fainter mark. So when the family's Victorian genealogist came to write Robert down five centuries later, he could manage only "a second son, whom we believe was named Robert." For a long time that bare phrase was all of him. But the trail is not empty. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n1" id="ref-1b">1</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n5" id="ref-5b">5</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n3" id="ref-3b">3</a></sup>

Read closely, two independent witnesses bring a real man into view. The family's own historian, Daniel Gurney — whose nineteenth-century compilation, though a secondary work, has proved careful and accurate wherever it can be checked — recorded that Edmund had a second son of this generation, "whom we believe was named Robert." Quite separately, Norfolk's land and court records preserve a Robert Gurnay around Little Cressingham, in the stretch of central Norfolk where the family had long held land — barely a dozen miles from their manor of Gurneys and Swathing at Hardingham, a holding the junior Gurney line had kept for generations. In 1405 he joined in a settlement over land and grazing rights there and at neighbouring Hopton, and between 1400 and 1406 he sat five times on local juries summoned to report on the lands of men who had lately died — once for an inquest that took in Little Cressingham itself. He is the only person of that uncommon name anywhere in Norfolk in Robert's generation. No surviving record states his parentage in so many words; but a reliable pedigree and the contemporary record, pointing independently to the same rare name, the same years, and the family's own country, together place him securely. Behind the pedigree's careful "we believe was named Robert" stands this landholder and juror of Little Cressingham. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n1" id="ref-1c">1</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n12" id="ref-12b">12</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n18" id="ref-18b">18</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n19" id="ref-19b">19</a></sup>

Faint as Robert's own record is, the people around him are documented in extraordinary depth — and they tell us what his world was like. His father Edmund was a lawyer of county-wide standing: retained by John of Gaunt to help run the Duke's East Anglian estates, drawing a fee even from the Broadland abbey of St Benet's Holme, and standing counsel to both Norwich and Bishop's Lynn. The family held their manor of Harpley of the earls of Arundel — whose Norfolk estates Robert's elder brother John would himself come to steward. His mother Katherine was the heiress who had brought the manor of West Barsham into the family. Robert grew up, in other words, in the most prosperous and best-connected household the Gurneys had ever kept, moving in a world of lawyers, sheriffs, merchants, and magnate servants. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n7" id="ref-7b">7</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n9" id="ref-9b">9</a></sup>

The public face of his generation was his elder brother, Sir John Gurney V. While Robert kept to the background, Sir John was sheriff of Norfolk and Suffolk in 1399 and again at his death in 1408, <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escheator">escheator</a> (the crown officer who handled estates that fell back to the king) and justice of the peace, a friend of Sir Thomas Erpingham, and member of Parliament for Norfolk in the Coventry parliament of 1404. He married Alice Heylesdon, daughter and heir of a wealthy London mercer, and with her came the manors of Hellesdon and Drayton, houses in Norwich, and a City of London warehouse called "La Selde Coronata." He carried the family's Lancastrian loyalty into the field too, taking out letters of attorney — authorising agents to manage his affairs while he was away — for John of Gaunt's 1394 expedition to Aquitaine. Robert, for his part, married Joan de Norwich — a name that points toward the Norwich civic world his father served, though the records keep nothing of her beyond it. The two brothers are the story of this generation: one in the full light of the public record, the other almost entirely in its shadow. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6b">6</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n4" id="ref-4b">4</a></sup>

The family's own emblem ran straight through Robert even where its paperwork did not. The Gurney arms — argent, a cross engrailed gules, a red scalloped cross on a silver field — had been borne by his ancestor Sir John de Gournay on crusade with the future Edward I, sealed by his grandfather Sir William, and displayed by his father Edmund joined with the Wauncy coat his marriage had brought in, in glass at Norwich and at Denton church. Robert's own son Thomas would carry the same cross and add a gurnard fish for a crest, a pun on the family name. No seal of Robert's survives, but the heraldic line is unbroken immediately above and below him — a small, concrete sign that the pedigree's claim is sound. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n11" id="ref-11b">11</a></sup>

Then came the turn that made this quiet younger son matter. Sir John V was reappointed sheriff on 15 November 1408 and died less than three weeks later, on 4 December. His only son Edmund, just ten years old, "followed him to the grave not long afterwards," and the senior line failed with him. The whole estate Edmund Gournay had assembled — West Barsham, Harpley, Hardingham, the Suffolk lands at Depden, the London warehouse and the Heylesdon manors — passed by right of blood to Robert's son Thomas. Whether Robert lived to see it we cannot say; his dates are too vague. But that is much of the point: the line continued not by any achievement of Robert's, but by where he stood within it. And the dense lattice of family ties around him — to Lancaster and to Arundel, to Norwich and to Lynn, through marriage, soldiering, and shared arms — is exactly what lets us trust that descent even where Robert's own record stays faint. <sup class="fn"><a href="#n6" id="ref-6c">6</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n13" id="ref-13b">13</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n14" id="ref-14">14</a></sup><sup>, </sup><sup class="fn"><a href="#n10" id="ref-10b">10</a></sup></section>
